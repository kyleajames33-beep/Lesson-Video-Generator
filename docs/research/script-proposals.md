## Molar mass opening: turn contrast into a real prediction

**Before, V2 hook:**

> A dozen eggs and a dozen apples contain the same number of items, but have different masses. Moles work the same way. One mole of different substances can have different masses. Molar mass tells us how much mass corresponds to one mole.

**Proposed:**

> One mole of carbon atoms. One mole of oxygen atoms. Both contain the same number of atoms. Which sample has more mass? Make a prediction.

**Production:** hold the two same-entity-count representations for an initial four seconds, with no answer, changing scale or hint in captions. Invite a longer pause if needed.

> The oxygen sample has more mass because each oxygen atom is heavier. A mole tells us how many entities we have. Molar mass tells us the mass per mole. That is how we connect an amount in moles to a mass measured on a balance.

**Intended improvement:** retain V3's direct contrast and usefulness while adding a genuine response opportunity. Do not call the initial claim a universal misconception without learner evidence. Show schematic collections labelled one mole; a single drawn atom must not appear to be the entire sample.

**Delivery:** light curiosity at the question; calm explanatory contrast on “same number” and “heavier”. This delivery choice is a hypothesis. Four seconds is a starting allowance, not an evidence-established optimum.

## Molar mass formula: make the relationship do the teaching

**Before, V2 formula excerpt:**

> Three quantities here. Lowercase m. That's the mass of your sample, in grams. Lowercase n. That's the amount of substance, in moles. And capital M. That's the molar mass, in grams per mole. Now watch the units. Moles times grams per mole. The mole units cancel. You're left with grams.

**Proposed:**

> Carbon atoms have a molar mass of twelve point zero one grams per mole, using our supplied value. Each mole contributes twelve point zero one grams. So two moles contribute twice that mass. That is why we multiply the amount in moles by the molar mass: m equals n times capital M. Moles times grams per mole leaves grams. The units fit a mass answer. We still need the right substance and values.

**Production:** keep `m`, `n`, `M` labels with their meanings on the existing formula board. Highlight the amount, then the per-mole contribution. Show the unit cancellation without moving the glyphs being read. If no previous scene introduced the symbols, briefly define them before using the relationship.

**Intended improvement:** explain proportionality and preserve dimensional checking without claiming units prove the entire answer. Avoid reading every definition card once it is familiar. The accessible transcript should retain all symbol meanings.

## Bracket example: reduce number recitation and protect the learner's decision

**Before, V2 excerpt:**

> Using the values on screen, calcium contributes forty point zero eight. Hydrogen contributes four point zero three two. Phosphorus contributes sixty-one point nine four. Oxygen contributes one hundred twenty-seven point nine nine two. Add these contributions without rounding the intermediate values.

**Proposed:**

> Calcium dihydrogen phosphate has the formula shown. The outside two means there are two H two P O four groups. How many oxygen atoms are there altogether? Count before calculating.

**Production:** separate prompt and answer audio. Initial five-second answer-free gap. Keep only the formula and supplied atomic values visible until the gap ends.

> Eight. Four in each group, with two groups. The complete count is one calcium, four hydrogens, two phosphorus atoms and eight oxygens. Multiply each count by its supplied atomic value. The board shows the four contributions. Add them with the extra digits retained. The total is two hundred and thirty-four point zero four four grams per mole. For this example, report two decimal places: two hundred and thirty-four point zero four grams per mole. The count comes first; the calculation follows it.

**Production:** retain the existing four-column board and supply `Ca 40.08; H 1.008; P 30.97; O 15.999`. Show `234.044 → 234.04 g mol⁻¹`. Label contributions as molar-mass contributions, not masses of individual atoms. Supply the requested reporting convention on screen. Review lower-frame caption collisions.

**Intended improvement:** let the board carry repeated contributions while narration supplies bracket scope, causal counting and final interpretation. Adding “and” in a large number is an Australian delivery proposal, not a required accent test. “Formula shown” needs the formula in the descriptive transcript; viewers relying on audio description need an accessible route to its full structure.

## Chlorine quick check: feedback repairs the specific error

**Before, original quick-check excerpt:**

> Quick check. How many moles are in seventy-one-point-zero grams of chlorine gas? Pause and try this yourself. First, the molar mass of chlorine gas.

**Proposed prompt:**

> You have seventy-one point zero grams of chlorine gas, Cl two. How many moles is that? Use thirty-five point four five for chlorine. First choose the molar mass of the complete molecule, then choose the conversion. Take five seconds to start, or pause for longer.

**Production:** insert exactly five seconds between separately recorded prompt and solution. This is start time, not enough-time-for-everyone wording. Keep the question, supplied Cl value and blank working visible. Prevent answer fade-in before the end of the gap.

**Proposed feedback:**

> Cl two contains two chlorine atoms per molecule, so its molar mass is seventy point nine zero grams per mole. We want an amount in moles, so divide mass by molar mass. Seventy-one point zero divided by seventy point nine zero gives about one point zero zero one four one moles. To three significant figures, report one point zero zero moles. If your answer was close to two moles, check whether you used the atomic value only once. If your units are not moles, check the operation as well.

**Intended improvement:** preserve problem visibility, make the response opportunity real and connect a plausible answer error with its cause. This independently recomputed example must use its own new recording and final assembled timeline.

## Limiting reagents: explain normalisation, do not just order it

**Before, original misconception excerpt:**

> You must divide by the coefficient.

**Proposed:**

> The equation needs two sodium atoms for each chlorine molecule. Compare how far each amount can take the same reaction. Divide sodium's amount by two and chlorine's by one. Sodium can support zero point two one seven five moles of reaction as written; chlorine can support zero point two eight two. Sodium supports less, so it sets the limit. The bars compare reaction capacity. They are not showing sodium disappear while we divide.

**Production:** use the existing prototype amounts `0.435 mol Na` and `0.282 mol Cl₂` as supplied for this comparison. The sodium bar halves; keep its original dashed reference. Display `0.218 mol` as a rounded label but calculate bar heights from unrounded values. A full mass/yield lesson needs its own consistent atomic-value set, not these rounded prototype inputs silently treated as exact.

**Intended improvement:** give the operation a stoichiometric meaning and prevent physical misreading of the animation. Say the reaction extent is for the equation as written. Do not teach the bars as molecule counts or instantaneous reaction kinetics.

## DNA mechanism: connect direction to fragments and state model limits

**Before, original worked example excerpt:**

> Polymerase can only build in one direction. On one template that's toward the fork, so it builds continuously, the leading strand. On the other it's away from the fork, so the lagging strand is made in short fragments, each with its own primer.

**Proposed:**

> New DNA is extended at its three-prime end, so it grows in the five-prime to three-prime direction. The two templates run in opposite directions. Trace the new strand behind this moving fork. One can extend towards the fork. The other grows away from it, so synthesis restarts in short sections as more template is exposed. Each section needs a primer. Later, after primer processing, ligase seals the joins. This simplified view does not show every enzyme or the proofreading that also helps copying stay accurate.

**Production:** check this against the exact fork orientation; keep template ends and a new-strand growth arrow visible. Do not show new synthesis in the wrong direction while drawing an aesthetically convenient line. Omitted enzymes may be acceptable for a narrowly scoped pairing clip, but need explicit additional stages if the lesson promises the complete process.

**Intended improvement:** supply the missing directional reason, avoid template-only explanations of fidelity and disclose simplification. Use a held tracing question before the fragment answer in a full mechanism lesson. More terms are justified only when needed for its verified curriculum scope.

## Enzyme graph: distinguish observation from a causal explanation

**Before, original substrate narration excerpt:**

> At that point every active site is occupied. The enzyme is saturated, so adding more substrate can't speed it up. [...] The only way to raise the plateau is to add more enzyme.

**Proposed:**

> At this fixed enzyme concentration, the rate approaches a plateau as substrate concentration rises. A saturation model explains this: the available enzyme is increasingly occupied, so adding more substrate has little effect on the rate. A plateau alone does not demonstrate enzyme damage. Under the same assay conditions, increasing the active enzyme concentration is one way to test this explanation. What result would you predict?

**Production:** show axes, units and “enzyme concentration fixed”. Hold the prediction before drawing a comparison curve. Mark curves as schematic unless linked to an actual dataset. Avoid implying all substrate curves perfectly identify their factor without axis context.

**Intended improvement:** retain school-level saturation reasoning while bounding “every” and “only”, separate evidence from model and make the next observation meaningful.

## Practical reasoning: replace guaranteed reliability with a diagnosis

**Before, original practical misconception excerpt:**

> Repeat each level at least three times and average, and your results become reliable.

**Proposed:**

> Repeat measurements at each temperature so you can inspect their spread and estimate a mean. Repeats do not fix a leak or a consistently late stopwatch start. Keep the enzyme source, preparation, substrate conditions and timing method consistent, then inspect the apparatus for systematic problems. Measure a gas-volume change over a stated interval, or a slope over time, and be clear which rate measure you are comparing.

**Intended improvement:** distinguish random variation from systematic error and preserve measurement meaning. Three repeats is a feasible starting plan, not a guarantee. An optimum-temperature prediction must be justified for the actual enzyme source and conditions rather than inferred from human body temperature.
