## hook

Burn twelve grams of carbon and you can make about forty-four grams of carbon dioxide. That sounds like a very generous reaction. Where did the extra mass come from? The oxygen was part of the starting materials too. The carbon hasn't multiplied; its atoms have joined oxygen atoms. Today we'll work out that product mass, then try a reaction where the mole amounts change as well.

## title

Mass-to-mass stoichiometry. Connecting the reaction's amounts to grams.

## concept

From the last lesson, a balanced equation compares mole amounts. Here carbon and carbon dioxide are one for one in moles. But one mole of carbon dioxide includes oxygen, so it weighs more than one mole of carbon. That's why the one-to-one coefficients aren't a one-to-one mass ratio. Carbon's molar mass connects its grams to its moles. The equation then connects those moles to carbon dioxide moles. Carbon dioxide's own molar mass takes us back to grams. The moving packet marks a calculation step. These equal piles show the one-to-one amount relationship, not literal particles travelling across a bridge.

## definition

There is a condition hiding behind a product prediction: the stated reactant amount has to react completely by the equation we're using. For the carbon example, that means enough oxygen and complete combustion to carbon dioxide. For the iron example, we'll start with pure iron three oxide, rather than assuming a whole ore sample is pure oxide. The answer is a theoretical mass under those conditions. What we actually collect can be lower because the reaction or recovery isn't perfect. We'll deal with limiting supplies and measured yield in their own lessons.

## formula

If mass-to-mole conversion and mole ratios already make sense, this is where they meet. We divide the known mass by that substance's molar mass. The units become moles. We then multiply by the wanted coefficient over the known coefficient, because the equation compares amounts. Finally, the wanted substance's molar mass turns its moles into grams. Each end needs the molar mass of the substance at that end. The two numbers might happen to be equal; their identity still matters. You can combine the three relationships into one expression. Showing the mole pathway helps us see why that expression works.

## worked-example

Let's put numbers on the carbon question. Twelve point zero grams of pure carbon burns completely, with enough oxygen. What carbon dioxide mass does the equation predict? Carbon's molar mass turns the given mass into an amount: just under one mole. That's an approximation for talking about the result; the calculation keeps the unrounded value. The equation gives one mole of carbon dioxide for each mole of carbon, so the product amount is the same. For the mass, we need carbon dioxide's own molar mass. It includes one carbon and two oxygens, not just the carbon we started with. Multiplying by that molar mass gives forty-four point zero grams, to three significant figures. About thirty-two grams came from oxygen. Counting both starting substances, mass is conserved.

## worked-example-2

Now the relationship really changes the amount. We have eighty point zero grams of pure iron three oxide and enough carbon monoxide for complete reaction by the displayed equation. How much iron does it predict? The oxide's own molar mass includes two irons and three oxygens. Dividing the sample mass by that gives just over half a mole of oxide. We'll keep the unrounded value in the calculation. One mole of oxide produces two moles of iron, so the iron amount doubles to just over one mole. Those are approximate mole amounts, not a claim that the iron mass doubles. Now iron's molar mass converts that wanted amount into grams. The predicted iron mass is fifty-six point zero grams, to three significant figures. It's less than the original oxide mass because the oxygen ends up in carbon dioxide, not in the iron product.

## misconception

A useful check is to ask what your ratio is actually comparing. Carbon to carbon dioxide was one to one in moles, yet their masses differed. Oxide to iron was one to two in moles, yet the iron weighed less than the starting oxide. Multiplying the starting grams by the coefficients would miss both relationships. And a mass-to-mass expression isn't wrong just because it skips writing an intermediate line. It needs the correct species molar masses and the coefficient ratio. Keep unrounded values in the calculation, and round the final result to the precision supported by the supplied data.

## quick-check-answer

The known magnesium oxide includes magnesium and oxygen, so we use the oxide's own molar mass. Dividing twenty point zero grams by that gives just under half a mole. That's approximate; we'll keep the unrounded value in the calculation. Magnesium to magnesium oxide is two to two, so the required magnesium amount is the same. Now magnesium's own molar mass converts that amount into grams. Multiplying gives twelve point one grams of magnesium, to three significant figures. Less magnesium mass than product mass makes sense, because oxygen supplies the rest.

## summary

We started with a mole ratio and connected it to mass. Each substance's own molar mass links its grams and moles; the balanced coefficients connect the two amounts in the middle. That explains both the larger carbon dioxide mass and the smaller iron mass, without creating or losing atoms. The method also runs backwards when a product amount is known.
