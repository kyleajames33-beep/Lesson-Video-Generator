## 1. Concept through contrast

**Sequence:** useful contrast or prediction; show relevant quantities; explain what changes and stays fixed; introduce precise concept; apply to altered conditions; retrieve the distinction.

**Molar mass example:** equal numbers of carbon and oxygen atoms, different mass. Then double a sample of the same substance. Ask whether amount, total mass or molar mass changes. Distinguish oxygen atoms from O₂ molecules. Existing scale artwork supplies context; coded labels carry exact entities and quantities.

**Student evidence:** explains why mass doubles but molar mass does not. **Avoid:** a visual heap implying actual atom counts, immediate prediction answers or only recalling a definition.

## 2. Calculation with a causal relationship and fading

**Sequence:** identify given/target; explain relationship and operation; complete one example; fade the target decision in a close example; independent altered problem; diagnostic feedback.

**Example:** carbon moles to mass, then mass to moles, then chlorine molecule conversion. Use existing formula and calculation boards; retain given values while revealing the operation. Fading removes a meaningful step rather than random labels.

**Student evidence:** chooses multiplication/division, explains mass per mole, uses correct molecule and units. **Avoid:** reciting every contribution, calling unit cancellation proof of the whole solution or changing too many prerequisites at once.

## 3. Ratio and constraint reasoning

**Sequence:** predict from quantities; expose the recipe/coefficient requirement; normalise quantities to a common capacity; identify the limiting quantity; derive product; consider excess separately; transfer to a changed ratio.

**Example:** sodium and chlorine coefficients, using the existing bars and original-state ghost. Label bars as reaction capacity, not matter literally vanishing. Establish an explicit recipe if using an everyday analogy.

**Student evidence:** explains why raw moles alone cannot decide the limiter and applies a changed coefficient. **Avoid:** incorrect bar meanings, hidden rounding or deciding solely from smaller mass.

## 4. Causal mechanism

**Sequence:** identify starting entities and model scope; predict one next state; show causal step; maintain orientation/reference labels; connect sequential steps; inspect products; explain or trace an unfamiliar instance.

**Example:** DNA templates and daughter strands. Reuse the native hand-drawn fork for complementary pairing and semi-conservative products. Check antiparallel direction and 5-prime to 3-prime synthesis during motion. Enzyme/primer/fragment stages are added only where the selected objective requires them.

**Student evidence:** traces an original strand and justifies a new strand's direction. **Avoid:** animation that only shows chronology, implying omitted steps do not exist, or claiming base pairing alone accounts for fidelity.

## 5. Evidence interpretation

**Sequence:** read axes/units and conditions; describe observation before cause; compare models or explanations; choose discriminating evidence; predict a test; qualify conclusion.

**Example:** enzyme-rate plateau at fixed active enzyme concentration. Reuse graphs with stable scales; label schematics. Ask whether a plateau demonstrates damage and what an enzyme-concentration comparison would show.

**Student evidence:** cites the trend, separates saturation explanation from observation and names a useful further measurement. **Avoid:** graph-shape recognition without axes, reading a unique cause from one curve or moving scales during comparisons.

## 6. Investigation preparation and practical reasoning

**Sequence:** measurement problem or flawed plan; IV/DV and operational measure; controlled conditions; apparatus/sequence; variability and systematic errors; inference and limitation; actual supervised practical route.

**Example:** catalase experiment with stated tissue source, substrate and gas-volume/rate method. Reuse apparatus artwork but code exact connections and measurements. Ask what repeats estimate and which error they cannot fix.

**Student evidence:** repairs a confound, distinguishes endpoint from rate and bounds a control inference. **Avoid:** universal 37 °C optimum, equal tissue mass equalling active enzyme, reliability guarantees or treating viewing as conducting the investigation.

## 7. Revision and exam application

**Sequence:** unseen task; select method/evidence; answer-free hold with optional pause; reasoned response and error diagnosis; next prerequisite or harder application.

**Example:** chlorine mass conversion or a new enzyme graph. Preserve stimulus through feedback. Provide a response rubric without pretending an invented question is official NESA material.

**Student evidence:** transfers a decision and supports it under changed conditions. **Avoid:** promised marks, long repeated introductions or satisfaction as a mastery measure.

## Script and delivery checklist

- Make the opening a useful student decision rather than exaggerated stakes.
- State the causal relationship before or alongside the method. Give prerequisite vocabulary where needed.
- Use warm direct Australian English, precise terms and specific reassurance. Humour is optional and related.
- Narrate essential entities, quantities and spatial relationships; let stable labels carry repetition. Provide a descriptive transcript for necessary visual information.
- Keep production notes separate from speech. Write explicit prompt, gap and feedback segments.
- Do not reveal the answer in narration, visuals or captions during a response hold.
- Use one supplied value set, guard digits and a stated reporting convention. Check symbols, entities and units independently.
- Remove unsupported attainment/prevalence claims and prohibited punctuation from selected copy before recording.
- Keep Simon as preferred narrator, but record model/settings/take decisions separately. Exact silence is assembled, not assumed from a tag.
- Rebuild affected audio, alignment, captions and cue timing when speech changes. Do not attach an old take to rewritten text.

Proposed timing/speech ranges remain in the research report as hypotheses. Review completion of the learner task, phone readability and actual delivery instead of treating seconds, WPM or pixels as quality scores.
