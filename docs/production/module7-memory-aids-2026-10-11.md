## Chemistry Module 7

### Naming (IUPAC)

**"Monkeys Eat Peeled Bananas."** *Mnemonic*
**Meth** (1 carbon), **eth** (2), **prop** (3), **but** (4). From 5 onwards the prefixes match shapes and numbers students already know: pent (pentagon), hex (hexagon), hept, oct (octagon), non, dec (decade).

**ane, ene, yne: alphabetical order is bond order.** *Mnemonic*
-**a**ne (single bond), -**e**ne (double), -**y**ne (triple). a, e, y run in alphabetical order, just as 1, 2, 3 bonds do.

**Three naming rules that cover most marks.** *Shortcut / Trap*
1. Find the longest chain that contains the functional group. It may bend on the page.
2. Number from the end that gives the functional group the lowest number (if there is none, give the substituents the lowest numbers).
3. List substituents alphabetically, ignoring di-, tri- and tetra- (so 2-bromo-3,3-dimethylpentane: b before m).

**The ending tells you the group.** *Shortcut*
-ol alcohol, -al aldehyde, -one ketone, -oic acid carboxylic acid, -oate ester, -amine amine, -amide amide. Haloalkanes use a prefix instead: chloro-, bromo-, iodo-.

**Aldehyde at the end, ketone in the middle.** *Insight*
An aldehyde's C=O is always on an end carbon, so it never needs a position number (propanal). A ketone's C=O is inside the chain, so it often does (pentan-2-one).

**Ester names: the alcohol gets "-yl", the acid gets "-oate".** *Trap*
Ethanol + propanoic acid makes **ethyl propanoate**. The alcohol part comes first as an alkyl group; the acid part comes second. When drawing, the -oate part is the side that contains the C=O. Students most often swap the two halves.

### Isomers

**Same formula, different arrangement.** *Insight*
Three kinds to recognise:
- **Chain:** the carbon skeleton is branched differently (butane vs methylpropane).
- **Position:** the same group sits on a different carbon (propan-1-ol vs propan-2-ol).
- **Functional group:** a different group with the same formula. Aldehydes and ketones with the same number of carbons are isomers of each other; so are carboxylic acids and esters.

### Properties: boiling point and solubility

**Hydrogen bonding wins, then size.** *Shortcut*
For molecules of similar size: carboxylic acid > alcohol > aldehyde or ketone > alkane. Carboxylic acids are highest because pairs of molecules hydrogen-bond to each other twice (dimers). Within one family, a longer chain means more dispersion forces and a higher boiling point.

**Branches lower the boiling point.** *Insight*
Straight chains lie alongside each other like uncooked spaghetti, with lots of contact. Branched molecules are more like balls: less contact, weaker dispersion forces, lower boiling point. The same idea explains why branched LDPE is softer than unbranched HDPE.

**Polar head vs non-polar tail.** *Insight*
A small alcohol or acid dissolves in water because its polar group hydrogen-bonds with water. As the carbon chain grows, the non-polar tail wins, so solubility drops (alcohols are fully miscible up to propanol and much less soluble after about four carbons).

### Hydrocarbon reactions

**"Alkanes swap, alkenes add."** *Mnemonic*
Alkanes have no double bond, so they can only substitute (swap an H for a halogen, needing UV light). Alkenes have a double bond that opens, so they add across it (H₂, halogens, hydrogen halides, water).

**Bromine water test.** *Shortcut*
Alkenes decolourise orange-brown bromine water quickly at room temperature, without UV. Alkanes react only slowly in UV light. Alkynes (C≡C) also decolourise it, so this is the standard test for unsaturation.

**Markovnikov: "the rich get richer".** *Mnemonic*
When HX or water adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the H goes to the carbon that already has more hydrogens. Propene + HBr gives mainly 2-bromopropane. Both products form; this rule picks the major one.

**The four alkene additions and their conditions.** *Shortcut*
- Hydrogenation: H₂ with a nickel or platinum catalyst gives an alkane.
- Halogenation: Br₂ or Cl₂ gives a dihaloalkane.
- Hydrohalogenation: HCl or HBr gives a haloalkane.
- Hydration: water with a dilute acid catalyst (H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄) gives an alcohol.

### Alcohols

**Classify an alcohol by counting carbons.** *Shortcut*
Count the carbons bonded to the carbon that holds the OH: one makes it primary, two secondary, three tertiary.

**Primary goes all the way, secondary stops at ketone, tertiary won't budge.** *Mnemonic / insight*
With acidified dichromate or permanganate:
- Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid.
- Secondary alcohol → ketone (stops there).
- Tertiary alcohol → no reaction.

*Why:* oxidation removes a hydrogen from the carbon holding the OH. A tertiary alcohol's carbon has no hydrogen to remove.

**"Orange to green means oxidised."** *Shortcut*
Acidified dichromate turns from orange to green when it oxidises a primary or secondary alcohol. It stays orange with a tertiary alcohol, which gives a simple test. (Acidified permanganate goes from purple to colourless.)

**Distil to catch the aldehyde, reflux to go all the way.** *Insight*
An aldehyde boils at a lower temperature than the alcohol, so distilling it off as it forms stops it oxidising further. Refluxing keeps everything in the flask, so oxidation continues to the carboxylic acid.

**Fermentation vs hydration of ethene.** *Shortcut*

| | Fermentation | Hydration of ethene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw material | Sugar (renewable) | Ethene from crude oil (non-renewable) |
| Speed | Slow, batch | Fast, continuous |
| Product | About 15% ethanol, needs distilling | Higher-purity ethanol after separation |
| Conditions | Yeast, anaerobic, about 30 to 40 °C | Phosphoric acid catalyst, about 300 °C, 6 to 7 MPa |

Fermentation stops at about 15% ethanol because the alcohol kills the yeast. C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂.

**Each extra CH₂ adds about 650 kJ mol⁻¹.** *Insight*
Molar enthalpies of combustion rise steadily along the alcohol series (methanol about −726, ethanol −1367, propan-1-ol −2021 kJ mol⁻¹), because each CH₂ adds the same bonds to burn.

**Your spirit-burner result will be too small.** *Trap*
Heat escapes to the air and the can, and combustion is often incomplete (soot), so experimental ΔH is much less negative than the data value. Calculate per mole of alcohol burnt: moles = mass lost ÷ molar mass.

### Carboxylic acids, esters, amines and amides

**Esterification: acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water, with concentrated H₂SO₄ under reflux.** *Insight* (links to Module 5)
The concentrated sulfuric acid is a catalyst *and* absorbs water, which shifts the equilibrium right (Le Chatelier). Reflux lets you heat a slow reaction for a long time without losing the volatile reactants and products.

**Purifying the ester: wash, separate, distil.** *Shortcut*
Wash with sodium carbonate solution to remove leftover acid (it fizzes as CO₂ forms), separate the layers in a separating funnel, then distil.

**"Amines are organic ammonia."** *Insight*
Like ammonia, an amine's nitrogen has a lone pair that can accept a proton, so amines are weak bases. An amide forms when a carboxylic acid reacts with an amine, losing water.

### Soaps and detergents

**Head in the water, tail in the grease.** *Insight*
A soap or detergent has a charged or polar head that attracts water and a long non-polar tail that dissolves in grease. The tails bury into a grease drop, leaving heads facing outwards, forming a micelle that water can rinse away.

**Soap makes scum in hard water; detergents don't.** *Trap*
Calcium and magnesium ions in hard water form insoluble precipitates with soap (scum). Synthetic detergents don't, so they still clean. Know the three types: anionic (sulfonate head; laundry and dishwashing), cationic (ammonium head; fabric softeners and antiseptics) and non-ionic (polar chain, no charge; low-foam uses such as dishwasher detergent). Soap is made by saponification: fat + NaOH → soap + glycerol.

### Reaction pathways

**Alkene → alcohol → acid → ester is the main road.** *Shortcut*
Learn one sequence and the conditions for each step: hydrate the alkene (acid catalyst), oxidise the alcohol (acidified dichromate or permanganate), react the acid with another alcohol (concentrated H₂SO₄, reflux). Most multi-step pathway questions use this route, with haloalkanes as a side route (alkene + HX; haloalkane + OH⁻ gives an alcohol).

### Polymers

**Addition: nothing lost. Condensation: water lost.** *Mnemonic*
Addition polymers form when alkene monomers open their double bonds and join, with no other product. Condensation polymers release a small molecule (usually water) each time two monomers link.

**Draw an addition polymer: open the double bond, add brackets.** *Shortcut*
Copy the monomer, change C=C to C–C, draw a bond out of each side and put brackets around it with n. For any monomer with one C=C (ethene, chloroethene, propene, styrene, tetrafluoroethene), the backbone repeat unit is two carbons; everything else hangs off the side.

**Find a condensation monomer: add the water back.** *Shortcut*
Find each ester or amide link and break it, adding OH to the C=O side and H to the O or N side. Each monomer needs a reactive group at both ends. Polyesters (such as PET) come from a dicarboxylic acid and a diol; polyamides (such as nylon) from a dicarboxylic acid and a diamine.

**Ester and amide links are weak points.** *Insight*
Water can break ester and amide links (hydrolysis), which is why some condensation polymers such as PLA are designed to biodegrade. Addition polymers have an all-carbon backbone with no such weak point, which is why polyethylene persists. Many condensation polymers, such as PET, still break down very slowly.

## Biology Module 7

### Pathogens

**Not a cell means antibiotics are useless.** *Insight* (sorts the whole topic)
Antibiotics target bacterial structures such as cell walls and bacterial ribosomes. Viruses and prions have none, so antibiotics do nothing to them, which is why antibiotics don't treat colds or flu. Fungi and protozoa are cells, but they need antifungal or antiparasitic drugs.

**Prions are the bad apple.** *Insight*
A prion is a misfolded protein that makes normal copies of the same protein misfold. It has no DNA or RNA, so it can't be killed like a microbe and survives ordinary cooking. Examples: mad cow disease (BSE) and kuru.

**Ectoparasites live outside, endoparasites inside.** *Mnemonic*
"Ecto" = outside (ticks, lice). "Endo" = inside (tapeworms).

### Germ theory: Pasteur and Koch

**Pasteur's swan-neck flask: air in, microbes trapped.** *Insight*
Boiled broth in a flask with a long, curved neck stayed clear. Air could reach it, but dust and microbes settled in the bend. Tilt the flask so broth touches the bend and it soon went cloudy. So microbes come from other microbes, not from the broth itself (disproving spontaneous generation).

**Koch's postulates: "Find it, grow it, give it, find it again."** *Mnemonic*
1. The microbe is found in every case of the disease.
2. It can be grown in pure culture.
3. The cultured microbe causes the disease in a healthy host.
4. The same microbe is recovered from that host.

**Where Koch's postulates fail.** *Trap*
Viruses can't be grown on agar alone (they need living cells). Some pathogens only infect humans, so step 3 can't ethically be tested. Healthy carriers break step 1. Some bacteria can't be cultured at all (the leprosy bacterium).

### Transmission and testing

**Every control measure breaks one link.** *Insight*
Name the mode of transmission first (direct contact, contaminated surfaces, droplets or air, vectors, body fluids, food or water). Then the control follows: handwashing breaks contact, masks break droplets, bed nets break vectors, water treatment breaks water-borne spread.

**Microbial testing: label the base, tape don't seal, incubate upside down, under 30 °C.** *Shortcut* (practical marks)
- Label the base of the agar plate, because lids get swapped.
- Tape at a few points rather than sealing all the way round, so air can enter (sealed, airless plates favour some pathogens such as *Clostridium*).
- Incubate upside down so condensation doesn't drip onto the colonies.
- Incubate at 25 to 30 °C, not body temperature, to avoid growing human pathogens.
- Never open plates after incubation; sterilise them (autoclave) before disposal.

### Plant responses

**The hypersensitive response is a firebreak.** *Insight*
Cells around an infection deliberately die, so the pathogen is surrounded by dead tissue it can't feed on or pass through. It works mainly against pathogens that need living tissue. Plants also thicken cell walls (physical) and produce antimicrobial chemicals such as phytoalexins (chemical).

**Know one Australian plant disease in full.** *Shortcut* (syllabus requirement)
For example, *Phytophthora cinnamomi* (dieback) in jarrah or banksia, or myrtle rust on plants in the myrtle family. Know the pathogen, the symptoms, the plant's response and the control measures.

### Animal immunity

**Castle defence: walls, guards, snipers.** *Insight*
- **First line, the walls:** skin, mucus, cilia, stomach acid, lysozyme in tears. They stop entry.
- **Second line, the guards:** inflammation, fever and phagocytes attack any invader (non-specific).
- **Third line, the snipers:** B and T cells target one specific antigen, and remember it.

Lines 1 and 2 are the innate system; line 3 is the adaptive system. Some guards (macrophages) show captured antigens to helper T cells, which is how the snipers learn their target.

**Inflammation: red, hot, swollen, painful, and why.** *Insight*
Damaged cells and mast cells release chemicals, including histamine. Blood vessels widen (more blood, so red and hot) and become leaky (fluid escapes, so swollen). Swelling and other released chemicals stimulate pain receptors. Phagocytes reach the site quickly.

**"Antigen = antibody generator."** *Mnemonic*
An antigen is the foreign marker that triggers the immune response. An antibody is the Y-shaped protein made to bind it. The name tells you which causes which.

**B cells make antiBodies; T cells Terminate or Tell.** *Mnemonic*
- B cells (mature in **B**one marrow) become plasma cells that release antibodies into body fluids (humoral response).
- Killer (cytotoxic) T cells destroy infected cells. Helper T cells tell B cells and killer T cells to act. T cells mature in the **T**hymus.

**What antibodies actually do.** *Shortcut*
Neutralise (block the pathogen or toxin), agglutinate (clump pathogens together), tag them so phagocytes find them (opsonisation) and activate complement proteins. Antibodies don't kill pathogens directly.

**Clonal selection: the name is the process.** *Insight*
The B or T cell whose receptor fits the antigen is *selected*, then copied into many *clones*. Some clones become memory cells.

**Second exposure: faster, bigger, longer.** *Shortcut* (graph questions)
Memory cells mean the secondary response starts sooner, produces far more antibody and lasts longer. On the graph, the second peak is earlier and much higher. Vaccination gives you the primary response without having the disease.

### Vaccination and immunity

**Active: you do the work. Passive: you're handed the antibodies.** *Mnemonic*

| | Natural | Artificial |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Active** (memory, long-lasting, slow to start) | Catching the disease | Vaccination |
| **Passive** (immediate, short-lived, no memory) | Antibodies across the placenta or in breast milk | Antivenom, antibody injections |

**Herd immunity: break enough links and the chain dies out.** *Insight*
When enough people are immune, an infected person rarely meets someone susceptible, so outbreaks fizzle. This protects people who can't be vaccinated. The more contagious the disease, the higher the coverage needed (measles needs about 95%).

### Antimicrobials

**The antibiotic selects; it doesn't create.** *Trap*
Resistance comes from random mutations that already exist, or from resistance genes passed between bacteria on plasmids. The antibiotic kills the susceptible bacteria, and the resistant survivors reproduce. That's natural selection. Also: bacteria become resistant, not people.

**Antivirals target virus-only steps.** *Insight*
Viruses use the host's own machinery, so drugs that stop them risk harming host cells. Antivirals target virus-specific steps (entering cells, copying, release). Many control an infection rather than remove it (HIV), though some cure it (hepatitis C). There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics.

### Disease control

**Endemic, epidemic, pandemic.** *Mnemonic*
**En**demic: always present **in** a region. **Epi**demic: a sudden rise **upon** a population. **Pan**demic: spread across many countries (pan = all).

**Malaria and dengue: know the pathogen and the vector.** *Shortcut*
- Malaria: *Plasmodium* (a protozoan), spread by *Anopheles* mosquitoes. Control: bed nets, insecticides, draining standing water, antimalarial drugs.
- Dengue: a virus, spread by *Aedes* mosquitoes. Australian example: releasing *Aedes* mosquitoes carrying *Wolbachia* bacteria, which block dengue transmission, has sharply reduced dengue in Townsville and Cairns (about a 96% reduction in Cairns).

**Using Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge: consent, credit, benefit.** *Shortcut*
Use of traditional medicinal knowledge should follow the protocols of the community the knowledge belongs to: free, prior and informed consent, acknowledgement of the knowledge holders, and fair sharing of benefits. Smoke bush (*Conospermum*) in Western Australia shows what goes wrong without them: a 1993 licence to develop conocurvone as a possible anti-HIV drug gave Aboriginal people no royalties or recognition. Tea tree's antiseptic use by the Bundjalung people is another example of traditional knowledge behind a modern product.
