   The range describes repeat spread, not a complete uncertainty interval.

## Enzyme graphs (20 points)

1. Allocate 3 points for all plots with factor labels/units and relative-rate
   context. A rises to 70 at the tested 40 °C then falls; B peaks at the tested
   pH 6; C rises with diminishing increments (2). Sample spacing does not
   locate an exact optimum between points, and interpolation is a model (1).
2. Temperature can increase catalytic rate, with high heat a possible cause
   of lost activity (1). pH can change ionisation, binding or catalysis (1).
   A simple fixed-enzyme substrate model approaches a limiting rate (1).
   Each mechanism is consistent with a trend rather than directly observed
   by the rate data alone (1).
3. Rate loss does not prove permanent unfolding; pH effects need not unfold
   the whole protein; a finite-substrate limiting trend does not require
   every site occupied at every instant (1 each).
4. Compare treated/restored and untreated samples at the same final assay
   temperature/pH (1). Match concentration, dilution/transfer, substrate,
   mixing and time, including an appropriately transferred untreated control
   (1). Recovery supports a reversible contribution (1); continued low rate
   supports persistent loss under that tested recovery condition but does not
   uniquely prove irreversible denaturation or exclude other losses (1).
5. Choose a plausible alternative limit, such as readout saturation or product
   effects (1), and an appropriate calibration/dilution check or controlled
   time/product comparison that discriminates it (1). Under the explicit
   simple model, doubling the same active enzyme doubles its limiting rate
   if the assay/substrate conditions remain suitable; a real assay need not
   obey that prediction when another constraint limits it (1).

Do not diagnose an anonymous factor or structural mechanism from shape alone.
The synthetic C values are rounded examples of an approach to a limit, not a
fit that proves a kinetic equation or estimates a real enzyme's parameters.

## DNA model (20 points)

1. Bases separate while the original backbones remain intact, with helicase
   correctly assigned (1). Primase makes RNA primers (1), furnishing an
   extendable 3′ end before DNA extension (1).
2. Upper new strand is 5′-A T G C C A-3′ (1). It extends rightward in this
   supplied geometry (1), adding to its 3′ end and growing 5′ → 3′ (1).
3. Upper is leading and lower lagging relative to the specified advancing
   fork (1). New lower fragments extend leftward, still 5′ → 3′ (1).
   Antiparallel templates plus the chemical extension direction require
   repeated starts as lower template is exposed (1). Do not label upper/lower
   as universal strand identities independent of fork direction.
4. Show primer removal/replacement as a separate schematic action (1), DNA
   replacement before ligase sealing (1), and ligase sealing remaining
   phosphodiester-backbone nicks rather than removing primers or filling
   nucleotide gaps (1). Exact processing enzymes differ across organisms;
   the school activity need not invent one universal enzyme identity.
5. Each completed molecule has one original and one new strand (1). This
   composition does not establish perfect copying (1). Polymerase selectivity,
   proofreading and mismatch repair contribute to fidelity, with residual
   errors possible (1).
6. Allocate 1 for a specific teaching purpose, 1 for two represented features,
   1 for two relevant limits, 1 for a limit-linked inference and qualified
   judgement, and 1 for a useful improvement. Suitable limits include scale,
   concurrent dynamics, enzyme chemistry, primer-processing detail and error
   control. An inheritance-only model addresses strand composition and
   complementary partners, not a complete fork/fidelity mechanism.
