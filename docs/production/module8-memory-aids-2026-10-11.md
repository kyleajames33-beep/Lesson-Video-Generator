## How to use these in videos

- Teach the idea first, then give the aid as a hook to keep it. A mnemonic never replaces the explanation.
- Say why the aid works whenever that is short.
- One or two aids per video is usually right. Put the strongest one in the "Key notes: what to write in your book" hold.
- Tags: **Mnemonic** (a phrase to recall a list or rule), **Shortcut** (a faster method), **Insight** (a way of seeing it that makes the rule obvious), **Trap** (a specific mistake it prevents).

## Chemistry Module 8

### Testing for ions

**Flame colours anchored to real objects.** *Mnemonic*
- Sodium: yellow-orange, like old sodium street lights.
- Strontium: red, like red fireworks.
- Lithium: crimson red.
- Calcium: brick red (orange-red).
- Potassium: lilac (pale purple).
- Barium: pale green.
- Copper: blue-green, like a weathered copper roof or the Statue of Liberty.

*Why each element has its own colour:* heat lifts electrons to higher energy levels, and when they fall back they emit light at wavelengths set by that element's energy levels. The same idea is behind AAS.

**Test with the most selective reagent first.** *Insight / shortcut* (cation flowchart)
Add reagents in an order where each one precipitates only a few ions, so you can tell them apart:
1. **Dilute HCl:** a white precipitate means Ag⁺ or Pb²⁺.
2. **Dilute H₂SO₄:** a white precipitate means Ba²⁺, Ca²⁺ or Pb²⁺.
3. **NaOH:** blue precipitate means Cu²⁺; green means Fe²⁺ (it turns brown on standing as air oxidises it); red-brown means Fe³⁺; white means Mg²⁺ once Ba²⁺, Ca²⁺ and Pb²⁺ are ruled out.

Iron(III) hydroxide is red-brown because it's essentially rust. Flame tests separate Ba²⁺ (pale green) from Ca²⁺ (brick red). Separate Pb²⁺ from Ag⁺ another way: PbCl₂ dissolves in hot water, and with KI, Pb²⁺ gives a bright yellow precipitate.

**Acidify before testing anions.** *Trap*
Acidify with dilute nitric acid first. Carbonate ions also give white precipitates with Ba²⁺ and Ag⁺, and the acid removes carbonate as CO₂. Don't use HCl (it adds chloride) or H₂SO₄ (it adds sulfate), which would give a false positive. Then a white precipitate with Ba²⁺ means sulfate, and with Ag⁺ means a halide (AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI pale yellow). Carbonate itself is identified by fizzing with acid; the gas turns limewater milky.

### Gravimetric analysis

**Dry to constant mass.** *Shortcut*
Weigh, heat, reweigh until the mass stops changing. Constant mass is the evidence that all the water has gone.

**Too wet reads too high; lost precipitate reads too low.** *Trap*
A precipitate that isn't fully dry weighs more, so the calculated amount is too high. Precipitate lost through the filter paper or stuck in the beaker makes it too low. Answers to "effect on the result" questions follow from these two.

**Precipitation titration (Mohr method).** *Shortcut*
AgNO₃ goes in the burette, with chromate as the indicator. White AgCl forms first; the first permanent red-brown Ag₂CrO₄ marks the end point. Run it near neutral pH. Ag⁺ reacts with Cl⁻ in a 1 : 1 ratio.

**Calculation chain: mass → moles → ratio → target.** *Shortcut*
Mass of precipitate ÷ its molar mass = moles of precipitate. Use the formula ratio to get moles of the target ion (one BaSO₄ contains one SO₄²⁻). Convert to mass, then to percentage of the sample.

### Spectroscopy: UV-visible and AAS

**Read across, then down, and only inside the standards.** *Shortcut*
On a calibration curve, find the sample's absorbance on the y-axis, read across to the line, then down to the concentration. Only trust values within the range of the standards; don't extend the line.

**Multiply back by the dilution factor.** *Trap*
If the sample was diluted tenfold to fit on the curve, the original concentration is ten times the value you read.

**In AAS, the lamp is made of the element you're testing.** *Insight*
Each element absorbs exactly the wavelengths it emits. A lead lamp emits lead's wavelengths, and only lead atoms in the flame absorb them. That's why AAS can measure one metal even in a mixture.

### Chromatography

**Rf = spot distance ÷ solvent front distance. Always between 0 and 1.** *Shortcut*
Measure both distances from the baseline, to the centre of the spot. Compare Rf values only when the same solvent and stationary phase are used. In HPLC, compare retention times instead.

**Whatever sticks to the stationary phase travels less.** *Insight*
On polar silica or paper, polar compounds are held more strongly and stay near the baseline; less polar compounds move further with the solvent.

**Pencil baseline, above the solvent.** *Trap*
Ink would itself separate and run. If the baseline is below the solvent surface, the spots dissolve into the solvent pool instead of travelling up.

### Water quality

**Warm water holds less oxygen.** *Insight*
Gases are less soluble at higher temperature (think of warm soft drink going flat). So warm water, such as near a power station outlet, has lower dissolved oxygen.

**High BOD means bad water.** *Mnemonic*
Biochemical oxygen demand measures how much oxygen microbes use in a sample over five days. Lots of organic matter means lots of microbial activity, a high BOD and a polluted waterway.

**Eutrophication: feed, bloom, die, decay, suffocate.** *Mnemonic*
1. Nitrate and phosphate from fertiliser or sewage feed algae.
2. The algae bloom.
3. The algae die.
4. Bacteria decompose them, using up dissolved oxygen.
5. Fish and other animals suffocate.

The fish die mainly because of the bacteria using oxygen, not because of the algae directly.

**Water treatment: screen, clump, settle, filter, disinfect.** *Shortcut*
Screening removes large objects. Coagulants such as alum neutralise the charge on tiny suspended particles (coagulation) so they clump into flocs (flocculation). The clumps settle (sedimentation), sand filters remove what's left, and chlorine kills microbes. The action words describe each stage.

**Biomagnification multiplies up the food chain.** *Insight*
Heavy metals such as mercury aren't excreted easily, so they build up in an organism (bioaccumulation). Each predator eats many prey, so concentrations multiply at each level. Top predators and people who eat them get the highest doses (Minamata, Japan).

### Testing organic compounds

**Three quick functional group tests.** *Shortcut*

| Test | Positive result | Shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bromine water | Orange-brown to colourless, quickly, no UV | C=C or C≡C (unsaturation) |
| Acidified dichromate, warm | Orange to green | Primary or secondary alcohol (or aldehyde) |
| Sodium carbonate or hydrogen carbonate | Fizzing (CO₂) | Carboxylic acid |

A tertiary alcohol leaves dichromate orange (see Module 7: tertiary won't budge).

### Mass spectrometry

**Find the molecular ion cluster; the tallest peak is the base.** *Shortcut / Trap* (corrected 31 Oct 2026: "rightmost peak is the molecule" fails when isotope peaks such as M+1 or chlorine's M+2 sit to its right)
The molecular ion (M⁺) is the main peak of the cluster at the high-m/z end, and it gives the molar mass. Small peaks just after it (M+1, or M+2 for chlorine and bromine) come from the same structure with a heavier isotope. It can be small, especially for alcohols. The base peak is simply the tallest peak, set to 100%. They're often different peaks, and confusing them is a common lost mark.

**Common fragments and losses.** *Shortcut*
- m/z 15: CH₃⁺. m/z 29: C₂H₅⁺ (or CHO⁺). m/z 43: C₃H₇⁺ (or CH₃CO⁺). m/z 45: COOH⁺ (but alcohols can also give 45). m/z 31: CH₂OH⁺, typical of primary alcohols.
- A gap of 18 below M⁺ means loss of water, typical of alcohols.
- Two peaks 2 units apart in a 3:1 ratio mean chlorine; 1:1 means bromine (from their isotopes).

### Infrared spectroscopy

**Broad means O–H; sharp and strong near 1700 means C=O.** *Shortcut*
- Alcohol: a broad, rounded O–H band around 3230 to 3550 cm⁻¹.
- Carboxylic acid: a very broad O–H "beard" from about 2500 to 3000 cm⁻¹, plus a sharp C=O "sword" near 1700. Beard plus sword means acid.
- Ketone, aldehyde or ester: a sharp C=O between 1680 and 1750 with no broad O–H.
- Amine: an N–H band (3300 to 3500) that is sharper and weaker than an alcohol O–H.

The data sheet gives the exact ranges; learn to recognise the shapes.

**Don't try to assign the whole fingerprint region.** *Trap*
Below about 1500 cm⁻¹ there are too many peaks to assign one by one. A strong C–O band (1000 to 1300) can support an alcohol or ester assignment, but the region is mainly used to match a spectrum against a known compound.

### NMR spectroscopy

**¹³C NMR: one peak per carbon environment, and symmetry merges them.** *Shortcut*
Draw the molecule and look for a mirror line. Carbons that swap places under the symmetry are in the same environment and give one peak. Propan-2-ol has three carbons but only two peaks.

**More deshielded means further left; check the data-sheet group.** *Insight* (corrected 31 Oct 2026: shift is not a simple distance-to-oxygen rule, so match each peak to the data sheet's group ranges)
Higher chemical shift (ppm) means the nucleus is more deshielded, often by nearby oxygen or nitrogen, but bonding type matters too. C=O carbons appear far left, around 160 to 220 ppm.

**¹H and ¹³C use different scales.** *Trap*
¹H shifts run from about 0 to 12 ppm; ¹³C shifts run from about 0 to 220 ppm. Always read the matching data-sheet table.

**¹H NMR: the n + 1 rule.** *Shortcut*
A signal splits into n + 1 peaks, where n is the number of H atoms on neighbouring carbons. OH hydrogens usually appear as a single peak and don't split their neighbours. The area (integration) shows the relative number of H atoms in that environment.

**Pattern spotting.** *Shortcut*
- A triplet (3H) plus a quartet (2H) means an ethyl group, CH₃CH₂–.
- A doublet (6H) plus a septet (1H) means an isopropyl group, (CH₃)₂CH–.
- A singlet (3H) means a CH₃ with no H on the next atom (such as CH₃ next to C=O).

### Structure determination

**Mass, groups, map.** *Shortcut* (order of attack)
1. **MS** gives the molar mass (and useful fragments).
2. **IR** shows which functional groups are present.
3. **¹³C and ¹H NMR** map the carbon skeleton and where the hydrogens are.
Then check that the proposed structure explains every spectrum.

### Chemical synthesis

**Yield is how well you did; atom economy is how good the reaction is.** *Trap / insight*
Percentage yield compares the product you actually got with the theoretical amount. Atom economy = mass of desired product ÷ total mass of reactants (from the balanced equation) × 100. A reaction can have a 100% yield and poor atom economy if it makes a lot of by-product. Addition reactions have 100% atom economy because nothing else forms.

**Aspirin: salicylic acid + ethanoic anhydride → aspirin + ethanoic acid.** *Shortcut*
Know this example for synthesis questions (it uses an acid catalyst such as H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄): the ethanoic acid by-product lowers the atom economy, and the product is purified by recrystallisation.

## Biology Module 8

### Homeostasis

**Negative feedback: "Some Really Cool Elephants Run."** *Mnemonic*
**S**timulus, **R**eceptor, **C**ontrol centre, **E**ffector, **R**esponse. In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change, like a thermostat switching off the heater once the room is warm. Positive feedback (birth contractions, blood clotting) is rare and always has an end point.

**Sort endotherm adaptations into three types.** *Shortcut* (syllabus point)
Structural, behavioural and physiological. Example: a red kangaroo licks its forearms (behavioural) so evaporation cools the blood in surface vessels (physiological).

**The hypothalamus is the thermostat.** *Shortcut*
- Too hot: vasodilation (more blood near the skin loses heat) and sweating (evaporation cools).
- Too cold: vasoconstriction, shivering (muscles release heat), hairs stand up, and metabolic rate rises.

**Blood vessels widen; they don't move.** *Trap*
"Blood vessels move closer to the skin" loses marks. Arterioles supplying the skin dilate, so more blood flows through the skin capillaries.

**Insulin lets glucose in. Glucagon acts when glucose has gone.** *Mnemonic*
High blood glucose: the pancreas releases insulin, cells take glucose **in**, and the liver stores it as glycogen. Low blood glucose: the pancreas releases glucagon (glucose is **gone**), and the liver breaks glycogen down to release glucose.

**Glycogen is the store; glucagon is the hormone.** *Trap*
The two words are easily swapped. Glycogen is a polysaccharide stored in liver and muscle. Glucagon is the hormone that triggers its breakdown.

**Type 1: no key. Type 2: the lock is jammed.** *Insight*
Think of insulin as a key that opens cells to glucose. In type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys the insulin-making cells, so there's no key; treatment is insulin. In type 2, cells respond poorly to insulin (the jammed lock), and over time the pancreas often makes less insulin too. Risk rises with excess weight, inactivity, age and family history. It's often managed first with diet, exercise and medication, and some people later need insulin.

**ADH is the "anti-wee" hormone.** *Mnemonic*
Anti-diuretic hormone makes the kidney's collecting ducts reabsorb more water, so you produce less, more concentrated urine. Dehydration raises ADH. Alcohol suppresses ADH, which is why it makes you urinate more and become dehydrated.

**Water follows salt.** *Insight*
Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption in the kidney, and water follows the sodium by osmosis. Many water-balance questions are explained by this single phrase.

**Plants: shrink the exposed surface or trap a humid layer.** *Insight* (Australian plant adaptations)
Nearly every water-saving adaptation does one of these, or waterproofs the surface: sunken stomata and leaf hairs trap moist air next to the stomata; thick waxy cuticles, small leaves and vertically hanging eucalypt leaves reduce water loss and heating. Stomata also close when guard cells lose water.

### Causes of non-infectious disease

**Cancer: a stuck accelerator and failed brakes.** *Insight*
Proto-oncogenes are the accelerator: mutated into oncogenes, they push cells to divide constantly. Tumour suppressor genes are the brakes: mutated, they no longer halt division. Cancer usually needs several such mutations, which is one reason risk rises with age.

**Malignant moves; benign is bound.** *Mnemonic*
A benign tumour stays in one place, often enclosed. A malignant tumour invades nearby tissue and can spread through blood or lymph to form new tumours (metastasis).

**Slip, Slop, Slap, Seek, Slide.** *Mnemonic* (Australian skin cancer prevention)
Slip on a shirt, slop on sunscreen, slap on a hat, seek shade, slide on sunglasses. Australia has one of the highest skin cancer rates in the world, so this is a strong prevention example.

**Deficiency diseases: sCurvy is vitamin C; rickets needs Daylight (vitamin D).** *Mnemonic*
Scurvy comes from lack of vitamin C (sailors without citrus). Rickets comes from lack of vitamin D, which skin makes in sunlight. Also know iron-deficiency anaemia and protein deficiency (kwashiorkor). Excess intake (obesity, type 2 diabetes) is nutritional disease too.

**Autoimmune is friendly fire; allergy is an overreaction.** *Insight*
In autoimmune disease (type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis), the immune system attacks the body's own cells. In an allergy, it responds strongly to a harmless antigen such as pollen, releasing histamine.

### Epidemiology

**Incidence flows in; prevalence is the pool.** *Insight*
Picture a bath. Incidence is the tap: the rate of *new* cases over a period. Prevalence is the water in the bath: *all* current cases at one time. Cures and deaths are the drain. A disease that people live with for years (such as type 2 diabetes) can have high prevalence even with modest incidence. Better treatment that keeps people alive narrows the drain, so prevalence can rise even while incidence falls.

**Mortality is death; morbidity is illness.** *Mnemonic*
Mort as in mortuary. Morbidity measures how many people are sick or disabled.

**Cohort goes forward; case-control looks back.** *Mnemonic*
A cohort study follows exposed and unexposed groups forward in time to see who develops disease. A case-control study starts with people who have the disease and looks back to compare their past exposures with a control group.

**Evaluating an epidemiological study.** *Shortcut* (syllabus point)
Check sample size and how representative the sample is, whether there was a control group, confounding variables, how long the study ran, and whether the data were self-reported.

**Correlation is not causation.** *Trap*
Two trends rising together don't prove one causes the other. Look for confounding variables (such as age or smoking), a dose-response pattern and a plausible mechanism before concluding cause.

### Technologies for disorders

**Malleus, incus, stapes: "Make It Sound", from outside in.** *Mnemonic* (corrected 31 Oct 2026: the old "alphabetical" line was false, since incus comes before malleus alphabetically)
Sound path: pinna → ear canal → eardrum → malleus → incus → stapes → oval window → cochlea (hair cells) → auditory nerve. M, I, S, "Make It Sound", gives the three tiny bones in the direction sound travels.

**A hearing aid makes sound louder; a cochlear implant skips the broken part.** *Insight* (most important hearing distinction)
- **Conductive hearing loss:** sound can't reach the cochlea properly (wax, a damaged eardrum, stiff ossicles). A hearing aid amplifies sound, or a bone conduction implant bypasses the middle ear.
- **Sensorineural hearing loss:** sound reaches the cochlea, but hair cells are damaged. Hearing aids help mild to moderate loss. For severe to profound loss, a cochlear implant bypasses the damaged hair cells and stimulates the auditory nerve directly, so the nerve must still work. (Graeme Clark's team in Melbourne developed the multichannel cochlear implant, first implanted in 1978.)

**Myopia: you can see what's near "my" eyes.** *Mnemonic*
Short-sighted (myopia): the eyeball is too long or the lens too strong, so distant images focus *in front of* the retina. Corrected with a concave (diverging) lens: concave "caves in".
Long-sighted (hyperopia): "hyper" = beyond, so images focus *behind* the retina. Corrected with a convex (converging) lens.

**Presbyopia: "presby" means old.** *Mnemonic*
With age, the lens stiffens and can't focus on near objects, so reading glasses are needed. A cataract is a cloudy lens, replaced with an artificial one. Laser surgery reshapes the cornea to correct refractive errors.

**The nephron: filter everything small, take back what you want, add what you don't.** *Insight*
Filtration at the glomerulus pushes out water, salts, glucose and urea. Reabsorption returns the useful substances. Secretion adds extra wastes and excess ions into the urine.

**Dialysis fluid is "clean blood" without the waste.** *Insight*
Blood and dialysis fluid flow past each other on either side of a partially permeable membrane. The fluid has normal blood levels of glucose and salts but no urea, so urea diffuses out while useful glucose and salts stay. (Salt levels in the fluid can be set to remove excess ions, and pressure removes extra water. This describes haemodialysis.) Flowing in opposite directions (counter-current) keeps the concentration gradient steep along the whole membrane.

**Transplants need a match and immunosuppressants.** *Insight* (link to Module 7)
The recipient's immune system sees the donor organ's antigens as foreign and attacks them (rejection). Close tissue matching and lifelong immunosuppressant drugs reduce this, at the cost of more infections.
