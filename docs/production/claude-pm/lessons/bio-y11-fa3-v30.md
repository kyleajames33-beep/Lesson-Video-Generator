### 3. why the predator lags
> Usually the prey. Predators can only build up after their food becomes plentiful, because it takes time for them to breed and for their young to grow. And they only decline after the prey have already crashed. That delay is called a lag. So on a predator-prey graph, the curve that peaks first is usually the prey. The predator peak arrives late.

### 6. the fence-and-food experiment
> It can't. So ecologists in the Yukon tested it. They fenced some hare populations to keep predators out, added food to others, and did both together in a third. Eleven means they work together: hungry hares are easier to catch. So the lag shows predators depend on prey. It doesn't prove predators control prey on their own.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Predator-prey cycle: more prey → more predator young survive → more prey eaten → prey fall → predators go hungry and fall → prey recover.
- The predator peak usually comes later (a lag), because predators take time to breed and their young to grow. Lynx peak 1 to 2 years after hares.
- A lag shows predators depend on prey, not that predators alone control them. Yukon hares: predators out ×2, food added ×3, both ×11.
- Competition (−/−): intraspecific (within a species) is usually fiercer and grows as numbers rise; interspecific (between species) can push one out. Single-species controls (Gause's tubes) show competition caused the decline.
