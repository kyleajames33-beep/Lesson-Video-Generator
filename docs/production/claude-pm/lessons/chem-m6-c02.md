### 2. binary acids (hydro- ... -ic)
> First, names. Acids come in two families. **Binary acids** are hydrogen joined to one non-metal, with no oxygen. They're named **hydro**, then the non-metal, then **ic acid**. H C l in water is **hydrochloric acid**. H B r is hydrobromic acid, H F is hydrofluoric acid, and H two S is **hydrosulfuric acid**. As a pure gas, H C l is just called **hydrogen chloride**, which is what you saw in the last video.

Visual: the naming card writes: "Binary acid: H + one non-metal, no O. Name: hydro + non-metal + ic acid." On "hydro" "ic acid" the template blocks colour (hydro teal, root grey, ic acid amber). Formula tiles fill a table on their cues: HCl → hydro|chlor|ic acid; HBr, HF; H₂S → hydro|sulfur|ic acid. On "hydrogen chloride" a small chip "HCl(g): hydrogen chloride; HCl(aq): hydrochloric acid" with the C01 tube plate thumbnail. Hold about 1 second.

### 3. oxoacids (-ate to -ic, -ite to -ous)
> **Oxoacids** contain oxygen too, and they're named after the **ion** inside them. If the ion ends in **ate**, the acid ends in **ic**: sulfate gives sulfuric acid, nitrate gives nitric acid, phosphate gives phosphoric acid, and carbonate gives carbonic acid. If the ion ends in **ite**, the acid ends in **ous**: sulfite gives sulfurous acid, and nitrite gives nitrous acid. Oxoacids **never** start with hydro. So H two S O four is sulfuric acid, not hydrosulfuric acid. That name belongs to H two S.

Visual: second naming card: "Oxoacid: contains O. Name from the ion." Two columns build one row per cue; each earlier row dims to grey as the next starts, so only the current row is bright: "-ate → -ic": SO₄²⁻ sulfate → H₂SO₄ sulfuric acid; NO₃⁻ nitrate → HNO₃ nitric acid; PO₄³⁻ phosphate → H₃PO₄ phosphoric acid; CO₃²⁻ carbonate → H₂CO₃ carbonic acid. "-ite → -ous": SO₃²⁻ sulfite → H₂SO₃ sulfurous acid; NO₂⁻ nitrite → HNO₂ nitrous acid. Each acid formula shows H⁺ tokens joining the ion (number of H = size of the ion's charge). On "never" a teal bar strikes "hydro" off the oxoacid card. A chip "H₂SO₄: hydrosulfuric acid" fades in and is crossed (caption "has O: sulfuric acid"); the H₂S tile from scene 2 lights. Hold about 2 seconds.

### 4. naming bases
> Bases are named like any ionic compound: metal first, then the negative ion. **Sodium hydroxide**, calcium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide. Then there are the bases with no hydroxide that you met last video. **Ammonia**, N H three, accepts protons. Write it as ammonia, not "ammonium hydroxide". And metal **carbonates** and **hydrogen carbonates**, like calcium carbonate and sodium hydrogen carbonate, react as bases too.

Visual: base card: "Hydroxides: metal + hydroxide" with tiles NaOH, Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂ (charge tiles show one OH⁻ per positive charge). On "Ammonia" the NH₃ molecule from C01 with a violet "proton acceptor" outline; a chip "NH₄OH, ammonium hydroxide" fades in and is crossed (caption "write NH₃(aq)"). On "carbonates" tiles CaCO₃ and NaHCO₃ with the C01 tag "accepts H⁺". Hold about 1 second.

### 5. family properties
> These families share properties because acids give away protons and bases take them. Acids taste sour, like lemon juice and vinegar, and they turn blue litmus **red**. They react with **reactive metals** and with **carbonates**. Bases taste bitter, feel slippery, like soap, and turn red litmus **blue**. And acids and bases **neutralise** each other. Never test taste or feel in the lab, of course. How strongly each acid gives up its protons is a separate idea, with its own video.

### 6. the salt rule
> Those reactions are where prediction comes in. Every acid reaction in this video makes a **salt**: an ionic compound made of a **positive ion** from the other reactant, and the **negative ion** left from the acid. Hydrochloric acid makes **chlorides**. Sulfuric acid makes **sulfates**, nitric acid makes nitrates, and phosphoric acid makes phosphates. Get the salt's formula from the **charges** first, then balance.

Visual: the salt builder: left slot "positive ion (from the base or metal)" amber; right slot "negative ion (from the acid)" violet. On "chlorides" "sulfates" etc. a strip writes: "HCl → chloride, Cl⁻ | H₂SO₄ → sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | HNO₃ → nitrate, NO₃⁻ | H₃PO₄ → phosphate, PO₄³⁻", each acid losing its teal H⁺ tokens to leave the ion; the strip then shrinks and dims. On "charges" a demonstration (the only bright item): Ca²⁺ with Cl⁻ needs two Cl⁻ tiles, giving CaCl₂, with a charge tally "(+2) + 2(−1) = 0". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 7. acid plus hydroxide (salt and water)
> Pattern one: acid plus a **hydroxide** gives a **salt and water**. Why water? Each hydroxide ion **accepts a proton** from the acid, and a hydroxide ion plus a proton is a water molecule. Antacid tablets do this with stomach acid, which is hydrochloric acid. Magnesium hydroxide has a magnesium **two plus** ion and two hydroxides. Each hydroxide needs a proton, so it takes **two** hydrochloric acids. The products: magnesium chloride, M g C l two, and **two waters**.

Visual: pattern card 1 writes: "acid + hydroxide → salt + water". Lens: a teal H⁺ hops from HCl onto an OH⁻ and the pair becomes a pale blue H₂O (tag "OH⁻ + H⁺ = H₂O"). On "Antacid tablets" `m6-antacid-tablets-v1.png` stands in the left column with a tag "Mg(OH)₂". On "two plus" the salt builder shows Mg²⁺ with two Cl⁻ tiles. On "two hydrochloric acids" the strip writes: "Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)", with two proton hops shown in the lens and the 2s pulsing; a small check "H: 2 + 2 = 4 = 2 × 2" writes under it. As the scene closes, the third hook beaker returns as a thumbnail and a small line writes beside it: "hook: NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)" (screen only). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 8. acid plus ammonia (salt, no water)
> Now pattern one with a base that has **no hydroxide**. Fertiliser factories react ammonia with sulfuric acid to make **ammonium sulfate**. Each ammonia molecule accepts a proton and becomes an **ammonium ion**. Sulfuric acid can give two protons, so it needs **two** ammonias, and the salt is two ammonium ions with one sulfate. And here's the catch: there's **no water** in the products. With no hydroxide, there's nothing for the proton to turn into water.

On "no water" a chip "+ 2H₂O" fades in after the arrow and is crossed (caption "no OH⁻, so no water"). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 9. acid plus carbonate (salt, water and carbon dioxide)
> Pattern two: acid plus a **carbonate** gives a salt, water and **carbon dioxide**. The carbonate ion accepts **two** protons and becomes carbonic acid, which is unstable and breaks straight into **water and carbon dioxide**. That's the fizz. Marble is calcium carbonate. With hydrochloric acid, calcium two plus needs two chlorides, so two hydrochloric acids give **calcium chloride**, water and carbon dioxide. **Hydrogen carbonates**, like baking soda, follow the same pattern.

Visual: pattern card 2: "acid + carbonate → salt + water + CO₂". Lens: two teal tokens hop onto a CO₃²⁻ ion, making H₂CO₃ (dashed outline, tag "unstable"), which splits into H₂O and a grey-green CO₂ bubble that rises. On "Marble" `m6-marble-chips-acid-v1.png` in the left column. On "calcium chloride" the strip writes: "CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)", with Ca²⁺ and two Cl⁻ in the salt builder. On "Hydrogen carbonates" the clip `m6-clip-baking-soda-vinegar-before-v1.png` to `-fizz-v1.png` plays small with the tag "baking soda + vinegar: same pattern", and a second strip line writes small: "NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 10. acid plus reactive metal (salt and hydrogen)
> Pattern three: acid plus a **reactive metal** gives a salt and **hydrogen gas**. This one isn't a proton transfer. The metal atoms **give electrons** to hydrogen ions, which pair up as hydrogen molecules, and the metal ends up as positive ions in the salt. Aluminium with hydrochloric acid: aluminium is **three plus**, so the salt is A l C l three. Balancing the hydrogen takes six hydrochloric acids and two aluminiums, making two aluminium chlorides and **three hydrogen molecules**.

Visual: pattern card 3: "acid + reactive metal → salt + H₂". `m5x-strong-acid-mg-v1.png` in the left column. Lens: a metal surface atom releases small yellow electron dots to two H⁺ tokens, which join as a white H₂ bubble; the metal atom becomes an amber "+" ion that drifts into the solution. A tag "electron transfer (Year 11), not proton transfer". On "three plus" the salt builder: Al³⁺ with three Cl⁻, giving AlCl₃. On "three hydrogen molecules" the strip writes: "2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)" with a hydrogen tally "6 H = 3 × 2". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 11. only metals above hydrogen
> But only metals **above hydrogen** in the activity series do this, like magnesium, aluminium, zinc and iron. **Copper** and silver sit below hydrogen, so in a dilute acid like hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, nothing happens. No bubbles, no salt. And watch the gas formula. Hydrogen gas is **H two**, never just H.

### 19. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Binary acids (H + one non-metal, no O): hydro + non-metal + ic acid. HCl hydrochloric, HBr hydrobromic, HF hydrofluoric, H₂S hydrosulfuric.
2. Oxoacids (contain O), named from the ion: -ate → -ic (H₂SO₄ sulfuric, HNO₃ nitric, H₃PO₄ phosphoric, H₂CO₃ carbonic); -ite → -ous (H₂SO₃ sulfurous, HNO₂ nitrous). Never hydro-.
3. Bases: metal hydroxides (NaOH sodium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂); ammonia NH₃ (not "ammonium hydroxide"); carbonates and hydrogen carbonates. Acids: sour, blue litmus red; bases: bitter, slippery, red litmus blue.
4. Salt = positive ion from the other reactant + negative ion from the acid; formula from charges first.
   - acid + hydroxide → salt + water: Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
   - acid + ammonia → salt only: 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄
   - acid + carbonate or hydrogen carbonate → salt + water + CO₂: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why acids and bases share family properties: acids give away protons, bases take them (5). Why oxoacid names come from the ion: the acid is the ion plus enough H⁺ to cancel its charge (3). Why a salt forms: the partner's positive ion and the acid's leftover negative ion (6). Why hydroxides give water: OH⁻ + H⁺ = H₂O (7). Why ammonia gives no water: the proton makes NH₄⁺, and there is no OH⁻ (8). Why carbonates give CO₂: carbonic acid is unstable and splits (9). Why metals give H₂: metal atoms give electrons to H⁺ (10). Why copper does not react: it is below hydrogen, so it does not give electrons to H⁺ (11). Why the coefficients: charges set the salt formula, then atoms balance (7 to 10).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** hydrosulfuric for H₂SO₄ (3); ammonium hydroxide (4); water from ammonia (8); CO₂ forgotten (9, 15); H not H₂ (11); copper reacting with dilute acid (11, 17); acid + metal is a proton transfer (10).
- **Worked-example rule:** one equation per scene; the salt builder and its charge tally sit beside the equation only while that equation is built; earlier equations leave the strip (they return only in the key notes).
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen HSC-style tasks (a named acid and carbonate needing formulas; a metal pair with a supplied activity series), every condition visible (states requested; "dilute"; "separate sample"; stimulus), 10 seconds each with the invitation to pause, nothing answer-bearing. One crossed mistake each; feedback about 25 and 26 seconds at the measured rate.
- **Avoided double teaching:** Brønsted-Lowry (C01) is used in one clause per pattern; the activity series and ionic formulas (Year 11) are used, not taught; electron transfer is named as Year 11 in a tag.
- **Out of scope by design:** indicators (chem-m6-c13, chem-m6-p01); strong and weak (chem-m6-c05); net ionic neutralisation and its heat (chem-m6-c03); applications of neutralisation (chem-m6-c04).
