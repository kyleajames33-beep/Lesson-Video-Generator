## Decisions

- **Main teaching move: you can't see water leaving, so measure water arriving.** The hook states the problem (vapour is invisible) and the trick (time the water going in), then the whole video rests on one picture: the potometer with a coded bubble sliding along its painted scale. The bubble's speed is linked back to v13's leaf (scene 2) before any procedure, so the measurement means something before it is taken.
- **The proxy (scene 3; route stop "Uptake is a proxy for water loss").** Said plainly, with its known bias: a little water is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells, so uptake slightly overstates loss. The site's hooks-file aid, adapted: "A potometer measures the drink, not the sweat." ("weighs" changed to "measures": a bubble potometer measures volume moved, not mass; site finding 3.) Scene 8 then shows why the bias does not spoil the comparison (it affects both conditions).
- **Airtight set-up (scene 4; route "safe airtight").** Cut under water (the reason is v12's: an air bubble breaks the column), fit under water, petroleum jelly seal, dry the leaves, settle about ten minutes. The reason for airtightness is spoken: a leak moves the bubble for reasons that have nothing to do with the leaves.
- **The factor: wind (fan off, then on) on one shoot.** The site's own factor and data. The same shoot is used before and after (removes leaf area as a variable; site walk-through), with a thermometer beside the shoot because a fan can cool leaves (the site's named confounder). Light and humidity are shown as screen-only alternatives in the frame (scene 5 tag) and light is the factor in attempt 1, so students see the plan transfer without a separate spoken scene.
- **Rate as distance per time (packs' question answered).** Rate is in millimetres per minute; no cross-sectional area is given. A grey screen tag in scene 6 notes that a volume rate needs the tube's cross-sectional area ("volume = distance × cross-sectional area"). The site's data are processed in full: 18 mm in 6 min = 3 mm/min; 40 mm in 5 min = 8 mm/min; about 2.7 times. The trap the site names ("Comparing 18 mm with 40 mm directly ... the times differ") is the beat of scene 6.
- **Repeats (scene 7; route "process repeated uptake/time measurements").** Three runs per condition, the bubble reset with the reservoir tap, a few minutes' settling after the fan goes on. Illustrative repeats (labelled): no fan 3.0, 2.8, 3.2 → mean 3.0; fan 8.0, 7.7, 8.3 → mean 8.0, so the site's single readings become the means.
- **Trust (scene 8; route "evaluate controls/leaks").** Valid (only wind changed; temperature checked), accuracy (the proxy bias is the same in both conditions, so the comparison stands), leaks (bubbles at the seal, or a bubble that jumps rather than slides: reseal and restart) and scope ("in this shoot, not in every plant"; site short answer 3).
- **The prediction beat (scene 2).** "If the leaves lose water faster, what will the bubble do?" with a 3 s countdown, reasoned from v13.
- **Risk table (scene 9).** Three spoken rows, each hazard, harm and control: the scalpel (cuts: cut away from fingers against a firm surface, cover the blade); the glass tube (snaps and cuts: never force it into a bung; the teacher fits it); electricity near water (shock: cord and power point away from water; wipe spills). Teacher-note rows, not shown: choose a non-poisonous plant species (some ornamental shrubs have toxic or irritant sap); a guarded fan; glassware assembled by the teacher; disposal of plant material. Codex to confirm against current NSW Department of Education guidance.
- **Memory aids.** "A potometer measures the drink, not the sweat." (the site's l11 hook, adapted; scene 3 and the notes). "Change one thing. Two suspects means no conviction." (FA1 v21's aid, recalled in scene 5). Both checked below.

> Last time, we predicted that wind speeds up water loss. But how would you show it? You can't see water vapour leaving a leaf. So here's the trick: measure the water going in instead. ### 3. uptake stands in for water loss
> It moves faster. So the bubble's speed gives the rate of water uptake. Notice: that's water taken in, not water lost. We use it as a stand-in, because almost all the water a shoot takes up is lost in transpiration. Almost: a little is used in photosynthesis, or kept in the cells to keep them firm. So uptake slightly overstates water loss. A potometer measures the drink, not the **sweat**.

### 4. setting up: keep the air out
> Setting up is all about keeping air out. Cut the shoot under water, so no air gets into its xylem. A bubble there would break the water column, and the shoot couldn't pull water up. Fit it into the potometer under water too, and seal the joint with petroleum jelly, so it's airtight. Then dry the leaves gently, and let the shoot settle for about ten minutes. A leak would let air in, and the bubble would move for reasons that have nothing to do with the **leaves**.

### 12. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Potometer: water lost from the leaves is replaced by water drawn in through the stem, so the air bubble moves along the graduated tube towards the shoot. Rate of water uptake = distance ÷ time (mm/min).
2. Uptake is a proxy for transpiration: it reads slightly high (a little water is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells). "A potometer measures the drink, not the sweat."
3. Set-up: cut and fit the shoot under water (no air in the xylem); seal with petroleum jelly (airtight); dry the leaves; settle about 10 min; reset the bubble with the reservoir tap.
4. Fair test (wind): change fan off/on (fixed distance and speed); measure rate of uptake; keep the same shoot, light, temperature (check with a thermometer), humidity. Three runs each, mean. Example: 18 mm in 6 min = 3 mm/min; 40 mm in 5 min = 8 mm/min.
5. Trust: valid (one factor changed), reliable (repeats close), proxy bias the same in both conditions, check for leaks, claim only this shoot. Risk: scalpel (cut away, cover blade); glass tube (don't force; teacher fits); fan cord away from water.

## Safety facts check (self-check, 2 Nov; science check to confirm)

- **Scalpel or blade.** Cuts. Cut away from fingers against a firm surface (under water in a dish); cover or retract the blade; teacher may pre-cut shoots. Learner copy as scene 9.
- **Glass capillary and tubing.** Snaps and cuts if forced into a bung; teacher assembles glassware (lubricated with water or glycerol). Learner copy as scene 9.
- **Electricity near water.** The fan is mains-powered beside water; keep cord and power point away from water, dry hands before touching switches (teacher note), wipe spills; use a guarded fan, tested and tagged per school policy (teacher note).
- **Plant material.** Choose a non-poisonous species (avoid, for example, oleander, whose sap and leaves are toxic, and plants with irritant milky sap); dispose of as green waste (teacher notes).
- **Petroleum jelly.** Low hazard; wipe from skin (no row needed).
- **Lamp (attempt only).** Hot lamp near water is covered in v07; no risk content is needed for a question card.
