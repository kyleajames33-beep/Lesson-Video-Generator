### 1. hook: same side, same hypotenuse
> Here's a road sign, held up by a diagonal brace two metres long. It meets the ground at thirty degrees and the post at sixty degrees, and it reaches one metre up the **post**. From the thirty degree angle, that one metre side is opposite, so sine thirty degrees is one over two. From the sixty degree angle, the same side is adjacent, so cosine sixty degrees is one over two as **well**. Same side, same hypotenuse. So it's no coincidence. The two angles add to ninety degrees, which makes them complementary. So why does a ratio of an angle equal the co-ratio of its **complement**?

### 2. the proof in a triangle
> Take any right-angled triangle, with an acute angle theta. The angles add to a hundred and eighty degrees, so the other acute angle is ninety degrees minus theta. Call the side opposite theta a, the side next to it b, and the hypotenuse c. Now look from the other angle. Side a, which was opposite theta, is adjacent to ninety degrees minus **theta**, and side b is now opposite. So sine of ninety degrees minus theta is b over c, which is cosine theta. Cosine of ninety degrees minus theta is a over c, which is sine **theta**. Tangent of ninety degrees minus theta is b over a, which is cotangent theta. Flip all three, and secant of ninety degrees minus theta is cosecant theta, cosecant of ninety degrees minus theta is secant theta, and cotangent of ninety degrees minus theta is tangent **theta**. That's where the co comes from. Cosine is the complement's sine. Writing sine theta equals sine of ninety degrees minus theta is the **slip**.

### 3. any angle: the mirror in y = x
> That proof needs a triangle, so it only covers acute angles. For any angle, use the circle. Here's the point at angle theta, x, y. The ray at ninety degrees minus theta makes the same angle with the y-axis as theta's ray makes with the x-axis, just measured the other way. Reflecting in the line y equals x swaps the two axes, so it carries one ray onto the other, and the point x, y lands on y, **x**. Try a hundred and fifty degrees. Ninety minus a hundred and fifty is minus sixty degrees. On a circle of radius one, a hundred and fifty degrees gives minus root three over two, and a half. Minus sixty degrees gives a half, and minus root three over two. Swapped **exactly**. So cosine of ninety degrees minus theta is the new x over r, which is y over r, sine theta. And sine of ninety degrees minus theta is cosine theta, for every **angle**. The other four follow by dividing and flipping.

### 4. which angles are excluded?
> Which angles are excluded? Sine and cosine exist for every angle, so those two identities hold everywhere. Tangent of ninety degrees minus theta equals cotangent theta, except where they don't exist. Cotangent theta needs sine theta to be non-zero, so this one fails at zero, a hundred and eighty degrees, and every half turn. The same angles are excluded for secant of ninety degrees minus theta equals cosecant theta. And the last pair, cotangent of ninety degrees minus theta equals tangent theta, and cosecant of ninety degrees minus theta equals secant theta, fail where cosine is zero, at ninety degrees, plus every half **turn**.

### 5. worked example: no special angle needed
> Here's where these identities earn their keep. Without a calculator, find sine twenty degrees times secant seventy degrees, plus tangent thirty-five degrees times tangent fifty-five degrees. None of these is a special angle. But twenty and seventy add to ninety, and so do thirty-five and fifty-**five**. Secant seventy degrees is one over cosine seventy degrees, and cosine seventy degrees is sine twenty degrees. So the first product is sine twenty degrees over sine twenty degrees, which is **one**. Tangent fifty-five degrees is cotangent thirty-five degrees, and a tangent times its own cotangent is one. So the answer is one plus one, which is **two**. Angles that add to ninety degrees are your signal.
