### 2. the airway, ending in a huge surface
> Air takes a fixed route. Through the nose, it's warmed, moistened and filtered. It passes the pharynx and the larynx, then the trachea, held open by rings of cartilage. Alveoli are built for exchange: a huge surface, walls one cell thick, a dense net of capillaries, and a difference in oxygen that stays steep, because breathing and blood flow keep refreshing it.

### 3. breathing by pressure change

### 5. vessels and blood, matched to their jobs
> The vessels match their jobs. **Arteries** carry blood away from the heart, under high pressure, so their walls are thick, muscular and elastic. Smaller arterioles act as taps. In exercise, they open towards working muscle and close down elsewhere. **Capillaries** have walls one cell thick, and they're the only place exchange happens. **Veins** return blood at low pressure, with one-way valves, and contracting muscles squeeze them, pumping blood back up. The blood itself is mostly plasma, carrying red cells with haemoglobin for oxygen, white cells for defence, and platelets for clotting.

### 6. two circuits, one pump
> So follow the blood. The heart runs two circuits, one after the other. In the **pulmonary circulation**, blood from the body enters the right atrium through the vena cava, passes the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, and is pumped out through the pulmonary valve, along the pulmonary artery to the lungs. It picks up oxygen and returns through the pulmonary vein to the left atrium. In the **systemic circulation**, it passes the bicuspid valve into the left ventricle, and out through the aortic valve into the aorta, to the body's capillaries, then back through the veins to the vena cava.

### 7. the rule, and the exception everyone gets wrong
> Now the mark most often lost. Arteries aren't defined by carrying oxygenated blood. They're defined by carrying blood away from the heart. In the body, that blood happens to be rich in oxygen. ### 8. one mechanism, twice
> Gas exchange is **diffusion**: gases move from where their partial pressure is higher to where it's lower, and no energy is spent moving them. **Partial pressure** is the share of the pressure one gas contributes. At the alveoli, oxygen is at about a hundred and four, and the arriving blood is at about forty, so oxygen moves into the blood, and carbon dioxide moves out. At the working muscle, it reverses. The blood arrives at about a hundred. The muscle's is about forty, lower in exercise, because it's using oxygen up. So oxygen moves in, and carbon dioxide moves into the blood. In between, about ninety-eight per cent of the oxygen rides on haemoglobin.

### 9. why both systems rise together
> So why do breathing and heart rate rise together? Both answer the same signals. The brain's respiratory centre sets breathing, and it responds mainly to rising carbon dioxide, detected by chemical sensors called chemoreceptors. Sensors in muscles and joints detect movement too. Carbon dioxide, not low oxygen, is the main trigger. And exchange speeds up in exercise for three reasons. The working muscle steepens the gradients. More capillaries open. And haemoglobin lets go of oxygen more easily where it's warm and acidic, which is exactly the muscle that needs it.
