### 2. benefit is spread, risk is concentrated
> The puzzle goes away once you separate two kinds of risk. Across a whole population, regular activity lowers the risk of most chronic diseases. But for one person during a hard session, the heart rate, blood pressure and the heart's workload rise sharply, as you saw in Year 11. That briefly raises the risk of a serious event, and the extra risk sits almost entirely in people with heart disease they don't know about. **Pre-exercise screening** is a structured check to find that small group before they're asked to work hard. It does three jobs. Safety: spot conditions that make hard or unsupervised exercise unsafe right now. **Duty of care**: the legal and ethical duty to take reasonable steps to prevent harm you could foresee. And personalisation: the same answers describe the person's history, medicines, injuries and goals. So screening isn't there because exercise is dangerous.

### 3a. the questionnaire: Stage 1 and question seven
> In Australia the standard tool is the **APSS**, the Adult Pre-Exercise Screening System. It has two stages. Stage 1 is compulsory for everyone. Questions one to six ask about known heart disease or stroke, unexplained chest pain, feeling faint or dizzy during activity, a recent asthma attack needing urgent care, trouble controlling diabetes, and any other condition needing special care. If all six are no, question seven asks how active you are, as **weighted minutes**: light and moderate minutes, plus twice the vigorous minutes, because a vigorous minute is about twice the work. Say a nineteen-year-old walks ninety minutes a week and plays thirty minutes of hard football. That's ninety plus sixty, a hundred and fifty. Below one fifty, the advice is light to moderate activity, built up gradually. At one fifty or more, continue at your current level.

### 4. acting on the answers: guidance, scope and judgement
> A screen only matters if someone acts on it. A yes to any of questions one to six means the person should get guidance from a doctor or an appropriate allied health professional before exercising. That isn't a diagnosis. It's about **scope of practice**, the limits of what a professional is trained and allowed to do. A personal trainer can't diagnose chest pain, so they make a **referral**, directing the person to someone who can. Stage 2 works differently. There's no automatic referral. The professional uses judgement. With significant or several risk factors, they may seek further medical advice. Otherwise they use what they've learned to set a safer starting intensity, a slower progression, or closer supervision.

### 5. what a questionnaire can't do
> Be precise about the limits too. A questionnaire is a filter, not a diagnosis. First, it relies on **self-report**. People may not know they have a condition, may forget, or may play down symptoms because they want to train. Second, it can't see disease with no symptoms, and that's exactly the group screening most wants to find. Third, it's a snapshot. Health changes, so people should be screened again at intervals, and after any injury, illness or pregnancy. So it's the first step, followed by watching during sessions and teaching people the warning signs.

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **APSS (3).** Adult Pre-Exercise Screening System version 2 (2019): Stage 1 compulsory, questions 1 to 6 (known heart disease or stroke; chest pain; faintness, dizziness or loss of balance; asthma attack needing immediate medical attention in the last 12 months; difficulty controlling diabetes; other condition needing special consideration); question 7 weighted physical activity (light and moderate minutes plus twice vigorous minutes; under 150 per week: light-to-moderate with gradual increases; 150 or more: continue); Stage 2 recommended, with an exercise professional; no fixed risk categories (as the site states, from the APSS User Guide V2).
- **Worked minutes (3).** Illustrative: 90 + 2 × 30 = 150. Arithmetic checked.
- **Acute risk (2).** Vigorous exertion transiently raises the risk of acute cardiac events, concentrated in people with existing (often undiagnosed) cardiovascular disease; regular activity reduces long-term risk.
- **Duty of care and scope (2, 4).** General legal and professional principles for registered exercise professionals; no jurisdiction-specific claim.
- **Re-screening (5).** At intervals and after changes in health status (injury, illness, pregnancy).

## Memory aid check

- **"Benefit is spread; acute risk is concentrated."** Assumption: most acute events occur in people with underlying disease.
