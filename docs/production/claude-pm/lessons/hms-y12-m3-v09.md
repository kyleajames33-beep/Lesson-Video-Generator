### 2. who pays, who delivers, who sets the rules

Why split it this way? An entitlement that must be the same everywhere needs one national payer. Hospitals need daily local management. So trace a GP visit. Medicare pays, and a private practice delivers it.

### 3. the non-government sector
> Outside government is the **non-government sector**, and it has two parts. **Private for-profit** providers deliver most GP and specialist care, private hospitals, pharmacy and health insurance. **Not-for-profit** organisations put any surplus back into their purpose. They deliver much of aged care, community care and disability services, community-controlled health services, and health charities. You met them as advocates in Year 11. Here's what they do that government can't. They earn trust where government reaches people badly, they advocate and hold government to account, they fund research and peer support, and they test new ideas small. Their limits matter too. Contracts are often short, they sit where they happen to be, not where need is, and profit doesn't always line up with health, so government regulates them. So public and private are far less separate than they look.

### 4. the lines cross: seams
> Draw a line from each funder to each service it pays for, and the lines cross. Every crossing is a **seam**, a boundary where responsibility blurs and a person can fall through. It has benefits. Decisions sit closer to people, and states can adapt to local needs. But it creates four predictable problems. **Cost-shifting**: when two funders each pay part of someone's care, each gains if the other pays more. Gaps at the boundary: a patient is ready to leave hospital, but no aged care place is funded, so they wait in a hospital bed. Duplication: different rules, forms and records for the same person. And accountability that's hard to find, because when care goes wrong, nobody owns all of it. They hit hardest on people who need several services at once: older people, people with disability, and people with several chronic conditions.

### 5. person-centred care, and the carry test
> That's why this dotpoint pairs with **person-centred care**. It isn't politeness, and it isn't patient satisfaction. It means care is organised around the person's goals and circumstances, not around what suits the providers. You can check it five ways. The person's goals set the plan. Decisions are shared, with options and risks explained before choosing. There's one plan everyone can see, including the person. There's a known contact who knows the history. And housing, income, carers, culture and language count as part of the care. Then one quick test. Does the person have to carry information between services? If they do, the system has handed its coordination job to the patient.

### 6. collaboration at three levels
> They work together at three levels, and each fails differently. Around the person: a team that shares one care plan, discharge planning that starts on admission, a care coordinator, and shared records. Between organisations: regional bodies that buy in services to fill local gaps, referral agreements between hospitals and community providers, and government contracting not-for-profits to deliver care. At the system level: funding agreements between governments, national standards, and shared data. It works when someone is accountable for the whole person, information moves without them, and funding follows the person. It fails when it sits on top of funding that still pulls providers apart, or rests on goodwill and short contracts.

### 8b. feedback, item one
> The home aged care is federally funded and delivered by a not-for-profit. That's a seam, beside state-run hospitals, where discharge delays happen. ## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing. No figure on screen.
- **Federal role (2).** Medicare (Medicare Benefits Schedule), the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme, aged care funding and regulation, disability support (the NDIS is jointly funded by the Commonwealth and states, administered nationally; "funds disability support" is a fair simplification), a large share of public hospital funding under the national health reform agreement, regulation of medicines (Therapeutic Goods Administration) and private health insurance.
- **Practitioner registration (not narrated).** National Registration and Accreditation Scheme under the Health Practitioner Regulation National Law, adopted by each state and territory (site correction 27).
- **State role (2).** Public hospitals, ambulance services, community and mental health services, public health and outbreak response; licensing of private hospitals.
- **Local role (2).** Food premises inspection (under state food legislation), immunisation clinics in some states (notably NSW and Victoria councils), environmental health, the built environment.
- **Non-government sector (3).** Most GP and specialist practice privately delivered and publicly subsidised; not-for-profit providers deliver a large share of residential aged care and community care (author: "much of"); Aboriginal community-controlled health services are not-for-profit (named by kind only).
- **Why the division (2).** WE b1's principle: national consistency (one payer), local operation (states), local knowledge (councils). Fiscal imbalance (the Commonwealth raises most tax revenue) is not narrated.
- **Seams (4).** Cost-shifting between Commonwealth and state funders; patients medically ready for discharge awaiting aged care placement; duplication; diffuse accountability.
- **Person-centred care (5).** Five elements as the site lists them (consistent with the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care's person-centred care principles).
- **Collaboration (6).** Primary Health Networks commission services (described as "regional bodies that buy in services"); referral pathways; government contracting of not-for-profits; intergovernmental hospital funding agreements; national safety and quality standards.

## Memory aid check

- **"The payer is often not the provider."** Assumption: true for most services named (GP, aged care, NDIS, private delivery of public care); not for every service (a council inspection, a state ambulance). 8b uses the exception, so the aid is taught with its limit.
