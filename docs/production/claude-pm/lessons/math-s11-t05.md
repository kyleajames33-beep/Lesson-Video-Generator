### 2. three zones, and the half-hour centre
> Australia has three time zones. Start with standard time, which runs for about half the year, from April to October. Western Australia uses Western Standard Time. The Northern Territory and South Australia use Central Standard Time. Queensland, New South Wales, the ACT, Victoria and Tasmania use Eastern Standard Time. You'll see them written A W S T, A C S T and A E S T, with the S for standard. Each zone keeps one time on all its clocks, rounded from the Sun's time, like we saw with longitude. Western time is two hours behind Eastern time. Central time is only half an hour behind Eastern, so it's an hour and a half ahead of Western. Half-hour zones are unusual, and that's what makes Adelaide and Darwin tricky. And the rule from longitude still holds. Moving west, the clocks are **behind**, so you take time off. Moving east, you add.

### 4. daylight saving: the south-east moves
> Now summer. Daylight saving means moving the clocks forward one hour for the warmer months, so there's an extra hour of daylight in the evening. In Australia it starts on the first Sunday in October and ends on the first Sunday in April. But not every state does it. New South Wales, the ACT, Victoria, Tasmania and South Australia go forward. Queensland, the Northern Territory and Western Australia don't. Watch the map as the months roll from July to January. The south-east jumps an hour ahead, and three zones split into five different times. When daylight saving ends in April, those clocks go back an hour, and the five times become three again. Sydney is now three hours ahead of Perth, an hour ahead of Brisbane, and an hour and a half ahead of **Darwin**. And here's the strangest part. In winter, Brisbane is half an hour ahead of Adelaide. In summer, Adelaide is half an hour ahead of Brisbane, even though it's further **west**.

### 5. a summer match

And Perth is three hours behind, four fifteen in the **afternoon**. Notice that Adelaide and Darwin, which shared a time all winter, are now an hour apart, because only Adelaide moved its clocks. Always ask two questions. Which zones are they in, and is it daylight saving **time**?

### 6. a flight across zones
> Time zones matter most when you travel. A plane leaves Perth at one pm in January, and the flight takes four and a half hours, an example time. Do the flight on Perth's clock first. One pm plus four and a half hours is five thirty pm, Perth time. Now convert. It's January, so Sydney is on daylight saving, three hours ahead of Perth. So the plane lands at eight thirty pm, Sydney **time**. Forget daylight saving, use two hours, and you'd say seven thirty, an hour **early**. The flight is still four and a half hours. Only the clocks have changed. Going the other way, the clocks work against you. A five hour flight leaving Sydney at nine am lands at two pm Sydney time, which is eleven am in Perth, just two hours after take-off by the clocks.
