### 2. what a model is for, and how to judge one
> First, what's a model for? A model is a simplified version of something we can't see or handle directly. Replication happens far too small and fast to watch in a classroom, though specialised methods can follow single molecules, so a model is how we study its steps. Simplifying isn't a flaw: it's the whole point. To assess a model, you weigh what it does well against what it gets wrong, then make a judgement. And you need a criterion, the test you judge it by. Here's a good one: does the model get the important relationships right, and does it say what it leaves out?

### 7. enzymes that seem to plan
> Here's the subtlest problem, because the model actually teaches a wrong idea. When you build it, you move each enzyme card in order, so it looks as if enzymes plan their work and wait their turn. Real enzymes don't plan. They're molecules, moved around by random motion and recruited into working complexes at each fork, with many forks working in parallel. The cards also hide each enzyme's shape, which is what lets it fit the DNA. And the model leaves out proofreading, so replication looks accurate by magic, not because polymerase checks each base.
