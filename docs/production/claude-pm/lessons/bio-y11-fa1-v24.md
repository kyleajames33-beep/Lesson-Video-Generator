### 2. what a model is for, and how to judge one
> First, what's a model for? A model is a simplified version of something we can't see or handle directly. Replication happens far too small and far too fast to watch, so a model is how we study its steps. Simplifying isn't a flaw: it's the whole point. To assess a model, you weigh what it does well against what it gets wrong, then make a judgement. And you need a criterion, the test you judge it by. Here's a good one: does the model get the important relationships right, and does it say what it leaves out?

### 7. enzymes that seem to plan
> Here's the subtlest problem, because the model actually teaches a wrong idea. When you build it, you move each enzyme card in order, so it looks as if enzymes plan their work and wait their turn. The cards also hide each enzyme's shape, which is what lets it fit the DNA. And the model leaves out proofreading, so replication looks accurate by magic, not because polymerase checks each base.

### 9. the judgement
> So, the judgement. Go back to the criterion. A digital model fills some of those gaps. The best plan is to use both. It shows the steps, not the scale.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- A model is a simplified version of something too small or fast to see. Judge it by whether it gets the important relationships right, and whether it says what it leaves out.
- Physical (bead) model shows well: pairing rule, fork and templates, leading and lagging strands (if directions are marked), enzyme order, semi-conservative result; hands-on.
- It hides or distorts: helix twist and unwinding, scale, speed (about 1000 bases per second in bacteria, about 50 per fork in humans), tens of thousands of forks at once, enzymes moving by random motion (not planning), enzyme shapes, proofreading.
- Digital model: shows twist, real shapes, speed and many forks; but you watch rather than build, and it is still simplified.
- Judgement: very useful for learning the steps; not for scale, speed or chemistry. Use both and state the limits.

Memory: it shows the steps, not the scale.

1. (10a, assess, 4 marks.) A student's claim: "Our model shows exactly how DNA is copied in a cell."
   Model: Two accurate features, each explained, for example: the model represents complementary base pairing, since beads are paired only by the colour rule standing for A–T and C–G (1 mark); it shows semi-conservative replication, since each daughter model has one original strip and one new strip (1 mark). Two misrepresented features, for example: scale, since real DNA is about 2 nm wide, twisted into a helix and enormously long, while the model is flat, straight and millions of times larger; speed and number of forks, since real forks add about 50 (human) to 1000 (bacterial) bases per second at many forks at once, while the model is built slowly at one fork; or the model suggests enzymes act in a planned order, whereas they move by random motion and act at many forks simultaneously (1 mark). Judgement: the claim is not supported; the model is useful for showing the steps and relationships of replication but does not show exactly how it occurs in a cell (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed out: "the beads are the wrong colour" (colour is a code). Other wrong chains: listing features without saying whether they are accurate; no judgement.
2. (10c, calculate and identify, 3 marks.) E. coli, 4.6 million base pairs, one origin, two forks, 1000 base pairs per second each.
   Model: (a) Each fork copies 4 600 000 ÷ 2 = 2 300 000 base pairs (1 mark); 2 300 000 ÷ 1000 = 2300 s ≈ 38 minutes (1 mark). (b) Any one: two forks working at the same time in opposite directions; the real speed (about 1000 base pairs per second); the starting point (origin) from which both forks move (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed out: "4 600 000 ÷ 1000 = 4600 s ≈ 77 minutes" (one fork only). Other wrong chains: answering in seconds only when minutes are asked; dividing by 2000 without explaining.
