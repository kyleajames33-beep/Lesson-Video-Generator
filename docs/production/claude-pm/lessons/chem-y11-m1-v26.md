### 1. hook: two small molecules, far apart
> Water and methane are both small molecules, with ten electrons each. Water boils at a hundred degrees Celsius. Methane boils at about minus one hundred and sixty-two. That's more than two hundred and sixty degrees **apart**. And hydrogen sulfide, a bigger molecule than water, boils at about minus **sixty**. To explain this, we need to see what actually happens when a molecular substance **boils**.

On "sixty" an H₂S model (S yellow) appears with "18 e⁻" and its mark drops to "−60 °C" on the strip, a grey "?" beside it. On "boils" the heading writes on: "Boiling points of molecular substances".

### 2. what boiling breaks
> When water boils, the molecules move apart into the gas, but each molecule stays whole. H two O is still H two O in the steam. So boiling doesn't break the covalent bonds inside the molecules. It overcomes the attractions between molecules, called intermolecular **forces**. Inter means between. Stronger intermolecular forces need more energy to overcome, so the boiling point is **higher**. Writing that boiling breaks the O H bonds is one of the most common exam **mistakes**.

Visual: board clears to the boiling lens: water molecules in a liquid with violet dotted lines between them; as the first sentence runs, molecules leave the surface whole, each keeping an ink ring. On "forces" two labels write: "intermolecular: between molecules (overcome on boiling)" on the dotted lines, and "intramolecular: within a molecule (covalent bonds, unchanged)" on the O–H bonds. On "higher" a chip "stronger intermolecular forces → higher boiling point". On "mistakes" a chip "boiling breaks O–H bonds" fades in and is crossed (caption "molecules stay whole").

### 3. dispersion forces
> There are three kinds of intermolecular force. The first acts between all molecules, and it's called dispersion. Electrons are always moving, so at any instant one side of a molecule can have slightly more electrons than the other. That's a temporary **dipole**. It pushes on the electrons of the next molecule, making a matching dipole there, and the two attract for a **moment**. More electrons make a bigger, more easily distorted cloud, so dispersion gets stronger. Fluorine boils at minus one hundred and eighty-eight, chlorine at minus thirty-four, bromine at fifty-nine, and iodine at a hundred and eighty-**four**.

Visual: board clears to two F₂ molecules with soft blue clouds. On "dipole" the left cloud sloshes to one side, δ− and δ+ flicker on. On "moment" the right cloud shifts in response, a light blue dotted line flickers between them, tag "dispersion: temporary dipoles, in all molecules". On the fifth sentence the halogen row draws with electron tags "18, 34, 70, 106 e⁻" and growing clouds; marks drop onto the strip as each is named, "F₂ −188, Cl₂ −34, Br₂ 59, I₂ 184 °C". On "four" an arrow writes beside the marks "more electrons → stronger dispersion → higher boiling point", grey Sources tag "NIST; PubChem".

### 4. a fair test
> To see the other two forces, let's run a fair test. Here are three molecules with the same number of electrons, eighteen each, so their dispersion forces are similar. Fluorine, F two, boils at minus one hundred and eighty-eight. Fluoromethane, C H three F, boils at minus **seventy-eight**. And methanol, C H three O H, boils at sixty-**five**. Same electron count, so something else is adding **attraction**.

Visual: board clears to the fair-test card: three columns F₂, CH₃F, CH₃OH (turning models), header "18 e⁻ each" fixed with a grey tag "similar electron count: similar dispersion (shapes differ a little)". ### 5. dipole–dipole forces
> Fluoromethane is a polar molecule. As we saw in the molecular shapes video, one polar bond on a tetrahedral carbon isn't balanced, so the molecule has a permanent dipole, with a slightly negative fluorine **end**. The negative end of one molecule attracts the positive end of the **next**. That's a dipole dipole force. It acts on top of dispersion, so fluoromethane boils about a hundred and ten degrees higher than **fluorine**.

Visual: two CH₃F models side by side; on "end" the red resultant arrow from v23 draws on each, δ− at F and δ+ at the CH₃ end. On "next" the molecules turn so the δ− end of one faces the δ+ end of the other; an orange dotted line draws between them, tag "dipole–dipole: permanent dipoles, polar molecules only". The light blue dispersion line stays beside it, tag "dispersion too". On "fluorine" the fair-test card's CH₃F column fills "dispersion + dipole–dipole" and the first gap's "?" becomes "about 110 °C".

### 6. hydrogen bonds
> Methanol goes further. Its hydrogen is bonded directly to oxygen, which pulls the shared electrons so strongly that the hydrogen is left very positive. That hydrogen is attracted to a lone pair on an oxygen in the next **molecule**. That's a hydrogen bond. It forms only when hydrogen is bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine, and the next molecule has a lone pair on one of those **atoms**. Despite its name, it's an attraction between molecules, not a covalent bond. It's the strongest of the three, so methanol boils about a hundred and forty degrees higher than **fluoromethane**.

Visual: two CH₃OH models; the O–H hydrogen glows blue "δ+", the oxygen's two lone-pair lobes violet. ### 9. total attraction decides
> But don't rank by the strongest label alone. Iodine has only dispersion forces, yet it boils at a hundred and eighty-four degrees, higher than **water**. Each iodine molecule has a hundred and six electrons, so its dispersion forces add up to more than water's hydrogen bonds. Boiling point depends on the total attraction, not just the type of **force**.

### 10. the method and a line to remember
> So here's the method for any comparison. List every force, starting with dispersion, which is always there. Polar molecule? Add dipole dipole. Hydrogen on nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine? Add hydrogen bonding. Then compare electron counts, and conclude that the stronger total attraction needs more energy to **overcome**. To remember the hydrogen bond rule, think H-FON: H on F, O or **N**.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 52 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Boiling a molecular substance overcomes intermolecular forces (between molecules); covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break.
2. Dispersion forces: temporary dipoles; in all molecules; stronger with more electrons (F₂ −188 °C, I₂ 184 °C).
3. Dipole–dipole forces: between polar molecules (permanent dipoles), in addition to dispersion.
4. Hydrogen bonding: H bonded to N, O or F, attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule; strongest per contact (H₂O 100 °C against H₂S −60 °C).
5. Boiling point depends on the total attraction: list all forces, then compare electron counts (I₂ boils above water).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style).** "Hydrogen fluoride (HF) boils at 19.5 °C. Hydrogen chloride (HCl) boils at −85 °C, even though an HCl molecule has more electrons than an HF molecule. Explain this difference in boiling points in terms of intermolecular forces. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Both HF and HCl are polar molecules with dispersion and dipole–dipole forces between their molecules (1). In HF, hydrogen is bonded to fluorine, so HF molecules also form hydrogen bonds (H–F···H–F), which are much stronger than the other intermolecular forces (1). Although HCl has more electrons and so slightly stronger dispersion forces, the hydrogen bonding in HF gives a stronger total attraction, so more energy is needed to separate HF molecules and its boiling point is higher (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "the H–F bond is stronger, so more energy is needed to break it".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style).** "Propane (CH₃CH₂CH₃), methoxymethane (CH₃OCH₃) and ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) each have 26 electrons per molecule. Their boiling points, in no particular order, are −42 °C, −24 °C and 78 °C. Match each boiling point to its substance and justify your answer in terms of intermolecular forces. (4 marks)"
Model answer: The equal electron counts mean dispersion forces are similar, so the other forces decide (1). Propane, −42 °C: non-polar, dispersion forces only (1). Methoxymethane, −24 °C: polar C–O bonds in a bent arrangement give dipole–dipole forces as well as dispersion; it cannot hydrogen bond because no hydrogen is bonded to oxygen (1). Ethanol, 78 °C: its O–H group allows hydrogen bonding as well as dispersion and dipole–dipole forces, the strongest total attraction (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "methoxymethane forms hydrogen bonds because it contains oxygen".
