### 2. what boiling breaks
> When water boils, the molecules move apart into the gas, but each molecule stays whole. H two O is still H two O in the steam. So boiling doesn't break the covalent bonds inside the molecules. It overcomes the attractions between molecules, called intermolecular **forces**. Inter means between. Stronger intermolecular forces need more energy to overcome, so the boiling point is **higher**. The O H bonds stay **intact**.

### 3. dispersion forces
> There are three kinds of intermolecular force. The first acts between all molecules, and it's called dispersion. Electrons are always moving, so at any instant one side of a molecule can have slightly more electrons than the other. That's a temporary **dipole**. It pushes on the electrons of the next molecule, making a matching dipole there, and the two attract for a **moment**. More electrons make a bigger, more easily distorted cloud, so dispersion gets stronger. Fluorine boils at minus one hundred and eighty-eight, chlorine at minus thirty-four, bromine at fifty-nine, and iodine at a hundred and eighty-**four**.

### 5. dipole–dipole forces
> Fluoromethane is a polar molecule. As we saw in the molecular shapes video, one polar bond on a tetrahedral carbon isn't balanced, so the molecule has a permanent dipole, with a slightly negative fluorine **end**. The negative end of one molecule attracts the positive end of the **next**. That's a dipole dipole force. It adds to dispersion, which helps explain why fluoromethane boils about a hundred and ten degrees higher than **fluorine**.

### 6. hydrogen bonds
> Methanol goes further. Its hydrogen is bonded directly to oxygen, which pulls the shared electrons so strongly that the hydrogen is left very positive. That hydrogen is attracted to a lone pair on an oxygen in the next **molecule**. That's a hydrogen bond. It forms only when hydrogen is bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine, and the next molecule has a lone pair on one of those **atoms**. Despite its name, it's an attraction between molecules, not a covalent bond. It's the strongest of the three, so methanol boils about a hundred and forty degrees higher than **fluoromethane**.

### 8. water against hydrogen sulfide and methane
> Water. Its hydrogens are bonded to oxygen, and each oxygen has two lone pairs, so water molecules hydrogen bond to each other. Sulfur isn't electronegative enough, so hydrogen sulfide has only dispersion and weak dipole dipole forces. That's why water boils about a hundred and sixty degrees higher, even though hydrogen sulfide has more **electrons**. And methane is non-polar, so it has only weak dispersion, and boils lowest of the **three**.

### 9. total attraction decides
> But don't rank by the strongest label alone. Iodine has only dispersion forces, yet it boils at a hundred and eighty-four degrees, higher than **water**. Each iodine molecule has a hundred and six electrons, so its dispersion forces add up to more than water's hydrogen bonds. Boiling point depends on the total attraction, not just the type of **force**.

### 10. the method and a line to remember
> So here's the method for any comparison. List every force, starting with dispersion, which is always there. Polar molecule? Add dipole dipole. Hydrogen on nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine? Add hydrogen bonding. Then compare electron counts, and conclude that the stronger total attraction needs more energy to **overcome**. To remember the hydrogen bond rule, think H-FON: H on F, O or **N**.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style).** "Hydrogen fluoride (HF) boils at 19.5 °C. Hydrogen chloride (HCl) boils at −85 °C, even though an HCl molecule has more electrons than an HF molecule. Explain this difference in boiling points in terms of intermolecular forces. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Both HF and HCl are polar molecules with dispersion and dipole–dipole forces between their molecules (1). In HF, hydrogen is bonded to fluorine, so HF molecules also form hydrogen bonds (H–F···H–F), which are much stronger than the other intermolecular forces (1). Although HCl has more electrons and so slightly stronger dispersion forces, the hydrogen bonding in HF gives a stronger total attraction, so more energy is needed to separate HF molecules and its boiling point is higher (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "the H–F bond is stronger, so more energy is needed to break it".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style).** "Propane (CH₃CH₂CH₃), methoxymethane (CH₃OCH₃) and ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) each have 26 electrons per molecule. Their boiling points, in no particular order, are −42 °C, −24 °C and 78 °C. Match each boiling point to its substance and justify your answer in terms of intermolecular forces. (4 marks)"
Model answer: All three have dispersion forces, and with equal electron counts the large spread in boiling points comes mainly from the other forces present (1). Propane, −42 °C: non-polar, dispersion forces only (1). Methoxymethane, −24 °C: polar C–O bonds in a bent arrangement give dipole–dipole forces as well as dispersion; it cannot hydrogen bond because no hydrogen is bonded to oxygen (1). Ethanol, 78 °C: its O–H group allows hydrogen bonding as well as dispersion and dipole–dipole forces, the strongest total attraction (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "methoxymethane forms hydrogen bonds because it contains oxygen".
