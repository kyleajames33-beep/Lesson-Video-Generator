  1. L13 "why it matters": "your own aerobic respiration is why a breathalyser, or a CO₂ sensor, can detect that you are alive and active". A breathalyser measures ethanol in the breath, not carbon dioxide, and it detects drinking, not respiration. Proposed: "every breath out carries carbon dioxide, a product of the equation you have just learned; a carbon dioxide sensor can detect it".
  2. L13 explain answer step 3: "Releasing the energy of a glucose molecule in a single step would produce a large burst ... raising the cell's temperature enough to denature its enzymes". This is a hypothetical stated as fact, and a single glucose molecule's energy could not heat a cell to denaturing temperature. Proposed: "would release the energy as heat, with no way to capture it in ATP; small steps release amounts small enough to be transferred to ATP".
  3. L13 vocabulary and model answers: "release energy stored in its chemical bonds", "captured in the bonds of ATP", "transferred into the terminal bond of an ATP molecule". Breaking a bond takes in energy; energy is released overall because the products (carbon dioxide and water) store less chemical energy than glucose and oxygen. Year 11 Chemistry students meet this. Proposed: "release chemical energy stored in glucose" and "transferred to ATP". Wording finding, minor; this video never says energy is stored "in bonds".
  4. L13 card 3: "This is why you breathe hard after a sprint, your muscles ran short of oxygen and built up lactic acid." Fast breathing after exercise has several causes (repaying the oxygen used to restore stores and remove lactate, raised carbon dioxide); lactic acid build-up alone is a simplification. Not used in the video. Root to judge whether to log (possibly "design-suggested, not categorical").

### 3. the word equation

### 4. formulas, and why we balance
> Now the formulas. Glucose is C six, H twelve, O six: six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and six oxygen atoms. Oxygen gas is O two, carbon dioxide is C O two, and water is H two O. But written like that, the atoms don't add up. Atoms can't be created or destroyed in a reaction, so we balance the equation, one element at a time.

### 8. energy is released, not created
> What about the energy? It's written on the right, but it isn't a substance, and respiration doesn't create it. Glucose stores chemical energy, which plants originally captured from sunlight. Part of it is transferred to a molecule called ATP, and the rest is lost as heat, which is part of why your body stays warm.

### 9. ATP, the cell's coins

### 10. why the cell doesn't catch fire
> So why doesn't the cell catch fire? Because that single arrow is only a summary. It stands for dozens of small reactions, each controlled by an enzyme, a protein that speeds up one particular reaction. Each step releases a small amount of energy, small enough to be caught in ATP. A flame releases everything in one rush, so nothing can catch it. That's why respiration is called a controlled release of energy.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (2 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Equation balance (scenes 4 to 6, notes).** C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O. C: 6 = 6. H: 12 = 12. O: 6 + 12 = 18 = 12 + 6. Unbalanced row counts (scene 4): C 6 | 1; H 12 | 2; O 6 + 2 = 8 | 2 + 1 = 3. Correct.
- **Same overall equation for combustion (scenes 1, 2).** Complete combustion of glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O, releasing about 2,800 kJ per mole (not spoken). Correct. Note: the flame plate shows glucose burning on a spoon; pure sugar needs strong heating to ignite and also chars. The plate is an illustration, not a student practical; no burning is shown as a class activity (grey tag "demonstration image").
- **Oxygen ends in water; carbon dioxide's carbon from glucose (scene 7).** Oxygen is the final electron acceptor and is reduced to water; all six carbons of glucose leave as carbon dioxide (the CO₂ oxygen atoms come from glucose and water, not from O₂; isotope tracing). "oxygen picks up the hydrogen stripped from glucose" simplifies electrons and hydrogen ions carried by NADH and FADH₂ (some hydrogen originates from water added in the Krebs cycle). Acceptable at Year 11; flag for the science check. "keeps the whole process running" (without oxygen the electron transport chain stops, NADH accumulates and the Krebs cycle stops. Correct as a simplification.
- **Carbon dioxide diffuses out (scene 7).** Correct.
- **Energy (scene 8).** Glucose and oxygen have more chemical potential energy than carbon dioxide and water; the difference is released. "plants originally captured from sunlight": true for food chains based on photosynthesis (chemosynthetic exceptions not mentioned). About 30 to 40 per cent of the energy is captured in ATP and the rest is released as heat (no number spoken; "part of it"). Respiration heat contributes to body temperature in endotherms ("part of why your body stays warm"). Correct.
- **ATP uses (scene 9).** Active transport (pumps), protein synthesis, movement (muscle contraction). ATP is regenerated from ADP and phosphate (grey tag). Correct.
- **Steps (scene 10).** Aerobic respiration involves glycolysis (10 steps), the link reaction, the Krebs cycle (8 steps) and the electron transport chain: "dozens of small reactions" is safe; each step catalysed by a specific enzyme (the electron transport chain uses protein complexes; acceptable). Correct.
- **Location (scene 11).** Glycolysis in the cytoplasm splits glucose into two pyruvate (three-carbon) molecules with a small net ATP gain; the link reaction, Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria, where most ATP is made and oxygen is used. Muscle cells have many mitochondria. Correct.
- **Breathing and plants (scene 12).** Plants have no lungs and do not ventilate; they exchange gases by diffusion through stomata and lenticels; plant cells respire continuously, day and night. Correct.
- **Anaerobic (scene 13).** Without oxygen, the mitochondrial stages stop; glycolysis continues, followed by conversion of pyruvate to lactate (animals) or ethanol and carbon dioxide (yeast, some plant tissues); far less ATP per glucose (2 against about 30). "lactic acid" used as on the site (lactate in the body; author note). Yeast carbon dioxide raises bread dough. Correct.

Oxygen falling and carbon dioxide rising in a sealed flask over two days: plausible. Peas germinating in the dark inside a flask do not photosynthesise. Correct.
