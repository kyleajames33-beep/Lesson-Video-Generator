### 2. the variable is concentration
> The variable here isn't how much you drink. It's how concentrated your blood is: how much dissolved salt and other particles there are in each litre of blood plasma. Biologists call this **osmolarity**. Lose water by sweating or breathing out, and the same particles sit in less water, so osmolarity rises. Drink lots of water, and osmolarity falls. Remember osmosis from Year 11: water moves across a membrane towards the side with more dissolved particles. That rule explains most of this video.

### 3. a hundred and eighty litres
> Your kidneys control how much water leaves. Each kidney holds about a million tiny tubes called **nephrons**. At the start of each one, blood is filtered, and water and small particles are pushed into the tube. This fluid, the filtrate, then flows along the tubule, and most of it is taken back into the blood. That's **reabsorption**. Filtering isn't choosy about water, so control comes from what's taken back. Here's the surprise: your kidneys filter around a hundred and eighty litres a day, yet you pass only about one and a half. The last stretch, the **collecting duct**, sets how much of the rest you keep.

### 4. who's measuring: hypothalamus, pituitary, ADH
> So who's measuring? In the hypothalamus are **osmoreceptors**, cells that sense blood concentration. When blood gets too concentrated, water leaves them by osmosis and they shrink slightly. That's the detection. The hypothalamus then sends nerve impulses down to the posterior pituitary, the back part of a small gland just below it. The pituitary releases **ADH**, antidiuretic hormone, into the blood. ADH is made in the hypothalamus, but it's stored in and released from the pituitary. So one step is neural, and the next is hormonal. The hypothalamus also makes you thirsty, because your kidneys can save water, but only drinking replaces it.

### 5. ADH opens water channels
> ADH travels in the blood to the collecting ducts. There, it binds to the duct cells and moves water channels, called **aquaporins**, from inside the cells into the membrane facing the filtrate. The tissue around the ducts is very concentrated, so once the channels are in, water moves out of the duct by osmosis and back into the blood. Less water is left in the duct, so you make a smaller volume of more concentrated urine.

### 6. the loop closes
> As water returns to the blood, osmolarity falls back towards normal. The osmoreceptors swell back, their signals weaken, and less ADH is released. ### 8. aldosterone: water follows salt
> One more hormone often turns up beside ADH. **Aldosterone**, from the adrenal glands on top of the kidneys, responds mainly to low blood volume or pressure, such as after heavy fluid loss. It makes the later parts of the nephron reabsorb more sodium. When water channels are present, water follows the sodium by osmosis, helping restore blood volume. Aldosterone doesn't move water directly. ADH changes how much water can leave the duct, and aldosterone changes how much salt is taken back.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Water balance regulates blood osmolarity (the concentration of dissolved particles), not the volume drunk. Water moves by osmosis towards more dissolved particles.
- Nephron: blood is filtered, and most of the filtrate is reabsorbed (about 180 L filtered a day, about 1.5 L passed). The collecting duct sets the final water kept.
- Osmolarity rises → osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus shrink → nerve impulses to the posterior pituitary (neural) → ADH released into the blood (hormonal; made in the hypothalamus) → aquaporins inserted in collecting ducts → water reabsorbed by osmosis → small volume of concentrated urine → osmolarity falls (negative feedback). ## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (30 Oct) against standard references. The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Hook.** Small volumes of dark urine when water intake is low; a large volume of pale urine starting within about an hour of drinking a litre of water quickly (water diuresis typically peaks around 40 to 90 minutes). Spoken: "within an hour or so". Codex to confirm.
- **Osmolarity (scene 2).** Plasma osmolality is regulated around about 285 to 295 mOsm/kg; mainly sodium salts. No number is spoken or shown. "Biologists call this osmolarity": strictly, measured plasma values are osmolality (per kilogram of water); the syllabus-level texts use both. Codex to judge whether "osmolarity" or "osmolality" is the better learner term (site error 6).
- **Nephrons and filtration (scene 3, key note 2).** About one million nephrons per kidney (the site; standard texts give roughly 0.8 to 1.5 million). Glomerular filtration about 180 L a day in an adult; urine about 1 to 2 L a day. Spoken: "about a million", "around a hundred and eighty litres", "about one and a half". Correct.
- **Osmoreceptors and ADH (scene 4).** Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus (the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis and nearby regions) respond to cell shrinkage; magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei make ADH (vasopressin), and their axons carry it to the posterior pituitary, where nerve impulses trigger its release into the blood. Thirst is also triggered by the hypothalamus. Correct.
- **Aquaporins (scene 5).** ADH binds V2 receptors on the blood side of collecting duct principal cells, and aquaporin-2 channels stored in vesicles are inserted into the membrane facing the filtrate; water leaves by osmosis into the hyperosmotic medullary interstitium and returns to the blood. Spoken: "The tissue around the ducts is very concentrated". Correct. The medulla's gradient is stated as a condition, not explained (author note).
- **ADH level (scene 6; Revision 2, science S2).** ADH secretion varies with osmolality, and a large water load can suppress it to undetectable levels. Spoken: "ADH release varies with concentration and can become extremely low after a large water intake." The Revision 1 "doesn't drop to zero" claim is removed.
- **Reverse (scene 7).** Water load lowers osmolality, ADH falls, aquaporin-2 is removed from the apical membrane by endocytosis, and dilute urine follows. Correct.
- **Aldosterone (scene 8).** Revision 2 (science S4): water following sodium is now conditional in speech ("When water channels are present"). Secreted by the adrenal cortex in response mainly to angiotensin II (from low blood volume or pressure, via renin) and to high plasma potassium; acts on the late distal tubule and collecting duct to increase sodium reabsorption; water follows by osmosis. Spoken: "responds mainly to low blood volume or pressure". The potassium trigger is left out (not needed at this level; a review may judge it). Correct at this level.
- **Attempt 1 biology (Revision 2, learning L1 and science S1).** A salt load raises plasma osmolality, so ADH and thirst rise, and urine becomes more concentrated over the following hours. Conserving filtered water limits further water loss but adds no water, so without drinking it cannot by itself restore osmolarity; drinking supplies the water. Over a longer time the kidneys also excrete the extra sodium (credited, not taught). "drinks nothing for the next two hours" keeps the prediction about the first hours.
- **Attempt 2 data.** Invented teaching data shaped on classic water-load experiments (oral water load of about 1 L producing a diuresis peaking near 60 minutes at several mL per minute, returning by about 3 hours) and on the small response to isotonic saline over the same period. Real isotonic saline can raise urine output modestly over several hours through volume-sensitive pathways; the graph's slight rise (1.0 to 1.5 mL/min) allows for that. Codex to confirm the shapes and peak are plausible.
