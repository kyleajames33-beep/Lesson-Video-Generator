## Decisions

- **Anchor: the early bus (d02's frame).** Bus students get up earlier, so they may sleep less. A sample with too few or too many of them would lean the results. That gives "representative" a concrete meaning: the sample should look like the population in the ways that matter for the question (here, where students live).
- **One sampler, four methods (recurring model).** The 200-dot field is the population for every method, so the methods differ only in how dots are chosen. Order: census (1 sentence), random, systematic, stratified, self-selected; then a comparison.
- **Plain meanings at first use.** Census ("collecting data from every member of the population"); representative ("a sample that looks like the population in the ways that matter for the question"); random sample ("every member has the same chance of being chosen"); systematic sample ("choose every k-th member from a list, after a random start"); stratified sample ("split the population into groups, then randomly choose from each group in proportion to its size"); self-selected sample ("people choose to take part themselves"); convenience ("asking whoever is easiest to reach", one sentence).

### 1. hook: which twenty?
> Two hundred Year 11 students, and we want to ask twenty. Sixty of them catch the early bus, so they get up sooner, and they might sleep less. If our twenty were mostly bus students, our results would make the whole year look more tired than it is. If there were none, it would look less tired. Either way, the sample wouldn't look like the population. So which sampling method gives a sample that represents the **population**?

### 2. census, then a random sample
> Asking every member of the population is called a census. It's the most complete, but it takes time and money, and for big populations it's often impossible. So we sample. And a sample is only useful if it's representative, meaning it looks like the population in the ways that matter for our question. Here, that's where students live. The first method is a random sample, where every member has the same chance of being chosen. That's not the everyday meaning of random, just grabbing whoever. It means a fair draw, like a random number generator on a calculator, picking students by their roll numbers. Let's run it. This draw picked four bus students. Run it again, nine. Again, six. Random sampling is fair, because nobody chooses who's in. But with a small sample, chance can still give you a lopsided **one**.

### 3. a systematic sample
> A systematic sample picks every k-th member from a list. Divide the population by the sample size. Two hundred divided by twenty is ten, so we take every tenth student on the roll. Start at a random place between one and ten, say seven. Then seven, seventeen, twenty-seven, and so on, up to one ninety-**seven**. That's twenty students, spread evenly through the list. It's quick, and it usually behaves like a random sample. The catch is a list with a pattern. If every tenth student on the roll happened to be a bus student, a step of ten could pick all bus students, or **none**.

Visual: stage change: a numbered list strip (roll numbers 1 to 200 in rows of 20, 32 px) beside a smaller field. Row, 52 px: "k = 200 ÷ 20 = 10" (hand tag "every 10th"). On "say seven" a pointer lands on 7; on "seventeen, twenty-seven" it steps by 10 with a trail; on "seven" (first bold) row "start 7: 7, 17, 27, ..., 197" (framed green) and the 20 chosen dots light in the field (counter "bus: 6 of 20" for this list). On "a pattern" the list recolours so every tenth row entry is amber-outlined; the pointer steps again and lands only on amber (counter "bus: 20 of 20", red flash); on "none" it starts at 8 and lands on none ("bus: 0 of 20"). Grey chip "only if the list has a pattern".

### 4. a stratified sample
> A stratified sample makes sure the groups that matter are there in the right amounts. Split the population into groups, then randomly choose from each group in proportion to its size. A hundred and forty of two hundred is seventy per cent, so seventy per cent of twenty, fourteen students from nearby. Sixty of two hundred is thirty per cent, so six bus **students**. Then choose the fourteen and the six at random. Every run gives fourteen and six, so the bus students are always fairly represented. A common slip is ten from each group. That gives bus students far more say than their share of the **year**.

Visual: stage change: the field splits into two labelled blocks, "nearby 140" (blue) and "bus 60" (amber-outlined). Task strip, 44 px: "Stratified sample of 20 from 140 nearby and 60 bus students." ### 5. a self-selected sample
> The last method is a self-selected sample, where people choose to take part themselves. Say we pin a poster in the corridor. Scan this code to tell us about your sleep. Twenty replies come in, and fourteen say they slept under seven hours. Does that mean most of Year 11 is short on sleep? Not necessarily. Students who feel tired have a reason to answer, and students who sleep well might walk straight past. The sample chose itself, so it leans. And getting two hundred replies wouldn't fix it. A bigger self-selected sample still leans the same **way**. Asking whoever's easiest to reach, like the canteen queue, called a convenience sample, has the same problem.

### 6. comparing the four methods
> So how do they compare? Random gives everyone the same chance, but a small sample can be unlucky. Systematic is quick and spreads out, if the list has no pattern. Stratified guarantees the groups that matter are in proportion, but you need to know the groups first. Self-selected is easy, but it leans towards people with strong feelings. To justify whether a sample is representative, look at how it was chosen. Did every member, or every group, get a fair chance? For our sleep survey, stratified is the best choice, because where students live affects when they wake **up**.

Visual: stage change: a comparison table, 40 px, four rows (random, systematic, stratified, self-selected) and three columns "how chosen", "strength", "watch out": random | "same chance" | "fair" | "small samples can be unlucky"; systematic | "every k-th" | "quick, spread out" | "patterned lists"; stratified | "groups in proportion" | "groups always fair" | "need the groups"; self-selected | "volunteers" | "easy" | "leans: strong feelings" (red outline on "leans").
