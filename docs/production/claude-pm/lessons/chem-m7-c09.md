**Boundary:** this video starts by identifying the carboxyl group and its intermolecular forces, and stops at explaining why a carboxylic acid is a weak acid while an alcohol is not, with the observations that show it. It teaches: the carboxyl group (–COOH) is a carbonyl (C=O) and a hydroxyl (O–H) on the same carbon; the two oxygens differ, and there is only one O–H; inside the molecule the bonds are covalent, and both the C=O and the O–H are polar (the intramolecular part of `C7-IQ5-02`); naming (longest chain including the carboxyl carbon, drop the final "e", add "oic acid"; the carboxyl carbon is C1, so no locant; it is counted in the chain; branches numbered from it: 2-methylpropanoic acid); hydrogen bonding: the O–H donates and the C=O oxygen accepts, so two molecules can pair with two hydrogen bonds (a dimer), plus dipole–dipole forces from the C=O, so for similar sizes a carboxylic acid boils higher than an alcohol (ethanoic acid 118 °C against propan-1-ol 97 °C, both about 60 g/mol); within the series, boiling point rises with chain length (more dispersion), and solubility in water falls after butanoic acid; in water the O–H hydrogen can transfer to water as H⁺, forming H₃O⁺ and the carboxylate ion (ethanoate), but the reaction is reversible and only a small fraction ionises (weak, not dilute; Module 6 recall: pH about 2.9 at 0.10 mol/L, about 1 molecule in 80); why: the charge left on the carboxylate is shared across both oxygens, so it does not take the proton back as strongly; an alkoxide's charge would sit on one oxygen; so pKa about 4.7 against about 16 (Module 6 recall: lower pKa, stronger acid; each unit a factor of 10); evidence: blue litmus turns red with the acid and stays blue with the alcohol; reactions as an acid (Module 6 recall): with sodium hydroxide (salt and water), with magnesium (salt and hydrogen), salts named "-oate"; the sodium hydrogen carbonate test: the acid is stronger than carbonic acid, so it gives a proton to hydrogen carbonate, and the carbonic acid breaks up into water and CO₂, so it fizzes; an alcohol does not; the equation includes CO₂. **Not here:** the organic base and the acid against base comparison (chem-m7-c11, route review change 3); amines and amides; esters and esterification (chem-m7-c10, next); phenol (beyond the 2017 syllabus; the site marks it as extension; author note f); chloroethanoic acid and inductive effects (author note f); pH or Ka calculations (Module 6); salt hydrolysis and the pH of sodium ethanoate (Module 6); - **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Boiling points (CRC Handbook values, rounded for speech): ethanoic acid 117.9 °C ("one hundred and eighteen"); propan-1-ol 97.2 °C ("ninety seven"); methanoic acid 100.8 °C ("about one hundred"); propanoic acid 141.2 °C ("one hundred and forty one"); butanoic acid 163.8 °C ("about one hundred and sixty four"); 3-methylbutanoic acid 176.5 °C ("one hundred and seventy seven"); hexan-1-ol 157.6 °C ("one hundred and fifty seven"). Molar masses: ethanoic acid 60.05, propan-1-ol 60.10, 3-methylbutanoic acid 102.13, hexan-1-ol 102.17 g/mol. Electrons: ethanoic acid C₂H₄O₂ 12 + 4 + 16 = 32; propan-1-ol C₃H₈O 18 + 8 + 8 = 34; 3-methylbutanoic acid C₅H₁₀O₂ 30 + 10 + 16 = 56; hexan-1-ol C₆H₁₄O 36 + 14 + 8 = 58. In both pairs the acid has slightly fewer electrons, so dispersion cannot explain its higher boiling point (c05's "Same size? Hydrogen bonding wins." applies, with the carboxyl group's stronger hydrogen bonding). (b) Why acids boil higher: liquid carboxylic acids are extensively hydrogen bonded, as cyclic dimers and also in chains; the vapour just above the boiling point of ethanoic acid contains a large fraction of dimers. The standard HSC explanation (two hydrogen bonds per pair in a dimer, plus dipole–dipole forces from the C=O, so stronger attractions overall) is what the video teaches; it avoids the site's two contradictory claims (site correction 3). The C=O also makes the O–H more polar, strengthening its hydrogen bonds; not taught. (c) Solubility in water at about 20 to 25 °C: methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic and butanoic acids miscible; pentanoic acid about 2.4 g per 100 mL (sources range from about 2.4 to 5 g per 100 mL); hexanoic acid about 1 g per 100 mL. The narration says "up to butanoic" and "solubility falls". (d) Acid data at 25 °C: ethanoic acid Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ (some tables 1.75 × 10⁻⁵), pKa 4.74 to 4.76 ("about four point seven"); at 0.10 mol/L, [H₃O⁺] = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.34 × 10⁻³ mol/L, pH 2.87 ("about two point nine", chem-m6-c05's value), 1.3% ionised ("about one in eighty", chem-m6-c05's rounding of 1 in 79 from pH 2.9). Ethanol pKa about 15.9 and propan-1-ol about 16.1 in water ("about sixteen"; values for alcohols vary between sources, 15.5 to 16.1). ### 4. two hydrogen bonds per pair

That pair is called a **dimer**. Add dipole-dipole forces from the C double bond O, and acid molecules attract each other more strongly overall. More energy is needed to pull them apart, so the acid boils **higher**.

### 7. why the acid lets go, and the alcohol doesn't

If an alcohol lost its proton, the charge would be stuck on **one** oxygen, and it would snatch the proton straight back. That's why ethanoic acid's p K a is about four point seven, and an alcohol's is about **sixteen**. Each unit is a factor of ten.
