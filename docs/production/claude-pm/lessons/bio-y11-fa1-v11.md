### 2. name the solution: compare outside with inside
> Start by comparing the solution outside the cell with the solution inside it. Look at the solute, what's dissolved, like salt or sugar. If the outside has less solute than the inside, it's **hypotonic**. Hypo means under. The same amount, and it's **isotonic**. Iso means equal. More solute outside, and it's **hypertonic**. Hyper means over. So a solution isn't hypertonic on its own. It's hypertonic to a particular **cell**.

### 3. which way the water goes
> Now the water. Remember the sugar bag: water moved in, towards the sugar. More solute means fewer water molecules in each drop, so the net movement of water is towards the side with more solute. In a hypotonic solution, more water crosses **in** than out. In a hypertonic solution, more crosses **out**. In an isotonic solution, water still crosses both ways, but equally, so there's no net **movement**.

### 4. an animal cell in distilled water (a quick prediction)
> Let's try an animal cell, a red blood cell, dropped into distilled water. Quick prediction: what happens to it? Distilled water is hypotonic, so water moves **in**, and the cell swells into a ball. An animal cell has only its thin, flexible membrane, with nothing outside to push back. If water keeps coming in, the membrane stretches until it tears, and the cell **bursts**. That's called lysis. In a slightly hypotonic solution, it may just **swell**.

### 5. isotonic and hypertonic: the animal cell
> In an isotonic solution, like the plasma in blood, water enters and leaves at the same rate, so the cell keeps its normal, dimpled disc **shape**. In a hypertonic solution, such as salty water, water leaves, and the cell shrinks and wrinkles into a spiky shape. That's called **crenation**. So an animal cell is safest in an isotonic solution. That's why a hospital drip uses salt water at the same concentration as blood, not pure **water**.

### 6. the plant cell: what the wall does
> Plant cells react differently, and the reason is the cell wall. The wall is strong, but it's fully permeable, which means water and dissolved substances pass straight **through** it. So the wall doesn't change which way water moves. It changes what happens to the cell. And in a plant cell, most of the water moves into and out of the large central **vacuole**.

### 7. hypotonic: the plant cell goes turgid
> Put a lettuce leaf cell in water, which is hypotonic. Water moves in, and the vacuole swells, pressing the membrane and cytoplasm against the wall. The wall stretches only a little, then pushes back. That push is called turgor **pressure**. When it's strong enough, there's no more net water movement, and the cell is firm, or **turgid**. It doesn't burst. Millions of turgid cells pressing on their walls hold the leaf crisp. That's the lettuce **answer**.

### 8. isotonic: flaccid, and a surprise
> In an isotonic solution, there's no net movement of water, so the vacuole doesn't press hard on the wall, and the cell is soft, or **flaccid**. Here's the surprise. The solution that's safest for an animal cell isn't best for a plant cell. A plant needs a hypotonic surrounding, so its cells stay turgid and hold it **up**. Without that push, soft stems and leaves **droop**.

### 9. hypertonic: plasmolysis
> In a hypertonic solution, like strong salt water, water leaves the vacuole. It shrinks, and the membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the wall. That's called **plasmolysis**, and the cell is plasmolysed. So what fills the gap between the membrane and the wall? The outside **solution**, because the wall lets it straight through. The wall keeps its shape, but the living part of the cell inside has **shrunk**.

### 10. the rule, and the slug
> So here's the rule. Compare the solutes, outside versus inside. Send the water towards more solute. Then check for a wall. The direction is the same for both cells, and only the result **differs**. And the slug? Salt dissolves in the moisture on its skin, making a hypertonic film, so water leaves its skin cells, and it **shrivels**. A short way to hold the direction: hyper means more salt, not more water. Water follows the **salt**.

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Definitions (scene 2).** Hypotonic, isotonic and hypertonic are relative terms, comparing solute concentration (of solutes that cannot cross the membrane) outside with inside. Spoken: "It's hypertonic to a particular cell." Correct.
- **Net movement (scene 3).** Water crosses both ways; net movement towards higher solute concentration. Matches v09 and v10 wording ("fewer water molecules in each drop"). Codex to confirm against v09 once final.
- **Red blood cells (scenes 4, 5, A1).** In distilled water they swell and haemolyse; in isotonic solution (about 0.9% sodium chloride, not spoken) they keep their biconcave shape; in hypertonic solution they crenate. "In a slightly hypotonic solution, it may just swell": true (red cells tolerate some swelling before lysis).
- **Blood plasma is isotonic with red blood cells (scene 5).** Correct at normal concentrations.
- **IV drip (scene 5).** Isotonic saline is used for many drips; spoken as "salt water at the same concentration as blood". Some clinical fluids are not isotonic; the narration says "a hospital drip uses", which Codex should check for an absolute; if needed, "a hospital drip usually uses" (re-count +8).
- **Wall (scene 6).** The cellulose wall is fully (freely) permeable to water and small solutes; strong; resists expansion. Correct.
- **Turgor (scene 7).** Water entry stops when turgor pressure balances the tendency of water to enter; the cell does not burst. Spoken without water potential. Codex to confirm "When it's strong enough, there's no more net water movement".
- **Isotonic plant cell (scene 8).** Flaccid; no turgor; plants droop. Site b1 step 5.
- **Plasmolysis (scene 9, A2).** The protoplast shrinks away from the wall; the space between wall and membrane fills with the external solution. Reversible (deplasmolysis) if cells survive; A2 states the cells were still alive.
- **Slug (scenes 1, 10).** Salt dissolves in surface mucus, forming a concentrated (hypertonic) solution; water leaves the skin cells by osmosis. Correct in substance.
- **Lettuce (scenes 1, 7).** A wilted leaf in cold water regains crispness as cells take up water and become turgid again. Correct.
- **Elodea (A2).** A freshwater plant whose leaf cells are a standard plasmolysis specimen; the plate's leaf cell has chloroplasts, matching an Elodea leaf cell.
