**Boundary:** this video starts from the route's sample and the question to answer and stops at justifying a suitable method and stating what its measurement establishes. It teaches: the first decision (is the question the sample's pH, how acidic it is now, or the amount of acid or base in total?); why a pH reading is not an amount for a weak acid (C12 callback, one sentence and a tag); what a digital pH probe measures (a voltage across a thin glass membrane that depends on hydronium; about 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C) and why it must be calibrated with two buffers (pH 7.00 sets the zero point, a second buffer sets the slope); digital instruments in industry (continuous pH monitoring with automatic dosing at a treatment plant; the autotitrator that records a pH curve and finds the jump); choosing a titration from the sample's properties (clear, soluble, fast: direct with an indicator; - **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Probe slope: 2.303RT/F = 0.05916 V at 298.15 K ("about fifty nine millivolts per pH unit at twenty five degrees"); 0.06154 V at 310.15 K (new correction 4). The sign depends on the meter's convention; the narration says only "changes the voltage". (b) Glass electrode: hydrated gel layers on both glass surfaces exchange ions and respond to hydrogen-ion activity; the potential across the membrane, measured against a reference electrode, follows the Nernst relationship (draft correction 3; new correction 2). The video's sentence ("hydronium ions interact with the outer surface of the glass, and that sets up a small voltage across it") stays at that level; inner reference solution, reference electrode and Nernst equation are Extension. (c) Calibration: pH 7.00 (often 6.86 or 7.00) sets the zero point (offset); pH 4.00 (acidic samples) or 10.00 (basic samples) sets the slope; buffers should bracket the samples where possible (for vinegar at pH 2.4, a pH 4.00 and 7.00 calibration extrapolates a little; a pH 1.68 or 2.00 buffer would bracket it, so scene 4 says "ideally on either side"); rinse with distilled water between buffers; temperature compensation corrects the theoretical slope (draft correction 3; new corrections 5 and 14). "A neat number that's simply wrong" refers to an uncalibrated probe with drifted offset or slope. ### 4. calibrating with two buffers
> But the meter has to be told which voltage means which pH, and that **drifts** as the glass ages and as the temperature changes. So you calibrate it with two **buffers** of known pH.
