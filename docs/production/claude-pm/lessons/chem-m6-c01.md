### 2. how models change
> To answer that, follow how the idea changed. In chemistry, a model is a definition that predicts which substances will act as acids or bases. A model gets replaced when someone finds an observation it **can't explain**. That's happened three times in about a hundred and fifty years, and each new model fixed the failure of the one before.

Visual: the timeline band draws across the top with four empty dated slots: "1770s", "1810s", "1880s", "1923". On "three times" three small amber arrows appear between the four slots. Hold about 1 second.

### 3. Lavoisier (acids contain oxygen)
> In the late seventeen hundreds, Antoine Lavoisier studied sulfuric, nitric and phosphoric acid. Every one contained **oxygen**, so he decided oxygen is what makes an acid. He even named the element for it: oxygen means "**acid maker**". Then, in 1810, Humphry Davy showed that hydrochloric acid contains only hydrogen and chlorine. **No oxygen**. One acid without oxygen was enough to break the rule.

Visual: name card fills slot 1: "Antoine Lavoisier, Paris, 1770s: acids contain oxygen" (text only). Main zone: three coded formula tiles H₂SO₄, HNO₃, H₃PO₄; on "oxygen" every O in them glows red. ### 4. Davy (acids contain hydrogen)
> So Davy proposed that **hydrogen**, not oxygen, is the key ingredient in acids. But plenty of hydrogen compounds aren't acids at all. **Methane** is full of hydrogen, and it isn't acidic. And the idea said nothing about **bases**.

### 5. Arrhenius (ions in water)
> In the eighteen eighties, Svante Arrhenius brought in ions. He said an acid is a substance that breaks apart in water to release **hydrogen ions**, and a base breaks apart in water to release its own **hydroxide ions**. Hydrogen chloride gives hydrogen ions, and sodium hydroxide gives hydroxide ions. Mix them, and hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions join to make **water**. That explained neutralisation, and why so many acids behave alike: they all release the **same ion**.

### 6. Arrhenius and ammonia (a made-up compound)
> And the Arrhenius model still works for acids and hydroxide bases in water. But back to ammonia. Under Arrhenius, a base releases **its own** hydroxide ions, the way sodium hydroxide does. Ammonia has none to release. To make it fit, chemists kept writing ammonia solution as "**ammonium hydroxide**", N H four O H. But no one has ever isolated a substance made of ammonium ions and hydroxide ions. The model needed a **made-up compound** to cover a gap.

### 8. Brønsted and Lowry (donate and accept a proton)
> In 1923, two chemists, Johannes Brønsted in Copenhagen and Thomas Lowry in Cambridge, independently published the same fix. Stop asking which ions appear in water. Ask what **moves**. An acid is a **proton donor**: it gives away a hydrogen ion. A base is a **proton acceptor**: it takes one. A hydrogen ion is a hydrogen atom that has lost its only electron, so it's just a **proton**.

Visual: slot 4 fills with two linked cards: "Johannes Brønsted, Copenhagen, 1923" and "Thomas Lowry, Cambridge, 1923", joined by a bracket "independently". ### 9. hydrogen chloride in water (hydronium)
> Watch it work. Hydrogen chloride dissolves in water. One hydrogen moves, as a proton, onto a water molecule. So hydrogen chloride is the **acid**, the donor, and water is the **base**, the acceptor. Water with an extra proton is **H three O plus**, the hydronium ion. That's what H plus, aqueous, really means: a proton in water is always **attached to water**.

### 10. ammonia in water (where the hydroxide comes from)
> Now the hook. Ammonia dissolves in water, and this time **water** is the donor. A water molecule gives a proton to ammonia, which becomes an **ammonium ion**. What's left of the water molecule is a **hydroxide ion**. The hydroxide **comes from water**, not from ammonia. Only some ammonia molecules do this at any moment, which is why the equation has the **double arrow** you met in equilibrium. Ammonia is a base because it **accepts a proton**.

### 11. the gas tube and carbonate, solved
> The other two problems fall the same way. In the glass tube, hydrogen chloride hands a proton **directly** to ammonia. No water needed. The ammonium ions and chloride ions that form pack together as the white solid. And a **carbonate ion** in water takes a proton from a water molecule, leaving hydroxide behind. So carbonates are bases for the same reason ammonia is: they're **proton acceptors**.

1. Lavoisier (1770s): acids contain oxygen. Broken by HCl, an acid with no oxygen.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why models change: an observation the model cannot explain (2). Why Lavoisier failed: HCl has no oxygen (3). Why Davy failed: methane has hydrogen and is not acidic; no bases (4). Why acids behave alike under Arrhenius: they all release H⁺ (5). Why ammonia defeats Arrhenius: it has no OH⁻ of its own, and the patch compound was never isolated (6). Why the gas reaction defeats Arrhenius: no water (7).
