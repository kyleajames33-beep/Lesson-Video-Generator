### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Selective toxicity: a drug harms the pathogen much more than the host, often by hitting something the pathogen has and our cells lack, or have in a different form.
- Antibiotics act on bacteria: penicillin stops wall building, so growing bacteria burst (our cells have no wall); others block bacterial ribosomes. Some kill (bactericidal); some stop growth so the immune system clears the rest (bacteriostatic). They have no effect on viruses (no wall, no ribosomes), but still select resistant bacteria in the body.
- Antivirals block a step the virus needs: entry, copying (viral polymerase) or release (oseltamivir blocks flu neuraminidase). Many target a viral enzyme; some block a host part the virus needs. They stop new viruses but don't destroy existing ones, so the flu antiviral helps most early; for HIV, treatment controls an established infection. Most are narrow, though some act on several virus families; there are far fewer of them than antibiotics.
- Resistance: the drug selects microbes that already carry resistance; it doesn't create it. Bacteria share resistance genes on plasmids, even between species. Bacteria become resistant, not people. Combination therapy (HIV, three drugs) makes resistance to all three at once very unlikely.
- Use well: not for viral infections, not routinely in healthy farm animals, exactly as prescribed, reserve antibiotics. Assess effectiveness by selectivity (why it spares our cells), breadth and how long a drug keeps working (every use selects resistance; the burden in 2021: about 1.14 million deaths directly caused by resistant bacteria).

Memory: Antivirals block a step the virus needs. The drug selects resistance; it doesn't create it.

2. (12c, explain, 3 marks.) Virus W makes about 10¹⁰ new particles a day; about 1 in 10⁵ carries resistance to drug A, and the same for B and C, independently; each drug blocks a different step. Explain why A, B and C together are more effective than A alone.

With drug A alone, about 10¹⁰ ÷ 10⁵ = 10⁵ particles a day already resist A; drug A kills or blocks the others, so the resistant ones survive and multiply, and the treatment fails (1 mark). With all three drugs, a particle survives only if it resists A, B and C at once; because resistance arises independently, that is about 10⁻⁵ × 10⁻⁵ × 10⁻⁵ = 10⁻¹⁵ per particle, or about 10¹⁰ × 10⁻¹⁵ = 10⁻⁵ triple-resistant particles a day (about one every 270 years) (1 mark). So combination therapy prevents resistant virus from being selected and keeps the drugs effective (1 mark). Author note, not learner copy: the model is simplified (it ignores existing partly resistant variants, unequal drug levels and missed doses, which is why adherence matters). ## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (23 Oct) against the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Oseltamivir (hook).** Cochrane review (Jefferson et al., 2014): in adults, time to first alleviation of symptoms 16.8 hours shorter (95% CI 8.4 to 25.1); Dobson et al. (Lancet 2015, individual patient data): about 25 hours shorter in confirmed influenza (median 97.5 against 122.7 hours); guidelines advise starting within 48 hours of symptom onset, with greater benefit when earlier. Narration: "can shorten the illness by about a day, if it's started within two days of the first symptoms". Hedged with "can". Checked 23 Oct.
- **Penicillin.** Beta-lactams bind penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases) and block peptidoglycan cross-linking in growing bacteria; the weakened wall fails under osmotic pressure and the cell lyses. "Water rushes in, and the cell bursts": correct as a simplification. Human cells have no wall. Correct.
- **Ribosomes.** Tetracyclines bind the bacterial 30S subunit; bacterial ribosomes are 70S, human cytoplasmic ribosomes 80S. (Mitochondrial ribosomes resemble bacterial ones, which explains some side effects; not spoken.) "Shaped differently from ours": correct.
- **Bactericidal and bacteriostatic.** Penicillins kill; tetracyclines mainly stop growth, and the immune system clears the rest (site card 1). Correct as stated.
- **Viruses.** No wall, no ribosomes; use host ribosomes and raw materials. Antibiotics for a viral infection select resistant bacteria among the normal flora (site card 2). Correct.
- **Neuraminidase and oseltamivir.** Influenza neuraminidase cleaves sialic acid so new virions are released from the infected cell; oseltamivir inhibits it, so virions stay bound to the cell surface and clump (site MC explanation). Correct. "Other antivirals block a virus's own copying enzyme" (aciclovir: herpesvirus DNA polymerase; remdesivir: SARS-CoV-2 RNA polymerase) "or stop it getting into a cell" (enfuvirtide blocks HIV fusion; maraviroc blocks the host receptor CCR5). The screen tag says "HIV entry blockers", which covers both. Correct.
- **"Far fewer antivirals than antibiotics."** Approved antivirals number roughly a hundred, covering about ten viruses (De Clercq and Li, Clin Microbiol Rev 2016: 90 approved antiviral drugs in 13 groups for 9 diseases); antibiotics number in the hundreds across many classes. "Far fewer": correct as a comparison; the screen stacks are labelled "rough comparison, not to scale".
- **Resistance.** Selection of pre-existing or newly arising resistant variants; plasmid transfer (conjugation) between bacteria including different species. "The few that already carry resistance": hedged (site error 9 note: usually pre-existing). "Never the person": correct.
- **HIV.** HIV produces about 10¹⁰ virions a day in an untreated person, with a high error rate of reverse transcriptase (about 3 × 10⁻⁵ per base per cycle). Monotherapy (AZT from 1987) led to resistance; combination therapy with three drugs from 1996: two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (the "copy" step) plus a protease inhibitor (the "build" step: cutting viral proteins so new particles mature); the first entry (fusion) inhibitor, enfuvirtide, came in March 2003 (SC 1). Current first-line therapy is still three drugs from at least two classes. Revision 2 narration "hitting at least two different steps" and the two-copy, one-build lock picture follow this. Treated HIV is controlled, not cured; life expectancy with early treatment approaches normal. "Billions of times a day", "from 1996", "under control for decades": correct.
- **Resistance burden (scene 10).** GRAM study (Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, Lancet, 16 September 2024): about 1.14 million deaths directly attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2021, 4.71 million associated; forecast 1.91 million attributable in 2050. Narration "In 2021, resistant bacteria directly caused about one point one million deaths worldwide". Dated; time-sensitive only in that newer estimates may appear. Revision 3 (interim B11 learning review, finding 3): used only as evidence of the size of the burden in one year, never as evidence of a trend; the durability judgement rests on selection.
