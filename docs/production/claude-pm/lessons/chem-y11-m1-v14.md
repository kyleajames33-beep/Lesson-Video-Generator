## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "How do first ionisation energy and electronegativity follow atomic structure?" The answer, in order: both measure the nucleus's grip on outer electrons, one alone and one in a bond (2, 7); the grip rises across a period and falls down a group for v13's reasons (3, 5, 8); subshells explain the two dips (4); the two quantities still differ (9).
- **Opening frame (equals v13's final frame).** The sodium and chlorine shell models at true relative size, an orange "energy?" arrow tugging sodium's outer electron, a second chlorine sharing a pair with the first ("pull?"), and the chip "Next: how do first ionisation energy and electronegativity follow atomic structure?".
- **Two quantities, one cause (route stop: "define the two different quantities").** First ionisation energy is a measured energy for an isolated gaseous atom losing an electron. Electronegativity is a relative number for an atom in a covalent bond attracting a shared pair. Both depend on how strongly the nucleus attracts outer electrons (core charge and distance, v13), so they follow the same main trends. Scene 9 states the difference plainly and uses the noble gases as the test.
- **Real data (author note a).** First ionisation energies from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database (via the PubChem periodic table, eV converted at 96.485 kJ/mol per eV, rounded to whole kJ/mol): Period 3 Na 496, Mg 738, Al 578, Si 787, P 1012, S 1000, Cl 1251, Ar 1521; Group 1 Li 520, Na 496, K 419, Rb 403, Cs 376. ### 1. hook: one grip, two measurements
> Last time, we saw that chlorine pulls hard on its outer electrons, and sodium holds its outer electron loosely. Chemists measure that grip in two different ways. One asks how much energy it takes to pull an electron right off an **atom**. The other asks how strongly an atom pulls on electrons it **shares** with another atom. These are first ionisation energy and electronegativity. Let's see how both follow the structure of the **atom**.

### 4. two small dips

### 7. electronegativity: a tug of war
> Now the second measure. In a covalent bond, two atoms share a pair of electrons. Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a bond attracts that shared **pair**. It's a scale, called the Pauling scale, and fluorine is the strongest, at four **point** **zero**.

### 8. same grip, same trends
> Electronegativity follows the same atomic structure. So the most electronegative elements sit at the top right of the table, and the least at the bottom left.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style).** "The first ionisation energy of sodium is 496 kJ mol⁻¹ and that of potassium is 419 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain this difference in terms of atomic structure. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Potassium has one more occupied electron shell than sodium, so its outermost electron is further from the nucleus (1). Although potassium has more protons, it also has more inner electrons shielding them, so the core charge (effective nuclear charge) on the outer electron is similar (+1) (1). The outer electron of potassium is less strongly attracted, so less energy is needed to remove it (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "potassium has more protons, so it holds its electron more tightly".

Model answer: (a) Argon (1521 kJ mol⁻¹) (1). (b) Chlorine (3.2) (1). (c) Electronegativity describes the attraction of an atom in a covalent bond for the shared pair; argon almost never forms bonds, so it is usually given no electronegativity value, while first ionisation energy can be measured for any gaseous atom (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "argon for both".
