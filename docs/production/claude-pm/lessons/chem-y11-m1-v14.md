### 1. hook: one grip, two measurements
> Last time, we saw that chlorine pulls hard on its outer electrons, and sodium holds its outer electron loosely. Chemists measure that grip in two different ways. One asks how much energy it takes to pull an electron right off an **atom**. The other asks how strongly an atom pulls on electrons it **shares** with another atom. These are first ionisation energy and electronegativity. Let's see how both follow the structure of the **atom**.

### 2. first ionisation energy
> First ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms. Gaseous just means the atoms are separate, with nothing else attached. For sodium, each atom loses one electron and becomes a sodium ion. That takes four hundred and ninety-six kilojoules for a mole of sodium **atoms**. It's called first, because it removes only the first, most loosely held **electron**.

### 3. across Period 3: a tighter grip
> Now go across Period three. From sodium to argon, the first ionisation energy generally rises, from four hundred and ninety-six, to fifteen hundred and twenty-one kilojoules per **mole**. That's the same cause as last time. The core charge climbs while the electrons stay in the same shell, so the atoms get smaller, and the outer electrons are held more **tightly**. It takes more energy to pull one **off**. Removing chlorine's outer electron takes about two and a half times the energy needed for sodium's.

### 4. two small dips
> But look closely. The rise isn't perfectly smooth. Aluminium is lower than magnesium. Aluminium's outer electron is the first one in a three p subshell, which is higher in energy than magnesium's three s, so it's easier to **remove**. And sulfur is slightly lower than phosphorus. Sulfur's fourth three p electron has to share an orbital with another electron. The two repel each other, so one of them is easier to **remove**. Exam answers say first ionisation energy generally **increases** across a period.

### 6. down a group: further out, easier to remove
> Lithium. Going down Group one, each element has an extra occupied shell, so the outer electron is further from the **nucleus**. The core charge stays at plus one, so the extra protons don't help. Further away means a weaker attraction, so less energy removes the electron. First ionisation energy falls from five hundred and twenty for lithium, to three hundred and seventy-six for **caesium**.

### 7. electronegativity: a tug of war
> Now the second measure. In a covalent bond, two atoms share a pair of electrons. Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a bond attracts that shared **pair**. Think of a tug of war, with the shared pair as the **rope**. The atom with the stronger pull draws the pair toward itself. Electronegativity has no units. It's a scale, called the Pauling scale, and fluorine is the strongest, at four **point** **zero**.

Visual: board clears to two atoms side by side with a shared pair of blue dots between them, tag "covalent bond: a shared pair". A grey tag at the side: "not the same as electron affinity (energy change when an isolated atom gains an electron)".

### 8. same grip, same trends
> Electronegativity follows the same atomic structure. Across Period three, the core charge rises and the atoms shrink, so the pull on a shared pair gets stronger, from zero point nine for sodium to three point two for **chlorine**. Down Group seventeen, each extra shell puts the shared pair further from the nucleus, so the pull weakens, from four point zero for fluorine to two point seven for **iodine**. So the most electronegative elements sit at the top right of the table, and the least at the bottom left.

Visual: violet bars write across Period 3 as the narration runs: "Na 0.9, Mg 1.3, Al 1.6, Si 1.9, P 2.2, S 2.6, Cl 3.2"; on "chlorine" the tug-of-war pair slides further toward a chlorine nucleus with "+7". ### 9. two different quantities
> So both measures rise across a period and fall down a group, but they're not the same thing. Ionisation energy is an energy, for one separate atom losing an electron. Electronegativity is a number on a scale, for an atom in a bond pulling on a shared pair. The noble gases show the difference. Argon has the highest first ionisation energy in Period **three**. But noble gases almost never form bonds, so they usually have no electronegativity value at **all**. That doesn't mean zero. It means there's nothing to **measure**.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms, for example Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻ (496 kJ mol⁻¹).
2. It generally increases across a period (core charge rises, atoms smaller, outer electrons held more tightly) and decreases down a group (extra shell, outer electron further out, same core charge). Small dips: Mg to Al (new 3p subshell) and P to S (paired 3p electron).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style).** "The first ionisation energy of sodium is 496 kJ mol⁻¹ and that of potassium is 419 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain this difference in terms of atomic structure. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Potassium has one more occupied electron shell than sodium, so its outermost electron is further from the nucleus (1). Although potassium has more protons, it also has more inner electrons shielding them, so the core charge (effective nuclear charge) on the outer electron is similar (+1) (1). The outer electron of potassium is less strongly attracted, so less energy is needed to remove it (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "potassium has more protons, so it holds its electron more tightly".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style).** "Consider the Period 3 elements from sodium to argon. (a) Identify the element with the highest first ionisation energy. (b) Identify the element with the highest electronegativity. (c) Explain why the answers to (a) and (b) are different. (3 marks)"
Model answer: (a) Argon (1521 kJ mol⁻¹) (1). (b) Chlorine (3.2) (1). (c) Electronegativity describes the attraction of an atom in a covalent bond for the shared pair; argon almost never forms bonds, so it is usually given no electronegativity value, while first ionisation energy can be measured for any gaseous atom (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "argon for both".
