**Boundary:** this video prepares students to carry out and analyse the pH measurement practical; it does not claim that watching it is conducting it. It teaches: choosing a range of solutions (two acids, two bases, distilled water) at one concentration; what the meter's glass probe responds to and why calibration is needed; calibrating with buffers that cover the readings; the measuring routine (rinse, blot, immerse, wait for steady, repeat in fresh portions) and why each step matters; recording means; reading each value as hydronium against hydroxide ions, with each pH step a factor of ten; why distilled water open to air reads below 7; meter against universal indicator; variables; validity, reliability and accuracy; the risk assessment. **Placement limits (playlist order 7):** pH = −log[H⁺], pOH, Kw and every pH calculation are chem-m6-c07 (order 10), so no reading is calculated from a concentration and no concentration from a reading; expected values are not derived on screen. The relation to ions uses only what C05 taught (lower pH, more hydronium; one unit is ten times). Why HCl and acetic acid differ at the same concentration is C05's strength idea, recalled in one clause; **testing strength with pH is chem-m6-p04** (order 8), which uses 0.100 mol/L matched solutions and a dilute-HCl challenge, so this video makes no strength conclusion and no concentration-matching argument beyond "so the difference comes from the substance". Buffers are named as "solutions that hold a known pH" only (how they work is chem-m6-c11). Water's self-ionisation is chem-m6-c06; scene 7 says only "more hydronium than hydroxide" below 7.

- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) The meter: a glass electrode develops a potential that varies by about 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C (the Nernstian slope); the slope and the offset drift with electrode age, hydration and temperature, so a two- or three-point calibration fixes both. The narration says "a tiny voltage" and "the link between voltage and pH drifts as the probe ages, and it changes with temperature". Automatic temperature compensation corrects the electrode slope, not the sample's real pH change with temperature; hence "calibrate at the same temperature as your samples". (b) Ideal values at 25 °C (concentration model, not shown): 0.010 mol/L HCl pH 2.00; 0.010 mol/L CH₃COOH (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) pH 3.38; 0.010 mol/L NH₃ (Kb 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) pH 10.62; 0.010 mol/L NaOH pH 12.00. A meter responds to hydronium activity, not concentration: at an ionic strength of 0.010 mol/L the activity coefficient is about 0.90 (Davies equation), which raises a strong acid's measured pH by about 0.04 and lowers a strong base's by about 0.04. The class means (HCl 2.03, NaOH 11.96, acetic acid 3.40, ammonia 10.59) sit within that range, and NaOH and ammonia also lose a little to carbon dioxide absorption. None of this is learner copy; the video presents the readings as results. (c) Distilled water open to air: carbon dioxide at about 420 ppm gives equilibrium pH about 5.6; a sample not yet fully equilibrated reads about 5.7 to 6.2. Low ionic strength makes a glass electrode slow and drifting (high junction potential, poor conductivity), which is why the three water readings spread (5.82, 5.95, 6.10). Freshly boiled and cooled water reads closer to 7 until carbon dioxide redissolves. (d) Carry-over (A1): a film of about 0.05 mL of 0.010 mol/L NaOH on the probe holds about 5 × 10⁻⁷ mol OH⁻; in 20 mL of unbuffered water that is about 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L, enough to raise the pH to about 9.4 (partly offset by dissolved carbon dioxide), so 9.2 is plausible. In 20 mL of 0.010 mol/L acetic acid the same film would change the pH by only a few hundredths, which is why A1 uses water: samples with few ions of their own are the most sensitive. Scene 5 says carry-over "changes its reading" without claiming a size. (e) Significant figures: the meter displays two decimal places; readings and means are recorded to 0.01 as displayed. Concentrations are written 0.010 mol/L (2 s.f.). The HCl to acetic acid difference is 3.40 − 2.03 = 1.37, and 10^1.37 = 23.4, said as "about twenty three times" (2 s.f., from a difference with two decimal places). In A2 the readings have one decimal place, so the difference 2.0 gives 10^2.0 = 1.0 × 10², said as "about a hundred". (f) Blotting: blot, do not rub, because rubbing the glass bulb can build a static charge and slow the response (manufacturer guidance). Store the probe in its storage solution; never let it dry out (screen tag only). (g) Calibration order: pH 7.00 first (the offset), then 4.00 and 10.00 (the slope), following common meter instructions; schools follow their meter's manual (screen tag). (h) Pronunciation: "pH" as letters; "H three O plus" is not spoken ("hydronium"); "zero point zero one zero" digit by digit; "carbonic acid"; "acetic acid" (C05 usage; screen tag "ethanoic acid" once).

Main question: *A freshly calibrated pH meter reads distilled water as 5.8. Is the meter wrong, the method wrong, or the water? How do you measure the pH of a range of acids and bases so the readings can be trusted, and what does each reading tell you about the ions in the sample?*

No pH calculation (chem-m6-c07), no strength investigation (chem-m6-p04), no buffer chemistry (chem-m6-c11).

> This pH meter has just been calibrated, and it reads distilled water as **five point eight**. Shouldn't pure water be **seven**? So is the meter faulty? Hold that thought. In this practical you measure the pH of a range of acids and bases, and before you trust a number like this, the method has to be right.

### 2. the range of solutions
> Here's the range: hydrochloric acid, acetic acid, distilled water, ammonia solution and sodium hydroxide. The two acids and two bases are all **zero point zero one zero moles per litre**, so any difference comes from the substance, not the amount dissolved. Pour about twenty millilitres of each into a clean, **labelled** beaker, and use a **fresh portion** for every reading.

### 3. how the meter works
> How does the meter know? The probe has a thin glass **bulb** at its tip. Hydronium ions in the solution produce a tiny **voltage** across that glass, and the meter turns the voltage into a pH number. But the link between voltage and pH **drifts** as the probe ages, and it changes with temperature. So the meter has to be shown what the right answers look like.

Visual: the probe plate `m6-ph-probe-holder-v1.png` (Codex item 1: the probe alone, clamped in a small electrode holder) enlarges at centre; on "bulb" a pen ring circles the glass bulb and a cutaway lens opens on it: teal hydronium tokens near the outer glass surface. On "voltage" a small coded arrow labelled "tiny voltage" runs up the cable to the meter, and a converter strip writes: "voltage → pH". On "drifts" the strip's arrow wobbles and a grey tag writes "probe age, temperature". The lens closes. Hold about 1 second.

### 4. calibration with buffers
> That's **calibration**. You dip the probe into **buffer solutions**, which hold a known, steady pH. How they do that is a later video. Start with the pH seven buffer, then pH four, and add pH ten because you'll measure bases too. That way your readings fall **within** the range you calibrated. Calibrate at the same temperature as your samples, on the day you measure.

### 7. what the readings say about the ions
> So what does each number tell you about the ions? Below seven, a solution has more **hydronium** ions than hydroxide ions. Above seven, more **hydroxide**. And each pH step is **ten times**. Hydrochloric acid reads about one point four lower than acetic acid, so it has about **twenty three times** as many hydronium ions, at the same concentration. That's strength, from the strong and weak video, and the next practical tests it properly. Ammonia and sodium hydroxide both have extra hydroxide, sodium hydroxide far more.

### 8. so, the water
> Now the water. The meter wasn't faulty. **Carbon dioxide** from the air dissolves in water and forms a little **carbonic acid**, so water left open to the air really is slightly acidic. And with so few ions, the probe struggles to settle, so the readings **drift** and spread. Freshly boiled and cooled water reads closer to seven. So distilled doesn't always mean **neutral**.

Visual: the water beaker enlarges centre with its chip "5.96". On "Freshly boiled and cooled" a second beaker outline appears with a grey tag "boiled, cooled: closer to 7". Hold about 1 second.

### 9. meter or indicator paper?
> Why use a meter at all? **Universal indicator** paper gives a colour, which you match to a chart to about the nearest whole number, and two people can judge the same colour differently. The meter gives **two decimal places**. But careful: those decimals only mean something if the meter is **calibrated**. An uncalibrated meter can show a neat number that's simply wrong.

Visual: `m6-indicator-paper-chart-v1.png` in the right zone; coded pH numbers write under the six swatches. On "Universal indicator" a coded strip colour (orange-red) is held against the chart; a bracket spans two swatches, tag "about pH 2 to 3". On "two decimal places" the meter readout "2.03" sits beside it. On "calibrated" a chip "more decimal places = more accurate" fades in and is crossed (caption "only if calibrated"). A small uncalibrated meter outline flashes "7.43" against a "pH 7.00 buffer" label with a red cross. Hold about 1 second.

### 10. the variables
> So, the variables. The **independent variable** is which solution you test. The **dependent variable** is the pH. And the **controlled variables** are the concentration, the temperature, the volume in each beaker, the same calibrated meter, and the same rinsing routine.

Visual: the variables card writes in the right zone, one row per cue: "Independent: which solution"; "Dependent: pH (mean of three readings)"; "Controlled: 0.010 mol/L; 25 °C; 20 mL; same calibrated meter; same rinse routine". As each controlled item is spoken a pen link points to the matching thing (the bracket, the thermometer chip, the beaker levels, the meter, the wash bottle). Hold about 1 second.

### 11. validity, reliability and accuracy
> Now the three words from the equilibrium practical. **Validity**: are you measuring the pH of the solution you meant to? Rinsing, fresh portions and labelled beakers keep anything else out of the reading. **Reliability**: do repeated readings agree? For the acids and bases, yes. For the water, no, so it's less reliable. **Accuracy**: how close is the reading to the true pH? That depends on calibrating with buffers that cover your readings, at the right temperature.

Visual: three cards write in turn in the right zone, each with its plain question and this practical's answer, the previous card dimming: "Validity: the intended solution only? Rinse, blot, fresh portions, labels." "Reliability: repeats agree? Acids and bases within 0.02; water spread 5.82 to 6.10." (the table's columns return small with those ranges bracketed) "Accuracy: close to the true pH? Calibrate with buffers covering the readings; same temperature." A small grey footer: "NESA glossary: reliability, repeated measurements under identical circumstances give similar results." Hold about 2 seconds.

### 12. the risk assessment
> The risk assessment. At zero point zero one zero moles per litre, these acids and bases are too dilute to be **classified as hazardous**, but wear safety glasses anyway, because a splash in the eye can still irritate. ## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why one concentration: so differences come from the substance (2). Why calibrate: the voltage-to-pH link drifts with age and temperature (3, 4). Why the buffers must cover the readings: the meter is checked only across that range (4). Why rinse and blot: carry-over changes the next sample (5, A1).
