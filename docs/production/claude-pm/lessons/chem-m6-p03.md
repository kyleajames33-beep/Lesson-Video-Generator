- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) The meter: a glass electrode develops a potential that varies by about 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C (the Nernstian slope); the slope and the offset drift with electrode age, hydration and temperature, so a two- or three-point calibration fixes both. The narration says "a tiny voltage" and "the link between voltage and pH drifts as the probe ages, and it changes with temperature". Automatic temperature compensation corrects the electrode slope, not the sample's real pH change with temperature; hence "calibrate at the same temperature as your samples". Buffer values also change with temperature (the pH 10.01 buffer is about 10.06 at 20 °C; the pH 7.00 buffer about 7.02), so meters use the buffer's temperature table; another reason to calibrate at the samples' temperature. Calibration line: the meter fits signal against pH through the buffer points; readings a little beyond the outer buffers (here HCl 2.03 and NaOH 11.96, outside 4 to 10) use the line extended, with a small error; pH 2 and 12 buffers would cover them, and the honest rule is "buffers either side of 7, as close to the readings as you can". (b) Ideal values at 25 °C (concentration model, not shown): 0.010 mol/L HCl pH 2.00; 0.010 mol/L CH₃COOH (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) pH 3.38; 0.010 mol/L NH₃ (Kb 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) pH 10.62; 0.010 mol/L NaOH pH 12.00. A meter responds to hydronium activity, not concentration: at an ionic strength of 0.010 mol/L the activity coefficient is about 0.90 (Davies equation), which raises a strong acid's measured pH by about 0.04 and lowers a strong base's by about 0.04. The class means (HCl 2.03, NaOH 11.96, acetic acid 3.40, ammonia 10.59) sit within that range, and NaOH and ammonia also lose a little to carbon dioxide absorption. None of this is learner copy; the video presents the readings as results. (c) Distilled water open to air: carbon dioxide at about 420 ppm gives equilibrium pH about 5.6; a sample not yet fully equilibrated reads about 5.7 to 6.2. Low ionic strength makes a glass electrode slow and drifting (high junction potential, poor conductivity), which is why the three water readings spread (5.82, 5.95, 6.10). Freshly boiled and cooled water reads closer to 7 until carbon dioxide redissolves (Revision 2: grey screen tag only, "boiled to drive out CO₂, then cooled: closer to 7"). Carbonic acid gives hydronium by H₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) (small grey screen line in scene 8). (d) Carry-over (A1): a film of about 0.05 mL of 0.010 mol/L NaOH on the probe holds about 5 × 10⁻⁷ mol OH⁻; in 20 mL of unbuffered water that is about 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L, enough to raise the pH to about 9.4 (partly offset by dissolved carbon dioxide), so 9.2 is plausible. In 20 mL of 0.010 mol/L acetic acid the same film would change the pH by only a few hundredths, which is why A1 uses water: samples with few ions of their own are the most sensitive. Scene 5 says carry-over "changes its reading" without claiming a size. (e) Significant figures: the meter displays two decimal places; readings and means are recorded to 0.01 as displayed (A1's meter shows "9.21"; the question text keeps the student's "9.2"). Concentrations are written 0.010 mol/L (2 s.f.). The HCl to acetic acid difference is 3.40 − 2.03 = 1.37, and 10^1.37 = 23.4, said as "about twenty three times" (2 s.f., from a difference with two decimal places). A2 (Revision 2): range 2.32 − 2.30 = 0.02; mean 2.31; 2.31 − 2.03 = 0.28, said as "about zero point three". (i) A2 plausibility: a one-point calibration at pH 7.00 fixes the offset and assumes the ideal 59.16 mV per unit slope. If the aged probe's real slope is s times ideal, a sample whose correctly calibrated reading is 2.03 is displayed as 7.00 − s × (7.00 − 2.03). For 2.31, s = 4.69 ÷ 4.97 = 0.944; for 2.30 and 2.32, 0.946 and 0.942. So the readings fit a probe at about 94 percent of the ideal slope, a common reason to recondition or replace it. A pH 4.00 and 7.00 calibration measures the real slope, and readings near 2 then sit on the line extended about two units beyond pH 4, with a small extrapolation error. The narration says "the meter had to guess its slope"; slope percentages are not learner copy. (f) Blotting: blot, do not rub, because rubbing the glass bulb can build a static charge and slow the response (manufacturer guidance). Store the probe in its storage solution; never let it dry out (screen tag only). (g) Calibration order: pH 7.00 first (the offset), then 4.00 and 10.00 (the slope), following common meter instructions; schools follow their meter's manual (screen tag). (h) Pronunciation: "pH" as letters; "H three O plus" is not spoken ("hydronium"); "zero point zero one zero" digit by digit; "carbonic acid"; "acetic acid" (C05 usage; screen tag "ethanoic acid" once).

### 3. how the meter works
> How does the meter know? The probe has a thin glass **bulb** at its tip. Hydronium ions in the solution produce a tiny **voltage** across that glass, and the meter turns the voltage into a pH number. But the link between voltage and pH **drifts** as the probe ages, and it changes with temperature. So the meter has to be shown what the right answers look like.

### 4. calibration with buffers
> That's **calibration**. You dip the probe into **buffer solutions**, which hold a known, steady pH. Start with the pH seven buffer, then pH four, and add pH ten because you'll measure bases too. The meter draws a **straight line** through those known points. One buffer gives only one point, so the meter would have to guess the slope. Buffers on **both sides** of seven check the line for acids and for bases. Calibrate at the same temperature as your samples, on the day you measure.

### 5. the measuring routine
> Then, for every sample: **rinse** the probe with distilled water, and gently **blot** it. Any solution left on the glass would be carried into the next beaker, and change its reading. Lower the bulb fully in, swirl gently, and **wait** until the reading stops changing, because the glass needs time to respond. Take **three readings**, each in a fresh portion, so one contaminated portion can't affect all three.

## Teaching checks

## Open decisions

- **No pH calculation: kept (Revision 2; neither review asked for an expected column).** P03 sits before C07, so readings are not calculated from concentrations; ideal values stay in author note (b). If the reviewer wants an "expected" column, it would have to be labelled "calculated in a later video", which adds reading load without teaching; recommended no.
- **Calibration range (scene 4): closed in Revision 2.** Both reviews found that HCl (2.03) and NaOH (11.96) sit outside the pH 4 to 10 band, so "your readings fall within the range you calibrated" was false. Kept the three school buffers (option a, extended to NaOH) and replaced the rule with the calibration line: the meter draws a straight line through the buffer points, buffers on both sides of 7 check it for acids and bases, readings a little beyond 4 and 10 use the line extended (grey tag), and accuracy is "buffers either side of 7, as close to the readings as you can". Option (b), a pH 2.00 buffer, is not adopted (less common in schools).
- **Strength overlap with P04: closed.** The learning review confirmed that the one-clause strength mention and the 23-times comparison do not pre-empt P04 (P04 uses 0.100 mol/L and the dilute-HCl challenge).
