**Boundary:** this video starts by comparing the starting materials of three routes to ethanol (ethene from crude oil, a haloalkane, and glucose from plants), and stops at judging each route's conditions and how the ethanol is separated. It teaches: hydration recalled from chem-m7-c04a (ethene and steam, phosphoric acid catalyst, about 300 °C, about 60 to 70 atmospheres); the reaction is reversible, so it is written with an equilibrium arrow (chem-m5-c02 callback); at 300 °C all three substances are gases, two gas molecules become one, so high pressure favours ethanol (chem-m5-c04 callback); the catalyst speeds both directions and does not move the equilibrium (chem-m5-c04 callback); the forward reaction releases heat, so 300 °C is a compromise between yield and rate (one sentence; Module 5 temperature callback, see Open decisions); only a few percent of the ethene reacts per pass, so the mixture is cooled, ethanol and water condense, and unreacted ethene is recycled; the ethanol is then distilled from the water; substitution: a haloalkane heated with aqueous sodium hydroxide gives an alcohol and a sodium halide (bromoethane to ethanol and NaBr), because the halogen leaves the carbon slightly positive and the negative hydroxide ion is attracted to it; the OH takes the halogen's place on the same carbon (2-bromopropane gives propan-2-ol), so the route can make a chosen alcohol; heating under reflux speeds the reaction without losing the volatile haloalkane and alcohol, because the upright condenser returns their vapours; the alcohol is then distilled from the mixture; fermentation: glucose to ethanol and carbon dioxide, balanced, with yeast on the arrow because its enzymes are the catalyst; the school setup as a short apparatus sequence (glucose solution and yeast in a flask, a warm water bath at about 35 °C, a bung and delivery tube that keep air out and let carbon dioxide escape, limewater turning milky); why each condition (enzymes work fastest when warm and are damaged when too hot; with plenty of oxygen yeast mostly respires aerobically, so little ethanol forms; gas must escape so pressure cannot build); fermentation usually stops at about 15% ethanol, so the yeast is filtered off and the mixture distilled, the vapour richer in ethanol because ethanol boils at 78 °C and water at 100 °C (fractional distillation in industry, one clause); judging the routes by feedstock (renewable or not), conditions and energy, rate (continuous or batch) and separation, with no single best route. **Not here:** the four alkene additions and their conditions in full (chem-m7-c04a; (c) The catalyst lowers the activation energy of both directions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner and the position is unchanged (Module 5). (d) Temperature compromise: the forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature shifts the equilibrium right but slows the rate; 300 °C gives an acceptable rate with an acceptable yield, and recycling makes up for the low conversion. ### 3. why the pressure, the catalyst and the temperature
> At three hundred degrees, all three substances are gases. Two gas molecules make **one**, so high pressure shifts the equilibrium towards ethanol. The catalyst speeds up both directions equally, so equilibrium comes sooner, but it doesn't move the equilibrium. And the temperature is a compromise. The forward reaction releases heat, so a lower temperature would give more ethanol at equilibrium, but far too **slowly**.

### 5. route 2: swap the halogen for an OH
> Route two starts with a haloalkane. Heat bromoethane with aqueous sodium hydroxide, meaning it's dissolved in water, and the bromine is **swapped** for an O H, giving ethanol and sodium bromide. Here's why. Bromine pulls electrons from the carbon it's on, so that carbon is slightly positive, and the negative **hydroxide** ion is attracted to it. The hydroxide bonds to that carbon, and the bromine leaves as a bromide ion. It's a substitution, like the swap in the alkane video, but this time the halogen is the atom pushed **out**.

### 6. why reflux, and where the OH lands
> Why heat it under **reflux**? Heating speeds the reaction up, but bromoethane boils at thirty eight degrees and ethanol at seventy eight, so they'd escape as vapour. The upright condenser cools those vapours, and they drip **back** into the flask. Afterwards, the alcohol is distilled out of the mixture. And the O H lands exactly where the halogen was: two-bromopropane gives **propan-two-ol**. So this route can make a particular alcohol, not just ethanol.

### 8. the school setup, and why each condition

### 9. it stops at about fifteen percent, so distil

Ethanol boils at seventy eight degrees and water at one hundred, so the vapour that comes off is richer in **ethanol**. Industry uses fractional distillation to go further.

### 10. judging the three routes

Substitution suits making a particular alcohol in the lab, but the haloalkane has to be made **first**. So there's no single winner. It depends on the feedstock, the energy, and the purity you need.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Hydration (industrial): CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂OH (H₃PO₄ catalyst, about 300 °C, 60 to 70 atm). Reversible, so ⇌. High pressure: 2 gas molecules → 1 favours ethanol. 4. Judging: hydration is fast and continuous but needs high temperature, pressure and a non-renewable feedstock; fermentation uses a renewable crop and mild conditions but is slow, and its dilute product takes energy to distil; substitution makes a chosen alcohol but needs a haloalkane first.
