### 2. who you think you are
> Start with **personal identity**: your sense of who you are, built from the roles and groups you treat as defining. People tend to act like the person they believe they are. I want to get fitter is an intention, and it often loses to a cold morning. I'm someone who runs is an identity, and it usually doesn't. Identity works on sport in three ways. It decides whether you start, because people join where they can picture themselves belonging, so who already plays sends a signal. It decides whether you keep going. A bad session tells a committed player this happens to people like me, and tells a newcomer she was right to doubt. Same session, opposite conclusions. And it shapes performance. If your worth rides on the result, an ordinary game becomes a test of you, and that pressure can drag attention onto the movement.

### 4. inside the activity, or outside it
> Next, **motivation**: what you choose to do, and how hard you go at it. **Intrinsic motivation** comes from the activity itself: enjoyment, interest, getting better. **Extrinsic motivation** comes from something separate: a medal, selection, money or praise. Both work. The difference is how long they last. Ask one question: what happens when the person stops winning? Extrinsic motivation lasts as long as the reward. Intrinsic lasts as long as the activity. Now the park. Paying for something the kids already enjoyed changed why they were there. Play became a job. When the money stopped, the new reason had gone, and the old one had been pushed out. That's the **overjustification effect**. Not every reward does this. A reward that shows real improvement can build motivation. A reward that controls, do this to get that, crowds it out.

### 6. three needs that grow the durable kind
> So how does a coach grow the durable kind? Intrinsic motivation grows where three needs are met, and a coach controls each one. **Competence**: feeling capable and improving. Give challenges they can reach, and compare them with their own past, not with a ranking. **Autonomy**: feeling you chose this. Offer real choices, and give reasons instead of orders. **Relatedness**: feeling you belong and matter here. Learn names, give everyone a role, and build a group that would notice someone missing. Earlier, you saw a session aimed at the fittest player shut everyone else out. That's competence denied. And relatedness is often the cheapest need to meet.

### 9d. feedback, item two
> A public ranking compares players with each other, not with their own past. Those near the bottom feel less competent every week, even when they're improving, and being last in public can weaken belonging too. So they're the likeliest to drop out.
