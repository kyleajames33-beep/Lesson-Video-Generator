### 1. hook: sea view or spa
> Here's the hotel. It has forty rooms, and yours is given out at random, so every room is equally likely. Sixteen rooms have a sea view, ten have a spa, and six have both. What's the chance you get a sea view or a spa? We could count. Ten plus six plus four is twenty rooms, so twenty out of forty, a **half**. But often we're given probabilities, not counts. So how do the complement and addition rules work on a Venn **diagram**?

### 2. counts become probabilities
> Since the rooms are equally likely, each region's probability is its count over forty, the size of the sample space. So divide every number on the diagram by forty. Sea view only, ten over forty, is zero point two five. Both is six over forty, zero point one five. Spa only is zero point one. And neither is twenty over forty, zero point **five**. It's the same diagram, with probabilities in the same places. And the regions add to one, because every room is in exactly one **region**. That's the whole idea of this video. Each probability rule is a counting rule, divided by the size of the sample space.

### 3. the complement rule
> Now pick one event, a sea view. Every room either has a sea view or it doesn't, so the sea view rooms and the rest fill the whole hotel. As counts, sixteen plus twenty-four is forty. Divide by forty, and P of A plus P of A complement equals **one**. So P of A complement equals one minus P of A. The chance of no sea view is one minus zero point four, which is zero point **six**. This rule is handy whenever "not" is easier to count than the event itself. And A complement means "A doesn't happen". It isn't the other event, B. It's everything outside **A**.

### 4. the addition rule
> Next, A or B, a sea view or a spa. Adding P of A and P of B gives zero point four plus zero point two five, which is zero point six five. But we counted it at twenty rooms, a half. The six rooms with both were counted **twice**, just like the members at the library. So take one copy of the overlap away. P of A union B equals P of A plus P of B, minus P of A intersect **B**. It's last video but one's counting rule, divided by forty. Here it's zero point four plus zero point two five, minus zero point one five, which is zero point **five**, a half again.

### 5. worked example: given probabilities
> These two rules hold for any probabilities, not just ones we've counted, so we can use them on decimals we're given. Say the probability of A is zero point six, of B is zero point five, and of A intersect B is zero point three. Find P of A union B. It's zero point six plus zero point five, minus zero point three, which is zero point **eight**. Now, what's the chance of B but not A? That's the B only region. Take all of B, and remove the part shared with A. Zero point five minus zero point three is zero point **two**. And neither is one minus zero point eight, which is also zero point **two**.

### 6. mutually exclusive events
> Some events can't both happen. Your room can't be on the ground floor and the top floor at once. Events like that are called mutually exclusive. As sets of outcomes, they're **disjoint**, so on a Venn diagram the circles don't overlap. Then P of A intersect B is zero, and the rule loses its last term. P of A union B is just P of A plus P of **B**. Each floor has eight rooms, so that's zero point two plus zero point two, zero point four. They aren't complements, though, because the middle floors are neither. And events aren't mutually exclusive just because they're different. A sea view and a spa are different, but six rooms have **both**.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 8; pack question 1, original, HSC style, 2 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "For events A and B, P(A) = 0.45, P(B) = 0.35 and P(A ∪ B) = 0.6. Find P(A ∩ B) and the probability that neither A nor B occurs."
Model answer: 0.6 = 0.45 + 0.35 − P(A ∩ B), so P(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Neither: 1 − P(A ∪ B) = 0.4. Classic mistake (crossed): "neither = 1 − 0.45 − 0.35 = 0.2".

**Attempt 2 (scene 10; pack question 2, original, HSC style, 2 marks suggested).** "A bag holds 30 balls numbered 1 to 30, and one is drawn at random. Find the probability that its number is a multiple of 5 or a multiple of 6. Then find the probability that it is not a multiple of 5."
Model answer: multiples of 5: 6; of 6: 5; of both (30): 1. P = (6 + 5 − 1)/30 = 10/30 = 1/3. Not a multiple of 5: 1 − 6/30 = 4/5. Classic mistake (crossed): "11/30".
