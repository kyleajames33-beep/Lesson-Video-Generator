### 2. times repeated, times the chance
> A probability of zero point eight means about eight in every ten, in the long run. Thirty rides is three lots of ten, so about three lots of eight, which is **twenty-four**. That's called the expected frequency. It's the number of times the experiment is repeated, called n, times the probability it happens each time, called p. So the expected frequency is n times p, thirty times zero point eight, which is twenty-**four**. It only needs two numbers: how many times, and the chance each time.

### 3. an average, not a promise
> But you won't get exactly twenty-four every month. Watch three months of thirty rides. The first month, twenty-two arrive within five minutes. The next, twenty-five. Then twenty-**four**. Each month is its own random run, so the count moves around. Some months beat the expected number, some fall short, and that's normal. Run a whole year, and the average settles near twenty-four. So the expected frequency is a long-run average, not a **promise**.

### 4. p from theory, and decimals
> The probability can come from data, like the app's record, or from theory. A fair die lands on six with probability one sixth. Roll it a hundred and twenty times, and you'd expect a hundred and twenty times one sixth, twenty sixes. Roll it a hundred times, and it's a hundred over six, sixteen point six **seven**. You can't roll point six seven of a six, but that's fine, because it's an average. It means about seventeen, and a real run might give twelve, or **twenty-two**.

### 5. planning with np: phone cases
> Here's an exam-style question. A factory tested five hundred phone cases and found fifteen faulty. Estimate the probability that a case is faulty. How many faulty cases should it expect in the next two thousand? And the manager wants fewer than forty. Is that reasonable? First, p is the relative frequency, fifteen over five hundred, zero point zero **three**. Now n is the number of new cases, two thousand, so the expected number faulty is two thousand times zero point zero three, which is **sixty**. Sixty is well above forty, so at this rate the target isn't reasonable. Forty out of two thousand is zero point zero **two**, so the factory would need a faulty rate below that. All of this assumes the five hundred tested are typical, and the factory keeps working the same way. Using five hundred as n, and getting fifteen, is the slip. n is the two thousand still to be **made**.

### 6. planning with a band
> Here's how a canteen plans with this. On a normal day, about fifteen per cent of its six hundred students buy a pie. Fifteen per cent is zero point one five, so it expects six hundred times zero point one five, ninety **pies**. But some days it'll sell more, and some days fewer. If two per cent of light bulbs are faulty, a batch of five hundred should have about five hundred times zero point zero two, ten faulty bulbs.
