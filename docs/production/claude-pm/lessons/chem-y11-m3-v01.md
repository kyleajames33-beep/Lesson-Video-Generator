### 2. was it there before?
> Only the marble chips. The difference is whether the carbon dioxide was already there. In the sparkling water, carbon dioxide was dissolved under pressure when the bottle was filled. Opening it lets the gas escape, so the same substance just changes where it is. That's a physical change. In the flask, there was no carbon dioxide until the chips met the acid. It was made, so that's a chemical change, a change that forms at least one new substance, with different **properties**.

Visual: "chips" ticks. On "already there" chip "was it there before?" (amber) at the centre. On "dissolved under pressure" a small coded "sealed" thumbnail of `c1-sparkling-bottle-sealed-v1.png` beside the open bottle, tag "CO₂ already dissolved". On "properties" a teal label under the chips: "chemical change: a new substance forms, with different properties".

### 3. the model: atoms find new partners
> Here's a model that shows the difference. In the sparkling water, the same carbon dioxide molecules sit among the water molecules, then drift out as bubbles. Nothing inside any molecule changes. Now the chips. Calcium carbonate meets hydrochloric acid, and the atoms swap partners. The carbon and two of the oxygens join up as carbon dioxide. The third oxygen joins two hydrogens as water. And the calcium ends up with the two chlorines, as calcium chloride, which stays dissolved. Same atoms, new **arrangements**.

Visual: a magnified circle over each plate. Right circle: CO₂ molecules (grey C between two red O) among bent water molecules; on "drift out as bubbles" three CO₂ molecules leave upward intact (tag "same molecules"). Left circle: atom tiles for "CaCO₃ + 2HCl" (Ca green with a C grey and three O red as one group, tag "ionic: a formula unit"; two H–Cl pairs). On "swap partners" the tiles loosen. On "carbon dioxide" C and two O slide together into O–C–O and rise as a bubble; on "water" the third O and two H form a bent H₂O; on "calcium chloride" Ca and two Cl group loosely and drift apart in the liquid (tag "dissolved"). On "arrangements" the equation writes under the circle: "CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)".

### 4. every atom kept
> Now count them. Before the reaction, one calcium, one carbon, three oxygens, two hydrogens and two chlorines. After it, exactly the same. No atom is made or destroyed, they're only rearranged, which is why mass is conserved, as you saw in Module 2. But the new arrangements are new substances, with new properties. That's what a chemical change means at the level of **atoms**.

### 5. indicators, part one
> Of course, you can't see atoms in a test tube. So chemists look for indicators, observations that suggest a new substance has formed. A gas forming, like these bubbles. A colour change, like green copper carbonate turning black when it's heated, because the new substance absorbs different light. And a precipitate, a solid that appears when two clear solutions mix, like this yellow lead iodide, because the new substance doesn't **dissolve**.

### 7. every clue has a lookalike
> But here's the catch. Every one of these clues has a lookalike that isn't a chemical change. Sparkling water fizzes. Food colouring changes the colour of water. Salt crystals appear when salt water evaporates. A cold pack gets cold because a solid dissolves. And sugar disappears into water. None of these makes a new substance. So an indicator is a clue, not a proof. Ask what's **new**.

### 8. making the case
> So how do you make a strong case? First, look for more than one indicator. Second, identify the new substance. Bubble the gas through limewater, a solution that turns milky with carbon dioxide, and it turns milky. But careful, the gas from sparkling water does that too. The test tells you what the gas is. What shows a chemical change is that the carbon dioxide wasn't there before, and the marble was used **up**.

### 9. the exam sentence
> In an exam, name the indicator and the new substance that caused it. Don't just write, a precipitate formed. Write, a yellow precipitate formed, showing that a new substance, lead iodide, was produced. That one sentence connects what you saw to what **changed**.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Chemical change: at least one new substance forms, with different properties. Physical change: the same substance changes form, state or place (melting, dissolving, gas escaping).
2. Indicators: gas forms, colour change, precipitate forms, temperature change, a reactant used up. Each is a clue, not proof: each has a physical lookalike.
3. Strong evidence: several indicators together, plus a new product identified that was not there before (e.g. limewater turns milky: CO₂).
4. Model: in a reaction, atoms are rearranged into new substances and none is made or destroyed, so each element's count is the same on both sides.
