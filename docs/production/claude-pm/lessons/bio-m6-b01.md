### 2. gene, allele, gene pool
> To answer it, we need three words, at three different levels. A **gene** is a stretch of DNA that codes for a product, like a protein. An **allele** is one version of that gene. Different alleles of a gene have slightly different base sequences. And a **gene pool** is every allele of every gene in a whole population. So a gene pool belongs to a population, never to one organism.

Visual: the level board. Level 1: `SlimStrand` draws across the top third; on "a stretch of DNA" a soft band shades one region with the callout "gene: codes for a product". Level 2: on "one version of that gene" the band duplicates downward into two short strand pieces, each with one highlighted position; small callouts show "allele 1: ...A..." and "allele 2: ...T..." (only the one differing base is shown, the rest of each strand is plain lines), tag "same gene, different sequence". Level 3: on "every allele of every gene in a whole population" the camera pulls back; the strand shrinks into one bacterium plate, which multiplies into a population of about twenty plates, each with a small allele ring; a coded `PoolBasin` draws under them and the rings drop into it (tag "gene pool: all the alleles in a population"). On "never to one organism" a chip "gene pool = one organism's genes" writes on beside a single plate and takes a grey scribble cross. Hold one second.

### 3. a changed base makes a new allele
> A **mutation** is a change in the base sequence of DNA. If the change lands inside a gene, it makes a version of that gene with a different sequence, and that's a new allele. You've met one already. In one haemoglobin gene, one base changed, and that made the sickle cell allele. When a change like that first appears, the population holds a version of the gene it didn't have before.

Visual: the strand returns large. On "a change in the base sequence" one position flashes and its callout changes "A" to "T" (tag "mutation: base sequence changes"; grey tag "B8"). On "didn't have before" the gene pool basin returns small: its row of open rings gains one amber notched ring, and a counter beside it reads "alleles of this gene: 2, now 3". Hold one second.

### 4. one copy, often no visible effect
> But a new allele starts very small. A mutation happens in one cell, in one individual, usually from a copying mistake that isn't repaired, or from damage by a **mutagen**, something that harms DNA, which is the next video. So at first, there's just one copy in the whole population. And many mutations change nothing you can see, because they fall outside genes, or don't change the protein.

### 5. passing it on
> How does that one copy spread? It depends on how the organism reproduces. A bacterium copies its DNA and splits in two, so both daughter cells get the new allele, and so does every cell descended from them. In animals, a mutation is passed on only if it's in a cell that makes eggs or sperm. A mutation in a skin cell can affect that animal, but its offspring don't inherit it.

### 6. random with respect to need
> Now back to Fleming's question. Mutations happen by chance, whether or not the change would help. A bacterium can't sense penicillin and make the exact mutation it needs. But in a huge population, rare mutations keep turning up, so a few cells may already carry a resistance allele before any penicillin is used. Biologists say mutation is **random with respect to need**.

### 8. what penicillin actually does
> So what does penicillin actually do? It kills the bacteria that don't carry a resistance allele. The few that do carry one survive and keep dividing, and each daughter cell inherits the allele. Within days, most of the population can be resistant. And too little penicillin also spares cells that are only slightly resistant. The antibiotic didn't create the allele. It changed how common it is, and that's **natural selection**.

Visual: the population of sixty plates from scene 6 (two with "resistance" rings). A `DayStrip` runs along the top: "day 0", "day 1", "day 2", "day 3". ### 9. making, mixing and moving alleles
> Mutation isn't the only way a population's alleles change, so let's sort the processes. Meiosis and fertilisation make new combinations, but every allele in them already existed. **Gene flow**, when organisms or their pollen move between populations, can bring in an allele this population didn't have. But somewhere, at some time, that allele was made by a mutation. So mutation is the original source of every allele.

Visual: the source board. The gene pool basin sits centre, holding open rings and one amber ring. On "Meiosis and fertilisation" a chip "meiosis and fertilisation: mix" writes on (grey tag "B8"); two rings inside the basin swap partners and pair into new pairs, and no new ring appears (tag "new combinations, no new alleles"). On "made by a mutation" a faint dashed line runs from that ring back to a small strand icon in the other population with a base-change callout (tag "made by mutation there first"). On "original source of every allele" the chip "mutation: makes" sits at the top of the board. Hold one second.

### 13. decision rule and handoff
> So when a question asks how a population gets a new allele, trace it in order. A mutation changes the base sequence in one individual. ### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Gene: a section of DNA that codes for a product. Allele: one version of a gene. Gene pool: all the alleles in a population.
- Mutation: a change in DNA base sequence. Inside a gene it makes a new allele (e.g. sickle cell). It starts as one copy in one individual; many have no visible effect.
- Passed on: bacteria give it to both daughter cells; animals pass it on only from cells that make eggs or sperm.
- Mutation is random with respect to need (replica plating, 1952). An antibiotic selects existing resistance alleles, so their frequency rises.
- Mutation makes alleles (the original source). Meiosis and fertilisation mix them. Gene flow moves them, so it can add an allele new to a population.

Memory: Mutation makes, meiosis and fertilisation mix, gene flow moves, selection and chance change how common.

Self-check (18 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Griffiths et al., Introduction to Genetic Analysis) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Fleming 1945.** Nobel lecture, 11 December 1945: "the time may come when penicillin can be bought by anyone in the shops. "Scientist who discovered penicillin": Fleming discovered its antibacterial action in 1928. Correct as spoken.
- **Hospital staph.** Barber 1947 (BMJ; Hammersmith Hospital, April to November 1946): about 12.5% of *S. aureus* isolates penicillin-resistant, shown as "about 1 in 8"; Barber and Rozwadowska-Dowzenko, Lancet 1948, 2:641: about 59% by 1948 (confirmed by the science check via Shaffer 2013). The rise came mainly from a penicillinase-producing strain spreading by cross-infection. Screen tag only; the narration says "within a few years ... common in some hospitals" after "resistance did spread", with no claim about how.
- **Gene, allele, gene pool definitions.** Site key terms and standard texts. Correct.
- **Sickle cell allele.** One base substitution in the beta-globin gene (GAG to GTG on the coding strand, glutamic acid to valine), taught in B7. Correct. "When a change like that first appears": generic; the design avoids claiming a single historical origin.
- **Most mutations no visible effect.** Most of the human genome is non-coding and many coding changes are synonymous; the site's misconception box says the same. "Many" (not "most") is spoken. Correct. (b05 covers non-coding regulatory exceptions.)
- **Bacterial inheritance.** Binary fission: the chromosome is replicated and each daughter receives a copy; a mutation present before replication passes to both daughters. Correct. (A mutation arising during replication is first in one daughter strand and is fixed in one lineage after the next division; author note, not spoken.)
- **Animal germ line.** Only mutations in germ-line cells reach gametes. Correct for animals. Plants: germ cells arise late from meristem tissue, so somatic mutations in a shoot can reach its flowers; hence "In animals" and the grey tag "plants differ". Correct.
- **Random with respect to need.** Standard (Luria and Delbrück 1943; Lederberg and Lederberg 1952). Some antibiotics raise the general mutation rate through stress responses (for example the SOS response), but they do not direct which gene changes; author note, not spoken. "Can't sense penicillin and make the exact mutation it needs": correct.
- **Replica plating.** Lederberg J and Lederberg EM (1952) "Replica plating and indirect selection of bacterial mutants", J Bacteriol 63:399 to 406. A velveteen-covered block transfers colony positions from a master plate to selective plates; colonies growing on streptomycin plates mark positions on the master; cells from those positions, never exposed, were enriched for resistance, and after successive rounds a pure streptomycin-resistant culture was isolated without exposure. Revision 2 narration: "bacteria from those spots could grow on streptomycin too" (science check finding 4), which does not imply every cell in the spot was resistant. Organism *E. coli* K-12 (not named).
- **Penicillin as selection.** Penicillin kills growing susceptible bacteria; resistant cells survive and divide. "Within days, most of the population can be resistant": *S. aureus* divides about every 30 minutes in good conditions, slower in a host, so days give many generations. "Too little penicillin also spares cells that are only slightly resistant": low, sub-lethal antibiotic concentrations select low-level resistant mutants (Gullberg et al. 2011, PLoS Pathog 7:e1002158). Correct. Scenes 6 to 8 keep the single-allele model for the penicillin course as a teaching model; the assessed scenario uses rifampicin (below).
- **Resistance mechanism caveat.** Penicillinase (blaZ) genes in *S. aureus* are usually plasmid-borne and spread by horizontal transfer as well as by division (site correction 4). Covered by the grey tag on scene 8's end frame (moved from scene 9 in Revision 2).
- **Gene flow.** Movement of alleles between populations by migration of individuals or gametes (pollen); can add alleles absent from the recipient population. Correct (site correction 2).
- **Attempt 1 (rifampicin).** Rifampicin resistance in *S. aureus* arises by single point mutations in the chromosomal rpoB gene, at frequencies of about 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁸, and resistant mutants take over quickly when rifampicin is used alone (which is why it is given in combination). ## Memory aid check

- **"Who makes new alleles, and who only reshuffles them"** (bank insight, Module 6 Causes of genetic variation). Science: mutation is the original source of all alleles; meiosis (crossing over, independent assortment) and fertilisation make new combinations; gene flow moves alleles between populations; drift and selection change frequencies. The bank's first line, "the only source of genuinely new alleles", is narrowed to "makes new alleles" because gene flow can introduce an allele new to a local population (site correction 2). Limit: crossing over within a gene can very rarely create a new allele sequence (intragenic recombination); not spoken. Used in scene 11 after every process is explained (scenes 3 to 10), and in the notes.
- **"Individuals don't evolve. Populations do."** (bank trap.) True: an individual's alleles do not change in response to the environment; frequencies change across generations. Not spoken as a slogan; its content is scene 8's grey tag and its last sentence.
