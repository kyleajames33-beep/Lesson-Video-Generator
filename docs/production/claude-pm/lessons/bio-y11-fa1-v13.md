### 2. the membrane is the bag
> Here's the trick. The cell doesn't use a doorway at all. It uses the membrane itself. Remember, the membrane is fluid, and its water-avoiding tails always hide from water, so a torn edge seals itself up. That means a patch of membrane can fold, pinch off into a closed bubble, and later join with another membrane, a bit like soap bubbles splitting and merging. That small membrane bubble is a **vesicle**. Moving cargo in vesicles is called bulk transport, because it carries large amounts, or large particles, all in one **package**.

### 3. endocytosis, step by step
> First, bringing cargo in. That's called endocytosis, and endo means inside. Step one: the cargo sits against the outside of the membrane. Step two: the membrane folds inward around it, making a deep pocket. Step three: the edges of the pocket meet, and because the membrane seals itself, they join up. Step four: the pocket **pinches off** as a vesicle inside the cell. Watch this pale patch of membrane. It used to be part of the cell's surface. Now it's the wall of the **vesicle**.

### 4. phagocytosis: a white blood cell eats a bacterium
> Here's the real thing. This is a phagocyte, a white blood cell that hunts bacteria. It pushes out arms of cytoplasm, wrapped in membrane, that reach around the bacterium. The arms meet on the far side and their membranes **fuse**, so the bacterium ends up sealed inside a vesicle. Taking in a solid particle like this is called phagocytosis, which means cell eating. When a cell takes in droplets of fluid instead, with whatever is dissolved in them, it's called pinocytosis, or cell **drinking**. Both are kinds of endocytosis.

### 5. inside, but still wrapped (a quick prediction)
> Quick prediction: the bacterium is inside the cell now. Is it loose in the cytoplasm? **No**. It's still wrapped in the vesicle's membrane. Next, a lysosome, a sac of digestive enzymes, moves up to the vesicle, and their two membranes fuse into one. The enzymes pour in and break the bacterium down, while the membrane keeps them away from the rest of the **cell**. Two membranes joining into one is called fusion, and it's the key move in the next process **too**.

### 8. what the ATP pays for

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Sizes (scene 1, 10b).** A sodium-potassium pump or similar carrier protein is about 10 nm across; a typical rod bacterium (such as *E. coli*) is about 0.5 to 1 µm wide and about 2 µm long, so "about a hundred times longer than that whole pump protein" holds (length 2,000 nm ÷ 10 nm = 200; width about 50 to 100 times; C8, 23 Nov 2026). Paramecium about 200 µm long; *Amoeba proteus* about 250 to 750 µm, so 0.4 of an amoeba's length is fair. Codex to confirm the carrier size.
- **Phagocyte plates (scene 4).** A macrophage (about 20 µm) engulfing a rod bacterium; magnification label "about 2,000 ×" depends on the screen size and must be recalculated at build (or drop the figure and keep "magnified").
- **Membrane turnover (scene 7, spoken).** Steinman, Brodie and Cohn (1976, *Journal of Cell Biology* 68: 665) measured cultured mouse macrophages forming pinocytic vesicle membrane of at least 3.1 per cent of their surface area per minute (100/3.1 = about 32 minutes for an equal area), with recycling to the surface. It is an area-equivalent turnover rate for some cultured macrophages, not proof that each surface molecule is replaced in that time. "Some cultured white blood cells ... in about half an hour, they've used an area of membrane equal to their whole surface" rests on this; the screen chip says "measured in lab cultures". Codex to confirm; if it can't be confirmed, fall back to "they take in a large share of their surface membrane every hour" (re-count about −20).
- **Membrane return (scene 7).** Internalised membrane is largely recycled back to the surface by vesicles that fuse with it (exocytosis); correct at Year 11 level.
- **ATP use (scene 8).** Bending and pinching off the membrane (coat proteins and the GTPase dynamin, which uses GTP, and actin in phagocytosis, which uses ATP), carrying vesicles (motor proteins kinesin, dynein and myosin use ATP) and readying fusion (NSF uses ATP to recycle the fusion proteins). "ATP powers the proteins that ..." is a fair simplification; the GTP detail is author-only. For the science check.
- **Lysosome fusion (scene 5).** The phagosome fuses with lysosomes, whose enzymes digest the bacterium. Correct.
- **Insulin (scene 6, A2).** Beta cells in the pancreatic islets release insulin, a protein hormone, by exocytosis of vesicles from the Golgi body. Correct.
- **A2 water.** Osmosis continues without ATP when a cell is placed in a dilute solution (the gradient comes from the solution, not from the cell's metabolism). Correct.
- **Time-sensitive facts spoken.** None.
- **Simulation values (scene 7, Revision 3).** The readouts are model values, labelled "model" on screen: run 1, "taken in" 0 to 100% and "surface left" 100% towards 0% over 30 model minutes with nothing put back (a what-if, not a measurement); run 2, "put back" in step with "taken in" and "surface left" near 100%. Only "about half an hour" is a measured figure (the Steinman item above). No new spoken number.

### 8. what the ATP pays for

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Sizes (scene 1, 10b).** A sodium-potassium pump or similar carrier protein is about 10 nm across; a typical rod bacterium (such as *E. coli*) is about 0.5 to 1 µm wide and about 2 µm long, so "about a hundred times longer than that whole pump protein" holds (length 2,000 nm ÷ 10 nm = 200; width about 50 to 100 times; C8, 23 Nov 2026). Paramecium about 200 µm long; *Amoeba proteus* about 250 to 750 µm, so 0.4 of an amoeba's length is fair. Codex to confirm the carrier size.
- **Phagocyte plates (scene 4).** A macrophage (about 20 µm) engulfing a rod bacterium; magnification label "about 2,000 ×" depends on the screen size and must be recalculated at build (or drop the figure and keep "magnified").
- **Membrane turnover (scene 7, spoken).** Steinman, Brodie and Cohn (1976, *Journal of Cell Biology* 68: 665) measured cultured mouse macrophages forming pinocytic vesicle membrane of at least 3.1 per cent of their surface area per minute (100/3.1 = about 32 minutes for an equal area), with recycling to the surface. It is an area-equivalent turnover rate for some cultured macrophages, not proof that each surface molecule is replaced in that time. "Some cultured white blood cells ... in about half an hour, they've used an area of membrane equal to their whole surface" rests on this; the screen chip says "measured in lab cultures". Codex to confirm; if it can't be confirmed, fall back to "they take in a large share of their surface membrane every hour" (re-count about −20).
- **Membrane return (scene 7).** Internalised membrane is largely recycled back to the surface by vesicles that fuse with it (exocytosis); correct at Year 11 level.
- **ATP use (scene 8).** Bending and pinching off the membrane (coat proteins and the GTPase dynamin, which uses GTP, and actin in phagocytosis, which uses ATP), carrying vesicles (motor proteins kinesin, dynein and myosin use ATP) and readying fusion (NSF uses ATP to recycle the fusion proteins). "ATP powers the proteins that ..." is a fair simplification; the GTP detail is author-only. For the science check.
- **Lysosome fusion (scene 5).** The phagosome fuses with lysosomes, whose enzymes digest the bacterium. Correct.
- **Insulin (scene 6, A2).** Beta cells in the pancreatic islets release insulin, a protein hormone, by exocytosis of vesicles from the Golgi body. Correct.
- **A2 water.** Osmosis continues without ATP when a cell is placed in a dilute solution (the gradient comes from the solution, not from the cell's metabolism). Correct.
- **Time-sensitive facts spoken.** None.
- **Simulation values (scene 7, Revision 3).** The readouts are model values, labelled "model" on screen: run 1, "taken in" 0 to 100% and "surface left" 100% towards 0% over 30 model minutes with nothing put back (a what-if, not a measurement); run 2, "put back" in step with "taken in" and "surface left" near 100%.
