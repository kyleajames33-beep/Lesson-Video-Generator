### 2. the membrane is the bag
> Here's the trick. The cell doesn't use a doorway at all. It uses the membrane itself. Remember, the membrane is fluid, and its water-avoiding tails always hide from water, so a torn edge seals itself up. That means a patch of membrane can fold, pinch off into a closed bubble, and later join with another membrane, a bit like soap bubbles splitting and merging. That small membrane bubble is a **vesicle**. Moving cargo in vesicles is called bulk transport, because it carries large amounts, or large particles, all in one **package**.

### 4. phagocytosis: a white blood cell eats a bacterium

When a cell takes in droplets of fluid instead, with whatever is dissolved in them, it's called pinocytosis, or cell **drinking**. Both are kinds of endocytosis.

### 7. why doesn't the surface run out? (a quick prediction)
> Here's a puzzle. Some cultured white blood cells take in so many vesicles that, in about half an hour, they've used an area of membrane equal to their whole surface. So membrane keeps cycling in and out, and the surface stays about the same **size**.

### 8. what the ATP pays for
> All of this costs energy. Bulk transport isn't climbing a concentration gradient, so what's the ATP for? It powers the proteins that bend the membrane and pinch it off, the proteins that carry vesicles along tracks, and the proteins that get vesicles ready to fuse. In active transport, ATP changes the shape of one carrier protein. In bulk transport, ATP moves whole patches of **membrane**. Cut off the ATP, and the vesicles stop where they **are**.

### 9. every route, in one table
> Let's put every transport process together. Simple diffusion: no energy, down the gradient, straight through the bilayer. Facilitated diffusion: no energy, down the gradient, through a channel or carrier protein. Active transport: ATP, against the gradient, through a carrier protein. Endocytosis and exocytosis: ATP, in or out, inside vesicles, whatever the gradient. So all three active processes share one thing: they use ATP from the cell. And to keep the two bulk names straight: endo enters, exo **exits**.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Bulk transport moves large particles, or large amounts, in vesicles (small membrane bubbles). The membrane is fluid and seals itself, so it can fold, pinch off and fuse.
2. Endocytosis (in): the membrane folds inward around the cargo, the edges meet and seal, and a vesicle pinches off inside. Phagocytosis takes in solids (a white blood cell engulfing a bacterium); pinocytosis takes in fluid.
3. Exocytosis (out): a vesicle, often from the Golgi body, moves to the membrane, fuses with it and releases its contents outside; the vesicle membrane becomes part of the cell membrane (for example, insulin from pancreas cells).
4. Both use ATP: to bend and pinch off the membrane, carry vesicles along protein tracks and fuse them.
5. Active processes (active transport, endocytosis, exocytosis) use ATP from the cell; passive processes (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis) do not.

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Sizes (scene 1, 10b).** A sodium-potassium pump or similar carrier protein is about 10 nm across; a typical rod bacterium (such as *E. coli*) is about 1 to 2 µm wide by 2 µm long, so "more than a hundred times wider than that whole pump protein" holds (2,000 nm ÷ 10 nm = 200; 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100). Paramecium about 200 µm long; *Amoeba proteus* about 250 to 750 µm, so 0.4 of an amoeba's length is fair. Codex to confirm the carrier size.
- **Phagocyte plates (scene 4).** A macrophage (about 20 µm) engulfing a rod bacterium; magnification label "about 2,000 ×" depends on the screen size and must be recalculated at build (or drop the figure and keep "magnified").
- **Membrane turnover (scene 7, spoken).** Steinman, Brodie and Cohn (1976, *Journal of Cell Biology* 68: 665) measured cultured mouse macrophages forming pinocytic vesicle membrane of at least 3.1 per cent of their surface area per minute (100/3.1 = about 32 minutes for an equal area), with recycling to the surface. It is an area-equivalent turnover rate for some cultured macrophages, not proof that each surface molecule is replaced in that time. "Some cultured white blood cells ... in about half an hour, they've used an area of membrane equal to their whole surface" rests on this; the screen chip says "measured in lab cultures". Codex to confirm; if it can't be confirmed, fall back to "they take in a large share of their surface membrane every hour" (re-count about −20).
- **Membrane return (scene 7).** Internalised membrane is largely recycled back to the surface by vesicles that fuse with it (exocytosis); correct at Year 11 level.
- **ATP use (scene 8).** Bending and pinching off the membrane (coat proteins and the GTPase dynamin, which uses GTP, and actin in phagocytosis, which uses ATP), carrying vesicles (motor proteins kinesin, dynein and myosin use ATP) and readying fusion (NSF uses ATP to recycle the fusion proteins). "ATP powers the proteins that ..." is a fair simplification; the GTP detail is author-only. For the science check.
- **Lysosome fusion (scene 5).** The phagosome fuses with lysosomes, whose enzymes digest the bacterium. Correct.
- **Insulin (scene 6, A2).** Beta cells in the pancreatic islets release insulin, a protein hormone, by exocytosis of vesicles from the Golgi body. Correct.
- **A2 water.** Osmosis continues without ATP when a cell is placed in a dilute solution (the gradient comes from the solution, not from the cell's metabolism). Correct.
- **Time-sensitive facts spoken.** None.
