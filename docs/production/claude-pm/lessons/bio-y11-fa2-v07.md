## Decisions

- **Main teaching move: turn the graph into a test.** v06's graphs are predictions. This video picks one condition (light intensity), writes the testable question and the justified prediction first, then builds the method that could catch the plateau: a measure of the rate, one changed variable, the hidden suspect controlled, a settling time, repeats, and a record of what was seen. It stops when the record is complete (route stop); v08 processes and interprets it.
- **Choosing the condition (scene 2; site L06 Activity 1).** Light, carbon dioxide or temperature could each be the independent variable; light is chosen because it is the easiest to change in steps (lamp distance) and v06's rise-and-plateau graph is about light. One sentence names the other two choices; v08's contrasted dataset uses carbon dioxide.
- **Testable question and prediction (scene 2; site L05 Activity 1 and the L06 Predict walk-through).** Question: "How does light intensity affect the rate of oxygen release by pondweed?" Prediction: "The rate will rise as light intensity increases, then level off, because once light is no longer the limiting factor another condition limits the rate." The 3 s prediction beat asks what the bubble rate will do as the lamp keeps coming closer.
- **Measuring oxygen output, with its limits (scene 3; route stop "safe oxygen-output measurements"; pack update "oxygen-measurement limits"; site flag for L06).** The bubbles are mostly oxygen, a product of photosynthesis, so bubbles per minute are a measure of the rate. Three limits are named in one breath each: bubbles vary in size (so the same count can mean different volumes: gas volume is the better measure); some oxygen dissolves in the water before it forms bubbles; and the plant uses some oxygen in its own respiration, so the bubbles show the oxygen left over. v08 develops net and gross production; this video only says what the bubbles do and don't show.
- **Variables (scene 4; site card 2).** Independent: light intensity, set by lamp distance at five distances (10, 15, 20, 30, 40 cm). Dependent: bubbles per minute. Controlled: the same sprig of pondweed, the same concentration of sodium hydrogen carbonate, the water temperature, room lights off (Claude's addition, standard), the counting time. Recalled from FA1 v21 in one phrase.
- **The hidden suspect (scene 5; the site's "single most commonly awarded validity mark").** The lamp warms the water; warmer water speeds the enzymes. Controls: a glass tank of water as a heat shield, or an LED lamp (runs cooler; Claude's addition), and a thermometer reading at every distance, written in the record.
- **Equilibrate, count, repeat (scene 6; site walk-through).** Five minutes after each move; one-minute counts, three times. Gas collection in an upside-down measuring cylinder over a funnel is shown as the better measure (site card 1).
- **The observation record (scene 7; route stop "a usable observation record").** Columns: distance, count 1, count 2, count 3, water temperature, notes. Raw counts are recorded as seen, including one odd count (47 at 15 cm, with the note "second stream of bubbles from a leaf; plant moved") that is checked and repeated (33) and kept in the record with its note, not erased (FA1 v21's anomaly practice). No means, no graph, no conclusion: those are v08's (route). The record is the handoff frame.
- **Prediction beat (scene 2).** "What will the bubble rate do as the lamp keeps coming closer?" with a 3 s countdown (root decision 7), answered from v06's model.
- **Memory aid.** The site's l06 hook, "**Change one thing. If two things move, the result explains nothing.**" (scene 5, after the lamp-heat suspect is explained, the decision rule and the notes; checked below). It names exactly this practical's trap (light and heat moving together).

### 1. hook (the bubbles stop speeding up)
> Put pondweed, a plant that lives under water, near a lamp, and tiny bubbles stream from its cut **stem**. Move the lamp closer, and the bubbles speed **up**. Closer again, and at some point they stop speeding up. Last video, a graph predicted exactly that. But a graph on a screen is a prediction, not evidence. So how do you design a test that could show it, **fairly**?

### 3. measuring oxygen, and what bubbles don't show

Some oxygen dissolves in the water first. And the plant uses some oxygen in its own respiration, so the bubbles show the oxygen left **over**.

### 5. the hidden suspect

Now two things are changing, so you can't tell which one caused the extra **bubbles**. So put a glass tank of water between the lamp and the tube to soak up the heat, or use an LED lamp, which runs **cooler**, and check the water with a thermometer at every distance. Change one thing. If two things move, the result explains **nothing**.

If your school has the equipment, there's a better measure than counting: collect the gas in an upside-down measuring cylinder, and read its volume after a set **time**.

## Safety facts check (self-check, 2 Nov; science check to confirm)

- **Hot lamp.** Filament and halogen lamps get hot enough to burn; LED lamps much less. Don't touch the bulb or shade; let it cool; move by the base. Learner copy as scene 8.
- **Electricity near water.** Shock risk. Keep the lamp, cord and power point away from the water, with the lamp on a dry bench; wipe spills at once; the teacher checks leads (tested and tagged equipment; teacher note).
- **Pondweed.** Biosecurity: aquatic weeds spread from discarded material. Dispose in the bin (or dry and bin), never in sinks, drains or waterways. Codex to confirm the current NSW status of Cabomba (Weed of National Significance; sale banned) and Elodea, and which species schools may use (site finding 1).
- **Sodium hydrogen carbonate.** Low hazard at school concentrations. No row.
- **Glass.** Test tube, beaker, funnel and measuring cylinder; breakage. Teacher note.
- **Teacher notes (not in learner copy).** Electrical safety tags on lamps; the heat-shield tank filled before the lamp is switched on; spills wiped before power is on.

## Memory aid check

- **"Change one thing. If two things move, the result explains nothing."** (Site hook, l06.) Assumptions: in a single-factor investigation only the independent variable may change; if another variable (here, temperature) changes with it, a change in the rate could be caused by either, so the result cannot be attributed to the independent variable. Holds for this practical, where light and heat move together unless the heat is blocked. Limit: "explains nothing" is strong; the result still shows that the combination changed the rate, just not which part caused it (the site's explanation says "you have measured a mixture and cannot say which part caused it"). Said in scene 5 after the hidden suspect is explained. Factorial designs are beyond Year 11. Supports new cases: in A1, the 9 °C rise is the second thing that moved.
