### 2. a formula counts atoms, not how they're joined
> A molecular formula only counts the atoms. It doesn't show how they're **joined**. In ethanol, the oxygen sits at the end of the chain with a hydrogen on it: an **O H** group. In methoxymethane, the oxygen sits **between** the two carbons. Compounds with the same molecular formula, but their atoms joined in a different order, are called **structural isomers**. They're different compounds, with different names and different properties. In the last video, every name pointed to one structure. Now one formula can point to several.

### 3. why the arrangement matters
> And the arrangement changes the properties. That O H lets ethanol molecules **hydrogen bond** to each other, which you met in Year 11: the hydrogen on one molecule's oxygen is attracted to the oxygen of the next. Methoxymethane has no hydrogen on its oxygen, so its molecules **can't** hydrogen bond to each other. They have the same atoms, so they're about the same **size**. The hydrogen bonds make the difference. Weaker attractions between molecules mean a lower boiling point. Isomers come in **three** kinds, and the kind depends on what changed.

### 4. chain isomers
> First, **chain** isomers. The carbon skeleton changes, but the family, here the alkanes, stays the same. Butane is four carbons in a row. Take a carbon off the end and hang it on the middle, and you get **two-methylpropane**. Both are C four H ten, both are alkanes. Camping gas often contains both, and they boil at **different** temperatures. The next video explains why branching does that.

### 5. position isomers
> Second, **position** isomers. The skeleton stays the same, and so does the family. Only the group moves to a different carbon. In but-one-ene, the double bond starts at carbon **one**. In but-two-ene it starts at carbon **two**. The same goes for an O H group. The ending **ol** means an O H, which is why ethanol ends in ol, and the number says which carbon carries it: propan-one-ol and propan-two-ol are position isomers too. Moving a group can change how it reacts, and you'll see later that these two alcohols give different products.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Structural isomers: same molecular formula, atoms joined in a different order. They are different compounds with different names and properties (ethanol 78 °C; methoxymethane about −25 °C; both C₂H₆O).
2. Chain isomers: same family, different carbon skeleton (butane and 2-methylpropane, C₄H₁₀). Position isomers: same skeleton and family, the group on a different carbon (but-1-ene and but-2-ene; propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol). Functional group isomers: different family, same formula (but-1-ene, an unsaturated alkene, and cyclobutane, a saturated cycloalkane, C₄H₈; ethanol and methoxymethane).
3. To classify: same formula? Different family → functional group. Same family → skeleton changed (chain) or group moved (position).

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why two compounds can share a formula: the formula counts atoms but not how they're joined (2). Why isomers have different properties: the arrangement changes the attractions between molecules (3: ethanol's O–H hydrogen bonds, methoxymethane's oxygen has no hydrogen; the two have the same atoms and about the same size, so the hydrogen bonds make the difference). Why a ring and an alkene of the same formula are functional group isomers: the ring is a cycloalkane, a different family with no C=C (6, feedback 1). Why each type gets its name: what changed (skeleton, position, group; 4 to 6). Why moving an O–H is not a new functional group: the group is the same, only its carbon changed (8). Why a different group matters: it decides the reactions, so the C=C makes but-1-ene react where cyclobutane cannot in the same way (7). Why the systematic method finds every isomer: each step removes one more carbon from the chain and every placement is tried, and a two-carbon chain only rebuilds a longer one (9).
