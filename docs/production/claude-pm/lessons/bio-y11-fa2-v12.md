### 6. water enters the root

### 7. the transpiration stream
> Put the three together. Osmotic pressure draws water into the root. Cohesion holds it as one continuous column. And transpiration pulls the whole column up, from the root to the leaf. That continuous flow is called the transpiration stream. The dissolved mineral ions ride along in it, so the same stream delivers minerals to the leaves. Stop the pull, and the minerals slow down too.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Transpiration: water evaporates from leaf cells into air spaces and escapes as vapour through stomata. Leaf cells take water from the xylem, putting the water column under tension (a pull). Energy: the sun.
2. Cohesion: hydrogen bonds between water molecules hold the xylem water as one continuous column, so the pull at the top moves the whole column. An air bubble breaks it (pits allow detours; lignin stops collapse).
3. Root cells pump in mineral ions (ATP), so water enters root hair cells by osmosis and crosses to the xylem. Osmotic pressure: how strongly a solution draws water in (the pressure needed to stop osmosis).
4. Transpiration stream: the continuous flow from root to leaf, carrying dissolved minerals. Water is mainly pulled from the top, not pushed (phloem sap is pushed).
5. Root pressure: a push that can build when ions and water collect in root xylem (guttation); usually only a few metres, often absent when transpiration is fast. Capillarity: only a short way in vessels. Neither explains tall trees.
