Main question: *How can evaporation at a leaf pull a continuous water column up xylem?*
Entry knowledge: see above. Students have watched FA2 v01 to v11.
Stops after: water in xylem is pulled, under tension, while phloem sap is pushed; a suction pump can only lift water about ten metres because it relies on air pushing, so a tree must genuinely pull; transpiration is the loss of water from leaves: water evaporates from leaf cells into air spaces and escapes through stomata, the cells take water from the nearest xylem, and the water column comes under tension; the energy comes from the sun; cohesion (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) holds the water as one unbroken column, so the pull at the top moves the whole column; an air bubble breaks the column and stops that vessel (pits let water detour; lignin keeps vessels open under tension); root hair cells give a large surface area; root cells pump ions in, so water enters by osmosis and crosses to the xylem; osmotic pressure is how strongly a solution draws water in (the pressure needed to stop osmosis); the transpiration stream is the continuous flow from root to leaf, carrying dissolved minerals; root pressure is a separate thing: a push that can build when ions and water accumulate in the root xylem (guttation); it varies with plant and conditions, usually lifts water only a few metres and is often absent when transpiration is fast; capillarity lifts water only a short way in tubes as wide as most vessels (it depends on tube width); the main force is the transpiration pull.
Handoff: if the pull depends on how fast the leaves lose water, what changes that rate? (route entry bio-y11-fa2-v13)

### 9. what about pushing from below?
