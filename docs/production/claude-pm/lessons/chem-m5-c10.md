### 1. hook (same concentration, different fizz)
> Here are two beakers of acid, both at **0.10 moles per litre**, with the same piece of magnesium in each. In hydrochloric acid it **fizzes hard**. In acetic acid, also called ethanoic acid, the acid in vinegar, it fizzes **slowly**. Same concentration, same metal, so why the difference? The answer is an equilibrium, and it comes with a K that has its own name.

### 2. why the difference (strong and weak)
> The bubbles are hydrogen gas, made when the magnesium meets **hydrogen ions**, H plus. In water, every hydrochloric acid molecule breaks into ions, H plus and chloride. Breaking into ions is called dissociating, or ionising, and because it goes all the way, hydrochloric acid is a **strong acid**. Acetic acid molecules break apart too, but the ions also **join back up**, and the two directions balance while most molecules are still whole. At this concentration only about **one in a hundred** is split at any moment. So the acetic acid has far fewer hydrogen ions to meet the magnesium. An acid that only partly ionises is a **weak acid**.

Visual: a particle lens opens above each beaker. On "hydrogen ions" an amber H+ in the first lens drifts to the ribbon edge and a small H2 bubble forms (one-beat loop, tag "H+ makes the bubbles"). On "every hydrochloric acid molecule" the HCl lens shows navy-amber pairs splitting until only amber H+ and violet Cl- remain, no whole molecules (tag "all split"). On "weak acid" the term writes under the second beaker: "only partly ionises". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 3. Ka is just K
> A balance between molecules and ions is an equilibrium, so it has a K, written the way you already know. Write any weak acid as H A, which splits into H plus and A minus. Products over reactants gives **H plus times A minus, over H A**. Every coefficient is one, so there are no powers. This K is called **Ka**, the **acid dissociation constant**, and the little a just says which kind of reaction it belongs to. It is **not a new kind of constant**: it follows every rule from the expressions video, and it changes only with temperature.

Visual: the equation strip writes "HA(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + A-(aq)" with the tag "any weak acid: HA". The extended C5 builder: each species is a tile; on "Products over reactants" the H+ and A- tiles fly to the numerator and HA to the denominator, giving "[H+][A-] / [HA]"; on "no powers" small "1"s on each tile fade. On "Ka" the K symbol writes with a large subscript "a" and the full name under it, "acid dissociation constant". On "not a new kind of constant" a C5 rules card slides in beside it ("products over reactants; coefficients as powers; pure solids, pure liquids and the solvent left out; changes only with temperature") with a teal tick on each line, and the label "Ka = K for an acid splitting into ions". The acetic acid version writes small beneath: "Ka = [H+][CH3COO-] / [CH3COOH]". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 4. written with water
> You will also see the same reaction written with water: H A plus water makes **H three O plus** and A minus. H three O plus is just a hydrogen ion carried by a water molecule. So does water go into Ka? **No**. Water is the solvent, there is a huge, steady amount of it, so it **stays out**, just as in the expressions video. Ka is H three O plus times A minus, over H A, the **same shape** as before.

Visual: a second equation strip writes under the first: "HA(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)". ### 5a. what a small Ka tells you
> Now read the size, the same way as any K. For acetic acid, Ka is **1.8 times ten to the minus five**. A tiny K means a small top and a big bottom, so at equilibrium most of the acid is still **whole molecules**.

### 5b. comparing two weak acids

Ka is not the concentration of H plus, and it is not the pH: those also depend on how much acid you dissolved.

### 6. strong acids
> What about hydrochloric acid? It ionises completely, so at equilibrium there are essentially **no H C L molecules left** to go on the bottom of the fraction. We don't use a Ka value for a strong acid. We simply say it **ionises completely**. Ka is the tool for **weak acids**, where molecules and ions are both present.

### 7. Kb for a weak base
> Bases work the same way. Ammonia, a gas that is dissolved in some household cleaners, reacts with water: it takes a **hydrogen ion from a water molecule**, making an ammonium ion and a hydroxide ion, O H minus. The ions also join back up, so this is an equilibrium too. Here water is a reactant, but it is still the solvent, so it **stays out**. The constant is **Kb**, the **base dissociation constant**: ammonium times hydroxide, over ammonia. For ammonia, Kb is **1.8 times ten to the minus five**, so most of the ammonia stays as molecules, which makes it a **weak base**.

### 8. the same rule for a solid
> One more, so you can see the pattern. A solid that barely dissolves, like silver chloride, sits in balance with its ions in the water around it. Silver chloride solid makes silver ions and chloride ions. The **solid stays out**, because it is a pure solid, so its K is just **silver ions times chloride ions**. It is called **Ksp**, and you will calculate with it in later videos.

Visual: the painted saturated beaker (`m5x-saturated-beaker-v1.png`, clear solution with solid at the bottom) on the left with a coded lens at the crystal surface: ions leave and rejoin the solid (two-way arrows). And read them the same way: when you compare reactions of the **same kind**, a bigger value means the reaction goes further to the right.

### 12. decision rule, boundary and handoff

### 13. key notes (quiet, hold about 70 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. A strong acid (for example HCl) ionises completely in water. A weak acid only partly ionises: its molecules and ions are in equilibrium. No Ka value is used for a strong acid.
2. Ka is K for a weak acid dissociating: HA(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + A-(aq), Ka = [H+][A-] / [HA]. Written with water, HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A-, Ka = [H3O+][A-] / [HA]. Water, the solvent, is left out.
3. Kb is K for a weak base reacting with water: NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq), Kb = [NH4+][OH-] / [NH3].
4. A bigger Ka means a stronger acid: at the same concentration and temperature, more of it is split into ions at equilibrium. Compare the powers of ten first. Ka is not [H+] and not pH.
5. The same rule gives Ksp for a solid dissolving: AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq), Ksp = [Ag+][Cl-] (the solid is left out).

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Ka, Kb and Ksp are just K with a name tag.* Condition tag: "products over reactants, water and solids left out; compare like with like at the same temperature."

## Teaching checks
