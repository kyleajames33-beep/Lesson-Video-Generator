### 2. from observation to question
> That's an observation, something noticed with our senses or instruments. It isn't an answer yet. To investigate it, we turn it into a scientific question, one a measurement can answer. "Why is falling so weird?" can't be measured. "Does a ball's mass change the time it takes to fall one metre?" can, because it names what we change, the mass, and what we measure, the fall **time**.

### 3. a hypothesis, with a reason
> Next, a hypothesis: a testable prediction, with a reason from what we already know. A useful pattern is if, then, because. If the mass of a steel ball doubles, then its fall time over one metre stays the same, because its weight doubles too, so its acceleration, weight divided by mass, is still g. Notice that it predicts the opposite of what we saw. That's allowed, when the physics gives a reason. If, then, because: the because is the physics. That's what makes it more than a **guess**.

### 5. a question only secondary data can answer
> Matching results support a hypothesis. They don't prove it. And some questions can only be answered with secondary data. Is g the same everywhere on Earth? We can't drop balls at the equator and at the poles ourselves. But measurement agencies publish g for places all around the world. Those values run from about nine point seven eight metres per second squared at the equator, to about nine point eight three at the poles. So it's a good question, answered with someone else's careful **measurements**.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Observation: something noticed with senses or instruments. Turn it into a scientific question that a measurement can answer: name what you change and what you measure.
2. Hypothesis: a testable prediction with a reason. If (the change), then (the result), because (the physics).
3. Primary data: measured by you. Secondary data: collected by someone else (a published film or data set). Some questions need secondary data (g around the Earth: about 9.78 to 9.83 m s⁻²). Matching data support a hypothesis; they don't prove it.
4. Judge with scientific knowledge. A question: narrow enough to test with data you can get. A hypothesis: testable, specific, a sound reason, and some result could show it wrong.
5. New evidence the first question can't explain: modify the question and hypothesis. If two things differ at once, you can't tell which one matters.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why an observation isn't an answer: it shows what happened, not why (2). Why a question must name variables: otherwise no measurement can answer it (2). Why a hypothesis needs a because: the reason is what the test supports or challenges (3). Why hypothesis 1 can go against the observation: the physics reason (a = W ÷ m = g) predicts it (3). Why a match doesn't prove: a later case could still disagree (5). Why some questions need secondary data: the measurements can't be made from one lab (5). Why B is a poor hypothesis: no result could contradict it (7). Why the paper changes the question: equal masses can't explain a difference, so mass isn't the cause (8). Why the first observation misled: two differences at once (9). Why the sheets land together on the Moon: with no air, nothing depends on shape (11).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** heavier things fall faster (1, 3, 4, 9); a hypothesis is a guess (3, crossed); a match proves a hypothesis (5, 11); a true statement makes a good hypothesis (7); the first comparison was a test of mass (9); evidence against a hypothesis means the law failed (feedback 2, crossed); vague hypotheses without variables (feedback 1, crossed).
