## Decisions

- **Learner question (route placeholder rewritten; root to enter).** The route's "How can I questioning and predicting in a defensible physics investigation?" becomes **"How does an observation become a hypothesis an experiment can test, and why should new evidence change it?"** One causal chain: an observation raises a question (1); a question is scientific when a measurement can answer it (2); a hypothesis predicts an answer and gives the physics reason (3); data, primary or secondary, test it, and some questions can only be answered with secondary data (4, 5); a question is judged by whether it is narrow enough to test, and a hypothesis by whether it is testable, specific, reasoned and could be shown wrong (6, 7); new evidence shows what the first question missed, so the question and hypothesis change (8, 9); the changed hypothesis predicts a new case, and secondary evidence fits it (10, 11).

### 2. from observation to question
> That's an observation, something noticed with our senses or instruments. It isn't an answer yet. To investigate it, we turn it into a scientific question, one a measurement can answer. "Why is falling so weird?" can't be measured. "Does a ball's mass change the time it takes to fall one metre?" can, because it names what we change, the mass, and what we measure, the fall **time**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why an observation isn't an answer: it shows what happened, not why (2). Why a question must name variables: otherwise no measurement can answer it (2). Why a hypothesis needs a because: the reason is what the test supports or challenges (3). Why hypothesis 1 can go against the observation: the physics reason (a = W ÷ m = g) predicts it (3). Why a match doesn't prove: a later case could still disagree (5). Why some questions need secondary data: the measurements can't be made from one lab (5). Why B is a poor hypothesis: no result could contradict it (7). Why the paper changes the question: equal masses can't explain a difference, so mass isn't the cause (8). Why the first observation misled: two differences at once (9). Why the sheets land together on the Moon: with no air, nothing depends on shape (11).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** heavier things fall faster (1, 3, 4, 9); a hypothesis is a guess (3, crossed); a match proves a hypothesis (5, 11); a true statement makes a good hypothesis (7); the first comparison was a test of mass (9); evidence against a hypothesis means the law failed (feedback 2, crossed); vague hypotheses without variables (feedback 1, crossed).
