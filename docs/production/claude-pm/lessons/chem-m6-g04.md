Where is the equivalence point, does the method find it, and if it misses, which way does the answer move?* Heading on screen: "Curves, indicators, back titration: where is equivalence?"
- **Why a practice video, and why these four questions.** C13 to C15 teach each tool once. Students lose these questions in four places: they read equivalence where the pH is 7 or choose an indicator "because neutralisation is neutral"; they apply "a bigger titre means a bigger concentration" without checking which solution is the unknown; they forget that a back titre measures what is left over, so its errors run the other way; and they read a conductivity equivalence point at a measured reading instead of where the lines cross. Each question hides one of these traps in a new case. - **Entry knowledge (do not re-teach; recall in one clause each).** From C12: a titration counts moles; n = cV; the balanced ratio; c = n ÷ V for the unknown; the unknown can be in the burette. From C13: the salt at equivalence (ammonium gives protons to water, so a strong acid with a weak base has an acidic equivalence point); choose the indicator whose colour change falls inside the steep part; a wrong indicator moves the endpoint; four ranges (methyl orange 3.1 to 4.4, methyl red 4.4 to 6.2, bromothymol blue 6.0 to 7.6, phenolphthalein 8.3 to 10.0). From C14: a curve plots pH against volume added; equivalence is in the middle of the steepest part; the strong acid/weak base profile, including the level stretch after equivalence when ammonia is added from the burette (excess ammonia and ammonium form a buffer); conductivity: hydronium and hydroxide conduct far better than other ions; a weak acid starts low and rises gently, then steeply after equivalence. From C15: the four back-titration steps (acid added, acid left over, subtract moles, mole ratio), percentage by mass; carbon dioxide boiled off; read conductometric equivalence where two best-fit lines cross; readings corrected for the volume added. From P06 (playlist order 19): rinse a burette with the solution it will deliver; a water-rinsed burette dilutes its solution. Year 11: n = m ÷ M. ### 2c. q1-indicator (which indicator?)
> Now the indicator. At equivalence, the flask holds ammonium chloride, and ammonium gives protons to water, so the pH there is **acidic**, about five. One drop either side of equivalence, the pH falls from about **six point six to four**. Methyl red changes between six point two and four point four, right **inside** that drop, so it fits best. Methyl orange changes at the **foot** of the drop: slightly late, but acceptable.

### 2d. q1-mistake (the classic mistake)

### 7. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. pH curve: equivalence is in the middle of the steepest part, not where pH = 7. Strong acid + weak base: equivalence acidic (NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O⁺), so methyl red (4.4 to 6.2), which changes inside the steep drop.
2. Error direction: first decide which way the titre moves, then follow it through the formula. Unknown in the flask: c = c(titrant) × V(titre) × ratio ÷ V(sample), so a larger titre gives a larger c. Unknown in the burette: c = n(standard) × ratio ÷ V(titre), so a larger titre gives a smaller c.
3. Ammonia in the burette with phenolphthalein: after equivalence an NH₃ and NH₄⁺ buffer holds the pH below 8.3 until extra ammonia is added, so the titre is too large and c(NH₃) too low.
4. Back titration: n(acid used) = n(added) − n(left over); the back titre measures the leftover. Anything that makes the back titre too large (a water-rinsed NaOH burette; CO₂ not boiled off) makes the % too low.
5. Conductivity, weak base with strong acid: starts low (few ions), rises gently (NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ form), then steeply (excess H₃O⁺). Equivalence where the best-fit lines cross, usually between readings.

- **"Move the titre, then follow it through the formula."** (new, not in the bank; flagged). Science: every titration error acts first on a measured volume (the titre), and its effect on the result depends on where that volume sits in the calculation: on top when the unknown is in the flask (direct), on the bottom when the unknown is in the burette, and subtracted when it measures an excess (back titration) (author note f). Assumptions: the error changes the titre, not the moles of the standard (an error in the standard's concentration, such as an unstandardised NaOH, acts on n directly and needs its own chain). Holds for Q2, Q3 and key note 4's error table. Confirmed by the science check (21 Oct). Learner tag carries the limit (standard concentration, mass or added-excess errors act on n, not the titre): "check which solution is the unknown and where it sits; if the error changes another value, follow that value instead". Not in the bank: Codex to add it as a draft with this assumption.
