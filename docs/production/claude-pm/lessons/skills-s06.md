- **The worked question (Biology, 8 marks in four parts):**
  - **Card:** "A hospital laboratory tested samples of the bacterium *Escherichia coli* from patients for resistance to antibiotic K. The table shows the percentage of samples that were resistant in five different years." Table: "Year | 2010 | 2012 | 2014 | 2017 | 2020"; "Samples resistant to antibiotic K (%) | 4 | 7 | 10 | 15 | 19". Blank grid below the table (coded grid paper, 20 by 15 squares).
  - **Why this case:** (1) the uneven year gaps (2, 2, 3, 3) create the classic scale trap, so part (a) teaches even scales with real data; (2) the data are close to linear, so a straight line of best fit is right and the points scatter a little around it; (3) 2016 is inside the data and 2040 is twenty years past it, so (c) and (d) contrast interpolation and extrapolation in one stimulus; (4) Biology viewers met resistance by selection in b11, and the card needs no content beyond itself, so Chemistry viewers can follow.

The video draws a straight line close to this fit and says "about thirteen" (accepted 12 to 14 in the illustrative guide) and "about fifty" (the news report's figure; the line reaches it). Numbers to verify.

(b) 2: direction (increasing steadily) and data with units (4% in 2010 to 19% in 2020; about 1.5 percentage points per year). (c) 1: about 13% (12 to 14%), read from the line with read-off lines shown. (d) 2: judgement (unreliable or weak) and reason (extrapolation twenty years beyond the data assumes the same rate continues; changes in prescribing, new drugs or infection control could change the trend).

### 1. hook
> Graph questions look easy, which is why they're easy to lose marks on. Across the last twelve Chemistry and Biology marking feedback reports, eleven commented on graphs drawn or read poorly, and nine on trends described without the **numbers**. So here's how to read a graph, draw one that earns its marks, describe a trend with data, and know when you can **extend** the line.

### 2. read the axes
> Before you read any value, read the **axes**: what's measured, in what units, and what one small square is worth. Then go from the axis to the line, across to the other axis, and write the value **with its unit**. If the line falls between gridlines, estimate to about half a small square. A number without its unit can cost the mark.

Visual:
- The hook graph enlarges. On "axes" the y-axis label lights amber ("Volume (mL)") and the x-axis label violet ("Time (s)"); on "one small square" a coded bracket spans one small square on each axis with chips "1 square = 2 mL" and "1 square = 10 s".
- On "from the axis to the line" a teal `Drop` read-off line runs up from x = 50 s to the line and across to the y-axis: "28".
- On "half a small square" the read-off point enlarges in a small inset showing the line crossing midway between two gridlines (tag "estimate: about half a square"). On "with its unit" the chip "28" grows to "28 mL"; a ghost chip "28" alone fades in beside it and takes a grey cross (caption "28 what?").

### 4. part a: the scale
> Part a. Put the independent variable, the year, along the **bottom**, and the percentage up the side. Each axis needs **even** steps, every gridline worth the same. Here's the trap: the years jump by two, then by three. Space the five years evenly, and you squash the later gaps and **bend** the trend. Place each year at its real value, and choose a scale that fills most of the grid, because a bigger plot lets you read values more precisely.

### 5. part a: plot and best fit
> Label each axis with what's measured and its **unit**, and add a title. Plot each point as a small, clear cross. Then draw a line of **best fit**: one smooth line that follows the trend, with the points scattered about evenly on both sides, as they are here. Don't join the dots. Real data has **scatter**, and dot to dot treats every wobble as real. Title, axes, intervals, labels, scale: **TAILS**.

### 6. part b: describe the trend
> Part b: describe the trend. Say the **direction**, then prove it with data. Resistance rose steadily, from four per cent in twenty ten to nineteen per cent in twenty twenty, about one and a half percentage points a **year**. If the pattern **changes** somewhere, say where. Here it doesn't, so steadily is the right word. Direction, data, change. And if the question says **explain**, give a plausible scientific reason: antibiotic use can select resistant bacteria, so they could make up more of the samples. The graph alone doesn't show that cause.

### 15. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, hold about 50 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Read the axes first: quantity, unit, value of one small square. Write every value with its unit.
2. Drawing (TAILS): Title, Axes (independent variable along the bottom), Intervals even (uneven years at their real positions), Labels with units, Scale. Small crosses; a line or curve of best fit, not dot to dot.
3. Describe a trend: direction, data (values with units, a rate if useful), and any change. Explain adds a plausible reason (the graph alone doesn't prove the cause). Say "percentage points" for a difference between percentages (4% to 19% = 15 points, a 375% increase).
4. Interpolate: read between data points from the line of best fit, with read-off lines shown.
5. Extrapolate: beyond the data, the value assumes the trend continues. If asked, extend it and state that assumption; judge how far beyond the data it goes.
6. Line graph: a continuous relationship or change over time (like years and percentages). Column graph: separate categories (like different antibiotics), with gaps between columns.

## Teaching checks

## Memory aid check

- **TAILS** (Title, Axes, Intervals, Labels, Scale). A common school aid, not NESA's. Assumptions: (1) a title is expected on a drawn graph: usual practice; HSC criteria shown in the pack name scale, labels, points and best fit, not a title, so the narration says "add a title" without claiming marks for it; (2) "Axes" here means the independent variable on the horizontal axis: the usual convention, true for both items; (3) "Intervals even": true; uneven data intervals are plotted at their real positions; (4) "Labels" means quantity and unit: true; (5) "Scale fills most of the grid": a convention that keeps reading precise; axes need not start at zero (not claimed either way). The aid lacks the best-fit rule, so it is always said with "then best fit".
- **Direction, data, change.** Author-written. Holds for the worked part (b) and drill 2. Condition: "change" may be "no change" (part b says so).
- **Inside the data, read it. Outside, doubt it.** Author-written. Condition, spoken: if a question asks you to extend a line, do it and state the assumption (Biology 2024 Q29(b) examined an extrapolation). It does not claim every extrapolation is wrong; a short extension near the data can be reasonable, which is why part (d)'s judgement rests on "twenty years past the last point".
