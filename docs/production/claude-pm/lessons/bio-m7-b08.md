### 6. helper T cells coordinate
> Helper T cells don't kill anything. They coordinate. ## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (22 Oct) against the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Doherty and Zinkernagel.** Peter Doherty and Rolf Zinkernagel, working at the John Curtin School of Medical Research, Australian National University, Canberra, from 1973, showed that cytotoxic T cells from virus-infected mice kill infected cells only if those cells share the same MHC type ("MHC restriction"); published in *Nature* in 1974. 1996 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries concerning the specificity of the cell mediated immune defence" (Nobel press release; American Association of Immunologists; Australian Academy of Science; checked 22 Oct). "Helped answer that" is correct. Revision 2 (Claude science check finding 1): D and Z showed that recognition needs virus antigen plus self MHC (1973 to 1975); the picture of a short peptide held in the MHC groove came later (Townsend and colleagues, *Cell* 1986; MHC I structure, Bjorkman and colleagues, *Nature* 1987). So scene 4 credits them only with "the clue: a T cell recognises a virus only together with the cell's own display molecule", and "So it checks the display molecule and the piece together" is the lesson's present-day statement (the T cell receptor recognises the peptide and the MHC molecule as one complex), not their finding. Correct.
- **Virus entry and replication.** Respiratory viruses bind host molecules on airway epithelial cells through surface proteins, enter, and use the host cell's machinery to make new virus particles, which leave and infect neighbouring cells. "Many new viruses" (yields vary widely by virus; no number spoken). Antibodies do not enter living cells, so they cannot act on virus inside a cell; they can neutralise virus released between cells. Correct.
- **Antigen presentation.** All nucleated cells process some of the proteins they make and display peptide fragments on MHC class I; an infected cell displays viral peptides among its own. Dendritic cells display peptides from engulfed material on MHC class II (to helper T cells) and can also display antigen on MHC class I to naive cytotoxic T cells (cross-presentation). Red blood cells lack nuclei and MHC I, so Revision 2 says "Almost every one of your cells" (science check finding 5: *Plasmodium* hides in red blood cells partly because they display no MHC I, so the absolute version could mislead later in the module).
- **T cell receptors.** T cells develop in the thymus; each has receptors of one specificity produced by random gene rearrangement (as B cells). T cell receptors recognise peptide bound to an MHC molecule, not free antigen. Correct.
- **Activation.** Naive T cells are activated in lymph nodes by dendritic cells displaying the antigen; only T cells with fitting receptors respond; they proliferate over several days into clones; helper (CD4) and cytotoxic (CD8) T cells come from separate naive cells. "The clones are of two main kinds" is the HSC simplification (regulatory T cells and others exist; not needed). Site error 1 resolved: scene 5 places activation in the lymph node; scene 7 places killing in the tissue with no helper signal.
- **Helper T cells.** Release cytokines; give linked help to B cells that display peptide from the same antigen (b07 site error 3); IL-2 and other signals support cytotoxic T cell proliferation; IFN-γ activates macrophages to kill engulfed microbes. "Make macrophages better at destroying what they engulf": correct. "Don't kill anything": correct for the HSC roles (rare cytotoxic CD4 cells exist; not needed).
- **Cytotoxic T cells.** Effector cytotoxic T cells leave lymph nodes, circulate, enter inflamed tissue, bind target cells displaying the matching peptide on MHC I, release perforin (forms pores) and granzymes (enter and trigger apoptosis), detach and can kill further targets (serial killing). "Others pass through": granzymes enter through perforin pores (and by related uptake routes); fair at this level. Apoptotic cells break into membrane-bound pieces that phagocytes clear. "Virus copies still being built inside are lost with it, so the virus loses its factory": hedged; correct (apoptosis halts virus production; finished particles may remain in apoptotic bodies that phagocytes take up).
- **NK cells.** Innate lymphocytes; kill infected or stressed cells when activating signals (stress-induced ligands) outweigh inhibitory signals (MHC I); some viruses reduce MHC I to avoid cytotoxic T cells, which makes them more visible to NK cells (b06 site error 12's correction). "They respond to stress signals on a cell, and to cells displaying too few display molecules": correct. NK cells have many receptors (inhibitory receptors for MHC I, activating receptors such as NKG2D); what they lack is a randomly made receptor for one antigen, so Revision 2 says "They don't have receptors made for one pathogen" (science check finding 4). "NK cells act early" (within the first days); cytotoxic T cell responses peak about a week or more after infection: correct as a comparison; no number spoken.
- **Two arms.** Humoral (antibody) immunity acts on extracellular pathogens and toxins; cell-mediated immunity acts on infected cells; helper T cells support both. Memory T cells form from both kinds (b09). Correct.
- **Attempt 1.** Classic cytotoxic T cell killing assay (the chromium release assay design used by Doherty and Zinkernagel, simplified, with same-strain target cells). Original wording; correct.
- **Attempt 2.** HIV infects helper T cells (CD4 plus co-receptor; site error 6); untreated infection lowers helper T cell numbers over years; antibody and cytotoxic responses both weaken progressively (not "fail": some T-independent antibody responses and innate defences continue; science check finding 2); most CD8 expansion relies on CD4 help, though some primary CD8 responses expand without it (finding 3, hence "rely on" and "usually depend on"); opportunistic infections follow. Original; correct.
