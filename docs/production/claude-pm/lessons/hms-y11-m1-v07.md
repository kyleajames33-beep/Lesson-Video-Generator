### 2. a question sharp enough to answer
> Most weak investigations fail before any data is collected, because the question can't be answered. Four tests catch most problems. Can you collect data to answer it? Is it specific about who and what? Is it neutral, or does it lead people to an answer? And would different answers actually be interesting? "Why do young people ignore their mental health?" fails, because it's decided they do. Try, "What do Year 11 students at this school say health means to them?"

### 3. choosing a method
> The syllabus names three methods, and each one buys something different. A **survey** reaches lots of people quickly, but its options are written in advance, so for a question about meaning, the options become the answer. An **interview** can follow up a surprising answer and ask why, but it's slow, so the sample is small. A **semi-structured interview** uses a set list of questions, with room to follow up. A **focus group** shows how views form when people hear each other, but louder voices dominate, and people hold back in front of peers. For meanings, a strong design is mixed: a short open survey to find the range, then interviews to understand a few in depth.

### 4. who you actually asked
> Next, be honest about who you actually asked. Your sample is the people who answered. The population is everyone you want to describe. They're not the same. If a student surveys her own basketball team about fitness and health, she's learned about her team, not about young people. And volunteers aren't random either. The students most interested in health are the most likely to sign up, which pushes the answers in a direction you can name.

### 5. ethics, which isn't paperwork
> Research about health asks people about their bodies, their families and sometimes their worst weeks, so ethics matters as much here as in a university study. People give **informed consent**, knowing what's involved and that they can stop, and under eighteen that usually means a parent or guardian too. Taking part must be truly voluntary, which is harder when the researcher is a friend or a captain. Decide whether you're offering anonymity or confidentiality, and say which. Plan for distress, and know where to send someone for support. Research with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people needs community consultation and control. And report everything you found, including the answers that spoil your pattern.

And never promise anonymity on a survey that asks for a name and a class.

### 7. validity, reliability and credibility
> Now, can your data be trusted? Three separate questions. **Validity**: did you measure what you meant to measure? Counting how often students exercise doesn't tell you what health means to them. **Reliability**: would you get the same result again? If interviewers word questions differently, answers move for the wrong reasons. **Credibility**: can you trust the source, who collected it, and what did they stand to gain? These are independent. A study can be reliable and still measure the wrong thing.

> The question needs meanings in people's own words. So choose semi-structured interviews, which can ask why. Then reject the alternatives with reasons. ## Practice question and feedback

Two unseen items (10a to 10d), "Pause here to answer." and a 10 s countdown each, cards on screen during and after; marks on the card; tag "HSC-style question, written for HSCScience". Item 1 is written for this video in Revision 2 (it tests the site's reliability idea on an unseen case); item 2 the site's WE b1 stem verbatim.

1. (10a, 3 marks.) The same survey run twice, after exams and in a normal week (Revision 2: the site's three-interviewer case was scene 7's worked reliability example).
   Model: reliability (1 mark), because the instrument and the participants were the same but the conditions changed between runs, so repeating the method did not reproduce the result (1 mark); repair: run both administrations at comparable times with the same instructions (1 mark).
