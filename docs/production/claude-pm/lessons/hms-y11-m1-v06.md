### 1. hook (two desks)
> We've followed the chain of determinants for whole groups. Now let's point it at one group, young people. Think of someone who was sixteen about thirty years ago, a parent, an aunt or a teacher. What was the same about being sixteen then, and what's changed? People love to answer that with a strong opinion. Today, we'll answer it with a method.

### 2. what stays the same
> Start with what's constant. Adolescence, the stage between childhood and adulthood, comes with the same developmental tasks in every generation we have records for. Your body changes faster than your sense of yourself. Your reasoning grows. You work out who you are, separately from your family, which is called identity formation. Friends start to matter more than family. And you take over decisions that used to be made for you, which is autonomy. One more constant: the brain's reward system tends to mature earlier than its impulse control, so risk-taking rises in every generation.

Visual: board change to the tasks spine (solid from its first frame): the desks recede to a soft strip at the top and a horizontal olive spine draws across the frame with five empty task slots. ### 3. what the constants mean for health
> These constants matter for health. More risk-taking is why injury is a leading cause of harm for young people, generation after generation. Friends mattering more is why programs run by peers often work better than lectures from adults. And growing autonomy is why habits set in adolescence tend to last. But don't turn this into a deficit story. The same brain that takes more risks also learns fast, adapts quickly and tries new things. That's a trade-off, not a fault.

### 4. task or form?
> Here's the move that turns opinions into analysis. Separate a change in the task from a change in its form, meaning how the task gets done. Take friends. A sixteen-year-old in the nineties cared a lot about what friends thought, and so does a sixteen-year-old today. The task hasn't changed. Its form has. Friends were once the people in the room or on the home phone. Now they're available all the time, the group is bigger, and approval arrives as a public number.

Visual: board change to the desks (solid from their first frame): the plate full width again, the olive spine now running across both desks at mid-height; at the foot the `TaskFormTable` frame with headings "the task" (olive) and "how its form has changed" (cool), rows empty. ### 5. identity and autonomy, in a new form

That longer stretch between adolescence and settled adult life is called emerging adulthood.

### 6. five influences, constant then change
> The syllabus asks about five influences, and each one has a constant and a change. Family is still the first source of health habits, but households are more varied and young people depend on them for longer. Peers matter as much as ever, now continuously. Culture still sets the norms, but some have shifted, so smoking is far less normal, and talking about mental health is more accepted. Every generation adopts new technology faster than adults, but one device now carries school, friends and advertising. And global events always shape a generation, but news now arrives instantly and never stops.

### 7. a claim you can defend
> So how do you make a generational claim that holds up? Use four tests. First, is it the age, or the era? Adults describing young people are often just describing being young. Second, compared with what, and measured how? More diagnoses can mean more people seeking help, not more illness. Third, which young people? The differences within a generation are usually bigger than the differences between generations. And fourth, does anything follow for health?

### 8. why it matters for health
> Why compare generations in a health course at all? Three reasons. And it stops the deficit story, because young people treated as a problem are less likely to use the services meant for them.

## Practice question and feedback

Two unseen items (10a to 10d), "Pause here to answer." and a 10 s countdown each, cards on screen during and after; marks on the card; tag "HSC-style question, written for HSCScience". Item 1 applies the site's Activity 1 method to a constant from scene 2 (Revision 2: the site's peer claim was scene 4's worked example); item 2 the site's WE b1 stem verbatim.

1. (10a, 3 marks.) "Teenagers today take far more risks than we ever did."
   Model: the task (risk-taking rising in adolescence, as the reward system matures before impulse control) is constant across generations, so the claim as stated is hard to support (1 mark); what has changed is the form: which risks (1 mark); a defensible rewrite names them, for example "some risks such as smoking and drink driving have fallen among young people, while online risks are new", supported with trend data by age on the same measure (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed: "phones made teenagers reckless". Other wrong chains: accepting the claim because of news stories (no measure, no comparison).
2. (10c, 5 marks.) Compare young people today with a previous generation, three influences.
   Model (the site's, shortened): the organising principle, tasks constant, compare form (1 mark); three influences each with a similarity and a difference in the same paragraph, for example peers, family and technology (3 marks); a judgement, for example more similarity than public discussion allows, with differences in scale, continuity and visibility, and differences within a generation larger than between (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed: a "then" paragraph and a "now" paragraph.

Feedback spoken lengths about 25 s (10b, Revision 2) and 29 s (10d, Revision 2 trim) by script; time the exact audio.

- **Peer-led programs (3).** "often work better than lectures from adults": the site's claim ("peer-based health interventions outperform authority-based ones"); the evidence is mixed by topic, so "often" is spoken, not "always". Reviewer to confirm.
