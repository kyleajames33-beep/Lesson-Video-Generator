### 1. hook: what does each one tell us?
> In an exam, the graph is usually drawn for you, and the marks are for reading it. Every display has its own rules, but the same routine works for all of them. Read the key and the scale. Read a value. Count the values that fit a condition. Compare. And then say what it means, in context. We'll use it on six displays, starting with this batter's season. So how do we read dot plots, stem-and-leaf plots, line graphs, sector graphs and divided bar **charts**?

### 2. stem-and-leaf plots
> A stem-and-leaf plot keeps every value. The stem is the leading digit, the leaf is the last digit, and the key tells you how they join. Two, four is twenty-four runs. Without the key, two four could just as easily mean two point four. The leaves are in order, so the batter's fifteen scores run from nought, four and seven, up to eighty-seven. How many times did the batter score thirty or more? Count the leaves from the three stem down. Thirty-three, thirty-six, forty-one, fifty-eight, sixty-three, eighty-seven. Six **innings**. And because the values are in order, the middle score is easy to find. Fifteen scores, so the eighth one, twenty-four. In context, this batter usually scores under forty, with the occasional big **innings**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): the scoreboard plate shrinks to a badge (about 300 px) at the top left; the stem-and-leaf enlarges (52 px) with stems in a grey column and leaves in blue. On "the key" the key rings (hand tag "key first"). On "Two, four is twenty-four runs" the leaf "4" on stem 2 lifts and joins "2" to show "24". On "The leaves are in order" the leaves on each row underline left to right (hand tag "leaves in order"); every leaf unfolds into a strip of 15 full values along the bottom (0, 4, 7, 12, ..., 87). On "thirty or more" stems 3 to 8 light; leaves tick off "33, 36, 41, 58, 63, 87"; on "innings" (first bold) row "30 or more: 6 innings" (framed green). On "the eighth one" the strip counts 1 to 8 and rings "24" (violet ring; grey chip "middle of 15: the 8th"). ### 3. back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots
> To compare two batters, share the stems. Batter B's leaves go to the right, as usual. Batter A's leaves go to the left, reading outwards from the stem, so four, two on the left is twenty-four, not forty-two. Now compare row by row. Batter A has three scores under ten, and Batter B has just one. Batter A also has the three biggest scores, fifty-eight, sixty-three and eighty-seven, while Batter B never passes forty-five. Their middle scores are close, twenty-four and twenty-eight. So Batter B is the more consistent batter, and Batter A is more likely to fail, or to win you the **game**.

Visual: stage change: the back-to-back plot (about 1,100 px wide), stems grey down the centre spine, A's leaves (blue) at the left, B's leaves (hollow blue) at the right; keys at each side: "A: 4 | 2 = 24", "B: 2 | 4 = 24". ### 4. dot plots
> The same season, from the bowler's end. This dot plot shows how many wickets one bowler took in each of sixteen matches. Each dot is one match, sitting above its number of wickets. Every dot is a real match, so nothing is grouped or lost. The tallest stack is above two, so two wickets was the most common result. How many matches did she take three or more? Count the dots, not the heights. Three above three, one above four, one above five, five **matches**. A dot plot shows every value and where they bunch up, and it works best for small sets of numbers, like **these**.

### 5. line graphs
> A line graph shows change over time. Here's a phone's battery level through one day, measured every two hours, from a hundred per cent at seven in the morning. Read a value. At one in the afternoon it's sixty per cent. Then look at the shape. It falls all day, except between four and five, where it jumps by thirty. That rise only makes sense one way. It was on the charger. Where's the steepest fall? From seven to nine at night, fifty-eight down to forty-one, seventeen points in two **hours**, perhaps a movie on the way home. The line between the points shows the trend, but only the dots were actually **measured**.

### 6. sector graphs and divided bar charts
> Sector graphs and divided bar charts both show parts of one whole. Here's a student's two hundred dollar weekly budget as a sector graph. The whole circle is three hundred and sixty degrees, so each sector's share is its angle out of three sixty. Food is a hundred and eight degrees. That's a hundred and eight out of three sixty, thirty per cent, and thirty per cent of two hundred dollars is sixty dollars. Now unroll the circle into a bar. Same parts, same order, now as lengths. A divided bar chart works the same way, with length instead of angle. Either way, the parts must add to the whole, a full circle or the full bar. This twenty centimetre bar shows how our two hundred Year 11 students get to school. Bus takes six centimetres, six out of twenty, thirty per cent, sixty **students**. The angle or the length gives the share. The share times the total gives the **amount**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 4): the coin jar `ma12-coin-jar-v1.png` (about 260 px) at the left with chip "$200 a week"; a sector graph (about 560 px) with five olive-tint sectors labelled (violet): food 108°, transport 54°, phone 36°, savings 90°, entertainment 72°. ## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why ordered leaves make reading easy (2); why shared stems allow comparison and why the left reads outwards (3); why dots are counted (4); why slope shows rate and a rise means charging (5); why angle ÷ 360 and length ÷ whole give the share (6).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** no key (2); left leaves inwards (3, A2); stack heights as counts (4); between-point values (5); angle as amount (6, A1).
- **Visual standard, item by item:** 1 the scoreboard and the routine strip in the first 20 s; 2 a new stage every 40 to 70 s; 3 the leaf unfold, the dot stack, the battery slider, the sector unrolling into a bar; 4 fixed roles; 5 large type; 6 hand tags; 7 g01 rows with framed answers and crossed slips; 8 framed card and five bullets; 9 the circle unrolling into a bar.
- **One idea at a time:** one display per scene (2 to 5), then the two part-of-whole displays together (6).
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen tasks with every demand visible; 10 s each; feedback about 20.0 s and 18.8 s.
- **Avoided double teaching:** d07's purposes are recalled in chips; the middle score is read, not called the median.
- **Syllabus coverage:** `cib2984676` (2 to 6, A1, A2; notes 1 to 5): stem-and-leaf (2), back-to-back (3, A2), dot plot (4), line graph (5), sector graph (6, A1), divided bar chart (6).
