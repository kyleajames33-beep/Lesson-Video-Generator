### 2. convert first
> No. It's ten times faster. Centimetres and milliseconds aren't metres and seconds, so the number came out in the wrong unit. Centi means a hundredth, so five point oh oh centimetres is zero point oh five oh oh metres. Milli means a thousandth, so forty-seven point eight eight milliseconds is zero point oh four seven eight eight seconds. Now divide. The speed is one point oh four four metres per **second**. So convert to metres, kilograms and seconds before you substitute, and keep the units in every **line**.

Visual: the "no: faster" chip fills; the "0.104 m s⁻¹ ?" readout is crossed (muted red scribble) with a small tag "units: cm per ms, not m per s". `UnitConverter` on "5.00 cm": the "c" lifts off as "× 10⁻²" (gloss "centi: a hundredth"), and the value rewrites "5.00 × 10⁻² m = 0.0500 m" (teal box). ### 3. a sense check
> Is that sensible? Convert it to a unit you can picture. One kilometre per hour is a thousand metres in three thousand six hundred seconds. So to go from metres per second to kilometres per hour, multiply by three point six. One point oh four four times three point six is about three point eight kilometres per hour, a slow walk. That fits a trolley rolling down a gentle **ramp**.

Visual: rows "1 km h⁻¹ = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s", "so m s⁻¹ × 3.6 = km h⁻¹" (gloss "kilo: a thousand"); "1.044 × 3.6 ≈ 3.8 km h⁻¹". `SpeedCompare` scale 0 to 25 km h⁻¹ with grey marks "slow walk ≈ 4", "jog ≈ 10", "cycling ≈ 20"; the marker slides to 3.8. ### 4. a processed column and the table
> Converting is one quantitative process. Another is calculating a new quantity from each reading. In the falling-ball practical, the model, s equals half g t squared, says distance should be proportional to time squared. So the table needs a t squared column, calculated from each time. Height, the independent variable, goes first. Each heading gives the quantity and its unit in brackets, so the cells hold plain numbers. Raw readings come first, then the processed **column**.

### 5. a spreadsheet, and a check
