You can't tell from the reading, because a buffer isn't defined by its pH. It's defined by what it **does**. So in this practical you'll make a buffer, and then prove it.

> From the buffers video, a buffer needs a **weak acid** and its **conjugate base**, both in large amounts. We'll use ethanoic acid, and sodium ethanoate to supply the ethanoate. ### 4. measure its pH
> Measure it with the calibrated meter, using the routine from the pH practical. One class's mean was **four point six eight**, a little below the prediction. The simple ratio calculation treats the ions as if they don't affect each other, and real ions **do**, a little. Stock concentrations aren't perfect either. What matters here is how much the pH **changes**.

Acid and base go into **different** portions, because base added after acid would just undo it.

### 6. the results: acid and base
> Here are the means. The buffer went from four point six eight to four point five nine with acid, and to four point seven seven with base. That's **less than a tenth** of a unit either way. The water started at five point nine three, because of dissolved carbon dioxide, and **plunged** to two point three five with acid, and shot up to eleven point six three with base. The buffer **resists** the change. It doesn't prevent it.

### 7. why the buffer barely moved
> Why so little? Each buffer portion holds zero point zero zero one zero zero moles of each partner, and the acid dose is **one tenth** of that. Ethanoate takes up the added hydronium, so the ethanoic acid goes up and the ethanoate goes down. Using hydronium equals K a times acid over base, the **ratio** moves from one to about one point two, and the predicted pH falls from four point seven four to **four point six six**. That's the same small drop you measured.
