## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "What changes when waves cross a boundary between media?" It is v19's handoff chip and already a causal, answerable question (open decision 1). The answer, built in order: the plan and its two pairs of media (1, 2); a controlled, traced method (3); one trace: the ray bends in, and leaves parallel (4 to 6); repeats and the table (7); the relationships: smaller in glass, the gap grows, not proportional (8); along the normal, nothing bends; some light reflects (9); in water waves, the frequency stays and the wavelength shortens with the speed (10, 11); so speed, wavelength and direction change, and frequency stays (12); limitations (13); risks (14).
- **The hook (v19's frame, made a plan).** "v19 said the light slows and bends. Today we test it, and find what else changes." No prediction in the hook; the two in-teaching predictions are the emerging ray (4) and the ripple tank's frequency (10).
- **Two pairs of media (`ci768c84eb`; site card 3 plus the ripple tank).** Air and glass with the ray box: the site's method, and the one that shows direction. Deep and shallow water in v18's ripple tank (a coded clear plastic sheet makes the shallow region): the only way to watch wavelength and frequency, because light's wavelength is far too small to see. Both are "refraction between 2 media"; deep and shallow water are glossed as "for water waves, a change of depth acts like a new medium, because it changes the speed" (author note e).
- **Illustrative data (labelled; generating model stated).** Air to glass, model n = 1.50, read to the nearest degree. Angles of incidence 0° to 70° in 10° steps; three traces each, symmetric about each mean: 0° 0, 0, 0; 10° 6, 7, 8; 20° 12, 13, 14; 30° 18, 19, 20; 40° 24, 25, 26; 50° 30, 31, 32; 60° 34, 35, 36; 70° 38, 39, 40. - **The trace method (site card 3 and Q3, made visual).** Block on white paper, outline traced; normal drawn at the entry point with the protractor (fa1-v21's plate); ray box turned so the beam meets the entry point at the set angle; two dots on the emerging beam far apart; block lifted off (coded fade); the dots joined to the exit point; the entry and exit points joined (the path in the glass); θr measured from the normal. - **Prediction 1 (3 s, scene 4).** "The beam leaves the far side of the block, back into air. Which way will it point?" Chips "parallel to the beam that went in?", "bent even further?". - **Variables and controls, with reasons (`ci9ce164ec` applied).** IV: the angle of incidence (set by turning the ray box; read from the normal). DV: the angle of refraction in the glass. Controlled, each with its reason: the same block and the same face (same glass, same n); the same ray box and slit (same light, same beam width); a darkened room and white paper (the faint beam is visible and its edges sharp).
- **Reliability (`ci6c11c587`, partial).** Three traces per angle, the ray box re-set each time, the spread read as a range; the method is the same at every angle. A range of 2° at every angle (illustrative) is said once.
- **Relationships (`ci7f1f6fbd`, partial; site card 3's "the larger the slow-down, the larger the bend" corrected by v19's site correction 2).** Qualitative: θr is smaller than θi at every angle but 0° (towards the normal), and the gap grows with θi (3° at 10°, 31° at 70°). Quantitative: θi ÷ θr rises from 1.4 to 1.8, so θr is not proportional to θi; doubling θi from 30° to 60° takes θr from 19° to 35°, not 38° (author note c). Shown with a θr against θi graph: the points curve away below the dashed line θr = θi, and a dashed grey "if proportional" line from the origin through the 10° point drifts above the later points. This is the question v21 answers.
- **Along the normal and the faint reflection (site Q3's "change of direction at the boundary").** The 0° row: no bend, but (v19) the light still slows. A faint reflected ray at each face, obeying v18's law, said in one clause.
- **Prediction 2 (3 s, scene 10).** "In shallow water, the waves slow down. What happens to their frequency?" Chips "it drops?", "it stays the same?". Answered in 11.
- **Why the frequency stays (the cause; `cibc9c30ac` applied).** "The bar makes ten waves every second. Each crest that reaches the boundary sends one across. Crests can't pile up at the boundary or vanish, so ten cross every second, on both sides." (Revision 2 wording; a little energy reflects, as scene 6 shows.) Then v = fλ (v05): same f, lower v, so a shorter λ, by the same factor. Illustrative tank values: f = 10 Hz; deep λ 3.0 cm, v 0.30 m s⁻¹; shallow λ 2.0 cm, v 0.20 m s⁻¹ (λ measured across five wavelengths, six crests, with a ruler in the tank, author note f). At an angle the crests turn towards the normal (v19's pivot, seen in water).
- **The conclusion (`ci8856aeb0` applied).** "When a wave crosses into a new medium, its speed changes, so its wavelength changes by the same factor, and unless it arrives along the normal, its direction changes. Its frequency doesn't, because the source sets it." For light: one line, "Light behaves the same way: its colour, set by its frequency, stays the same in glass, while its wavelength shortens", with a grey tag "too small to see here".
- **Limitations and improvements (`ci7e38e278` applied).** (1) Beam width: a wide beam leaves the entry point and line unclear; improve: the narrowest slit, a sharp pencil, dots at least 5 cm apart. (2) Protractor reading ±1°: 1° is about 14 per cent of 7° but under 3 per cent of 39°, so small angles are the least reliable; improve: take more readings at small angles and favour larger ones for comparisons (author note g). (3) The block shifting between traces: check it still sits inside its traced outline before each trace.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 15; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "In an investigation, a ray of light passes from air into a perspex block. The angle of refraction was measured three times at each angle of incidence (illustrative data). θi = 30°: θr = 19°, 20°, 21°. θi = 60°: θr = 35°, 36°, 37°. (a) Calculate the mean angle of refraction for each angle of incidence. (b) Use the data to assess whether the angle of refraction is proportional to the angle of incidence. (c) State what the results show about the speed of light in perspex."
Model answer: (a) 30°: (19 + 20 + 21) ÷ 3 = 20°; 60°: (35 + 36 + 37) ÷ 3 = 36°. (b) Doubling θi from 30° to 60° increases the mean θr from 20° to 36°, a factor of 36 ÷ 20 = 1.8, not 2, and the difference (16°) is larger than the spread of the readings (2°), so θr is not proportional to θi. (c) θr < θi, so the ray bends towards the normal on entering the perspex: light travels more slowly in perspex than in air. Classic mistake (crossed): "the angle of refraction is proportional to the angle of incidence".

**Attempt 2 (scene 17; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested).** "In a ripple tank, a straight bar vibrating at 12 Hz produces waves that travel at 0.24 m s⁻¹ in deep water. In the shallow region, the wavelength is 1.5 cm. (a) Calculate the wavelength in the deep water. (b) Calculate the speed of the waves in the shallow water. (c) Explain why the frequency of the waves is the same in both regions."
Model answer: (a) λ = v/f = 0.24 ÷ 12 = 0.020 m = 2.0 cm. (b) v = fλ = 12 × 0.015 = 0.18 m s⁻¹. (c) The frequency is set by the source (the bar, 12 Hz). Each crest that reaches the boundary makes a crest on the other side (some energy also reflects); crests cannot pile up at the boundary or disappear, so 12 crests cross each second and the frequency in the shallow water is also 12 Hz. Classic mistake (crossed): "the frequency drops in shallow water because the waves slow down".
