### 3. net movement down the gradient
> More cross to the right. Not because they're aiming that way, but because there are more of them on the left to wander through the gap by chance. A few still cross back, so what matters is the net movement, the overall result. A difference in concentration between two places is called a concentration gradient, and particles spread down it, from high to low. When both sides are even, particles keep moving both ways, with no net change. The energy comes from the particles' own motion, so this is passive: the cell spends nothing.

Visual: on "More cross to the right" the counters appear and run: "→" ticks fast, "←" slowly. ### 4. simple diffusion
> Simple diffusion is that spreading, straight through the phospholipid bilayer. It works for substances that dissolve in the oily core: small molecules with no charge, like oxygen and carbon dioxide. Inside a cell, mitochondria use up oxygen in respiration, so the oxygen concentration inside stays low. That keeps the gradient going, so oxygen keeps diffusing in. Carbon dioxide is made inside the cell, so it diffuses out. And the steeper the gradient, the faster the net movement.

### 8. a door, not an engine
> Here's the trap. Facilitated diffusion uses a protein, but it still needs no energy from the cell. If the gradient flipped, glucose would flow back out through the same carrier. A door is not an engine. The protein does set a speed limit, though. Once every carrier is busy, adding more glucose outside can't make it go any faster, so the rate levels off.

### 9. one cause, three routes
> So all three are passive, with one shared cause: random motion spreads particles down a concentration gradient. What changes is the route. Small uncharged molecules go straight through the bilayer. Water crosses a partially permeable membrane, towards the concentrated side. Ions and large polar molecules go through channels or carriers. For ions, electrical forces matter too; here we consider concentration alone. The gradient sets the direction, and the substance decides the route.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Particles move randomly. Where there are more on one side, more cross from that side by chance: net movement from high to low concentration (down the concentration gradient) until even. Passive: no energy from the cell.
- Simple diffusion: small uncharged molecules (O₂, CO₂) move down their gradient straight through the phospholipid bilayer.
- Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from higher to lower water concentration (towards the more concentrated solution). In this example the sugar is blocked by the membrane and water crosses in both directions. Many cells use aquaporins (water channels).
- Facilitated diffusion: ions and large polar molecules (glucose) move down their gradient through channel or carrier proteins. Still passive (no cell energy drives the downhill step). For ions, electrical forces matter too; here we consider concentration alone. The rate levels off when all the carrier proteins are busy.

Memory: the gradient sets the direction; the substance decides the route. A door is not an engine. Water moves towards the sugar (here the sugar is blocked).
