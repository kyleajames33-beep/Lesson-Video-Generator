### 2. the meter and the kilowatt-hour
> Start at the meter. It counts up forever, like a car's odometer. At the last bill it read eighteen thousand four hundred and fifty-two. Now it reads nineteen thousand two hundred and sixty-four. So the house used the difference, eight hundred and twelve kilowatt-**hours**. A kilowatt-hour is the energy a thousand-watt appliance uses in one hour. Kilo means a thousand. So a two-kilowatt heater running for an hour uses two kilowatt-hours, and at thirty-three cents each, that's sixty-six **cents**. An air conditioner using two and a half kilowatts, for six hours on a hot afternoon, uses fifteen kilowatt-hours, four ninety-**five**. The bigger the appliance, and the longer it runs, the more it **costs**.

### 3. building the electricity bill
> Every electricity bill has two parts. The supply charge is a daily charge for being connected, even if you use nothing. Here it's an example one dollar ten a day, for ninety-one days, a hundred dollars **ten**. Then the usage charge, a price for each kilowatt-hour you use. Eight hundred and twelve, at thirty-three cents, is two hundred and sixty-seven dollars ninety-**six**. Add them, and the bill is three hundred and sixty-eight dollars and six **cents**. These prices already include GST, so there's nothing more to add. Rates differ between suppliers and change each year. Forgetting the supply charge is the classic **slip**. It's there on every bill, whether you use a little or a **lot**.

### 4. checking a bill: cost per day
> Is three sixty-eight high? Comparing it with the last bill can mislead you, because bills cover different numbers of days, and winter and summer use different amounts. So divide by the days, and compare with the same season last year. This bill is three sixty-eight oh six over ninety-one days, about four dollars and four cents a **day**. The same quarter last year was four hundred and twelve dollars sixteen, over ninety-two days, four dollars forty-eight a **day**. So the house is using a bit less than last year. If the cost per day had jumped, you'd look for a reason, like a new heater, or a wrong **reading**.

Water bills also have a service charge, here a hundred and ninety dollars a quarter, so the whole bill is three hundred and forty-six **sixty**. If you rent in New South Wales, the landlord usually pays the service charge, and you pay the **usage**.

### 6. gas: megajoules in two blocks
> Gas is measured in megajoules. This quarter the house used thirty-nine hundred. Gas is often priced in blocks, a band of usage with its own price, like the sliding scale in commission. Here, the first eighteen hundred megajoules cost four point eight cents each, and the rest cost three point nine cents. Careful with cents. Four point eight cents is nought point nought four eight dollars. So the first block is eighteen hundred times nought point nought four eight, eighty-six forty. The other twenty-one hundred megajoules cost eighty-one **ninety**. Add the supply charge, seventy-five cents a day for ninety-one days, sixty-eight twenty-five, and the gas bill is two hundred and thirty-six dollars fifty-**five**. Charging all of it at the first price gives two fifty-five forty-five, which overcharges the house nearly nineteen **dollars**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why subtract readings: the meter counts up forever (2). Why kW × hours: energy depends on power and time (2). Why two parts: you pay to be connected and for what you use (3). Why per day and same season: bills differ in length and season (4). Why blocks change the working: each band has its own price (6).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** a reading as the usage (2); supply charge forgotten (3, A1 crossed); GST added again (3); all gas at one rate (6, A2 crossed); cents as dollars (6).
- **Visual standard, item by item:** 1 the meter from the first frame; 2 a new stage every 40 to 60 s; 3 the meter rolling, the power-time area filling, the bill filling with daily coins and units, gas spilling between blocks; 4 fixed roles; 5 large type; 6 hand tags; 7 g01 rows with framed answers and crossed slips; 8 framed card and five bullets; 9 19,264 shrinking to 812.
- **One idea at a time:** readings and kWh (2), the bill (3), the check (4), water (5), gas (6).
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen tasks with every demand visible; 10 s each; feedback about 27 s and 21 s.
- **Avoided double teaching:** per-unit sharing (m06), the sliding scale (e03) and GST-inclusive prices (m02) in chips; prefixes left to a04.
- **Syllabus coverage:** `ci345de108` (2 to 6, A1, A2; key notes 1 to 5): electricity (2 to 4), water (5), gas (6).
- **Out of scope by design:** budgets; time-of-use tariffs; council rates.
