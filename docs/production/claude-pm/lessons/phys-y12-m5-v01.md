### 3. projectile and the model
> Once B leaves the arm, nothing pushes it forward. An object moving under gravity alone is called a projectile, and its path is its **trajectory**. We'll make two assumptions. First, zero air resistance, the push of the air on a moving object. A feather fails that **badly**, but on the Moon, with no air, a hammer and a feather dropped together landed **together**. For a steel ball over a short drop, air resistance is small enough to ignore. Second, gravity's acceleration is constant, nine point eight metres per second squared, **downwards**.

### 4. horizontal: no force, constant velocity
> Start with the horizontal direction. What forces act sideways on the ball in the air? With no air resistance, **none**. Its weight acts straight down. No horizontal force means no horizontal acceleration, so by Newton's first law, the horizontal velocity can't change. B keeps moving sideways at two metres per second for the whole **flight**, the same twenty **centimetres** between every pair of flashes. So horizontal displacement is horizontal velocity times **time**.

### 5. vertical: weight only, same fall

Same starting vertical velocity, same vertical acceleration, so their vertical motions are **identical**. ### 6. two columns, one clock
> So one projectile becomes two simpler motions: constant velocity sideways, and constant acceleration vertically. Neither changes the other. Launching B faster doesn't hold it up, and falling doesn't slow it sideways. But both happen to one ball at the same moments, so they share one **clock**. Time is the only thing the two columns have in common. Find it from one motion, and you can use it in the **other**.

### 7. worked example: when does B land?
> Let's use that link to find where B lands. We know how far B **falls**, but not for how long, so start with the vertical column. Up is positive, so B's vertical displacement is minus one point two metres, its initial vertical velocity is zero, and a y is minus nine point eight. Using s equals u t plus a half a t squared, minus one point two equals minus four point nine t **squared**. So t squared is one point two over four point nine, and t is about zero point four nine **seconds**.

Visual: board clears to the calculation layout, the stage small at the left showing the bench, the floor and B's path. Task strip: "Where does ball B land?" Equation strip: "s_y = u_y t + ½a_y t²" (blue) and "s_x = u_x t" (teal, dimmed). Givens grouped in two navy boxes under the column colours: horizontal "u_x = 2.0 m s⁻¹"; vertical "s_y = −1.2 m (floor below launch)", "u_y = 0", "a_y = −9.8 m s⁻²". On "falls" the teal box gets a grey "t ?" and the blue box a tick "known: s_y, u_y, a_y"; the curved arrow from scene 6 points from blue to teal. On "Up is positive" the vertical givens write one by one, each with a short pointer to the stage (the 1.2 m scale lights). `AlignedAlgebra` rows: "−1.2 = 0 × t + ½(−9.8)t²"; on "squared" "−1.2 = −4.9t²". ### 8. worked example: from time to distance
> Now carry that time across to the horizontal column. s x equals u x times t, two metres per second times zero point four nine five seconds, which is about zero point nine nine **metres**. Here's a surprise. Launch B at four metres per second instead. Its fall time doesn't change at all, so it simply lands twice as far **out**. The vertical motion sets the time, and the horizontal motion turns that time into a **distance**.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 12; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "Two small balls, A and B, start 1.25 m above level ground at the same instant. A is dropped from rest. B is launched horizontally at 3.0 m s⁻¹. Neglect air resistance and take g = 9.8 m s⁻². (a) Calculate the time each ball takes to reach the ground. (b) Calculate the horizontal distance from the point directly below B's start to where B lands."
Model answer: (a) Up positive. Both have u_y = 0, a_y = −9.8 m s⁻², s_y = −1.25 m. s_y = u_y t + ½a_y t² gives −1.25 = −4.9t², t = √(1.25/4.9) = 0.505 s ≈ 0.51 s for each ball (B's horizontal motion does not affect its vertical motion). (b) s_x = u_x t = 3.0 × 0.505 = 1.52 m ≈ 1.5 m. Classic mistake (crossed): "B takes longer because it travels further."

**Attempt 2 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks).** "A ball is launched horizontally from the top of a cliff. Near the end of its flight the ball is moving steeply downwards. A student claims that the ball's horizontal velocity must have decreased during the flight. With reference to the forces acting on the ball, explain whether the student's claim is correct. Assume air resistance is negligible."
Model answer: The claim is incorrect. With air resistance negligible, the only force on the ball is its weight, which acts vertically downwards and has no horizontal component. The net horizontal force is zero, so the horizontal acceleration is zero (Newton's second law) and the horizontal velocity remains at its launch value (first law). The path becomes steeper because the vertical component of velocity increases under gravity, so the resultant velocity turns downwards; the horizontal component is unchanged. Classic mistake (crossed): "gravity slows it sideways".

Two identical balls leave a bench at the same instant. One is dropped, the other is knocked sideways at 2.0 m/s. Which lands first? See why they land together, why a projectile's horizontal velocity stays constant while its vertical velocity grows, and how one shared clock links the two motions. Then use it to find where a horizontally launched ball lands. Two exam-style questions at the end.

Year 12 Physics (2017 syllabus), Module 5: Advanced Mechanics.
Syllabus content (NSW Physics Stage 6, 2017):
"analyse the motion of projectiles by resolving the motion into horizontal and vertical components, making the following assumptions: a constant vertical acceleration due to gravity; zero air resistance"
