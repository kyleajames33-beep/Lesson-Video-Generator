## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "How can measured data test the separate effects of net force and mass?" The answer, built in order: the pull on the trolley is only the net force once friction is cancelled, and a nudged trolley moving at constant velocity shows it is, to the sensitivity of that check (3); one variable changes per series, so each effect is tested on its own (4); repeats give a mean and an uncertainty (5); a straight line through the origin that passes through every scatter bar is consistent with proportionality, and its gradient gives a physical quantity to compare with an independent measurement (6, 7); conclusions are conditional: they hold within the repeat scatter, the instrument limits and the leftover-force bound that were checked, over the range tested, and the friction fix is the main limitation (8).
- **The hook (owner: a useful question).** "If your points miss the line by a little, does that disprove the law?" Four illustrative points near, not on, a line through the origin. Answered in scene 6: "Judge the line by the bars, not the dots."
- **Method (pack method adopted, with one change).** Low-friction dynamics trolley on a track; a string from a force sensor on the trolley passes over a bench pulley to a hanging mass; a motion sensor at the far end records velocity against time; acceleration is the slope of the velocity-time best-fit line (v08). The pack's "correct for measured friction" is done by **friction compensation** (tilt the track until a nudged trolley rolls at constant velocity), which uses v13 as the check and needs no subtraction. Mass measured on a top-pan electronic balance (trolley plus sensor plus carried masses).
- **Why the force sensor (author note c).** The hanging mass also accelerates, so its weight is not the string's pull; measuring the pull on the trolley directly avoids that. Not explained to learners beyond "a force sensor on the trolley measures that pull", which is all the analysis needs.
- **Series and data (pack illustrative data, unchanged means).** Series A: total trolley mass 1.00 kg, net force 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80 N (±0.02 N), mean accelerations 0.21, 0.39, 0.61, 0.79 m s⁻² (±0.05 m s⁻²). Series B: net force 0.60 N, mass 1.00, 1.50, 2.00 kg (±0.01 kg), mean accelerations 0.61, 0.40, 0.29 m s⁻². Repeats (new, consistent with the pack's means and uncertainty): one table shown, at 0.40 N: 0.34, 0.39, 0.44 (mean 0.39, range 0.10, ±0.05); others in author note a. Series B's 1.00 kg point is the same setting as series A's 0.60 N point and has the same repeats, a consistency the student can spot.
- **Graphs (owner quality bar: cause before rule).** Series A plotted as acceleration (vertical) against net force (horizontal), because the independent variable goes along the bottom; the site's Q3 and the variable-matching task use this orientation (the pack's F_net against a, gradient = mass, is not used: it puts the dependent variable on the horizontal axis). Gradient 1.0 kg⁻¹ = 1/m, giving 1.0 kg against the balance's 1.00 kg; steepest and shallowest lines through the origin that pass through every bar give 1.08 and 0.90 kg⁻¹, so masses from about 0.93 to 1.1 kg. The route's stop for v15 requires analysing repeated data, and the brief requires uncertainty to be explicit, so this video teaches the minimum, with the honest scope the review required (Revision 2): half the range of three repeats is a first estimate of the repeat scatter only (not a bound on the true value and not the whole uncertainty); the bars are called scatter bars; the sensor's stated resolution (force ±0.02 N, mass ±0.01 kg) is carried into the steepest and shallowest lines; timing and fit scatter are inside the repeats because each acceleration is a fitted slope; leftover force is bounded by the no-pull check at each load; and conclusions are conditional on these limits. ### 1. hook (how close is close enough?)
> In the force and mass video, every number was perfect. Double the net force, double the acceleration. Real measurements never land exactly on a line, though. If your points miss the line by a little, does that disprove Newton's second law, or not? Let's find out how close is close **enough**.

### 3. cancelling friction (a quick prediction)
> But the track has a little friction, so the string's pull isn't the net force yet. The fix is to tilt the track slightly, so a small part of gravity cancels the friction. How will you know when the tilt is right? **Predict**. Give the trolley a gentle nudge with nothing attached. If it rolls at a constant velocity, the net force on it is close to zero, as far as this check can tell, so friction is cancelled. That's Newton's first law, used as a check. This run is the experimental control. It shows that with no pull, there's no acceleration. Now the sensor's reading is, to that accuracy, the net **force**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why compensate friction: otherwise the pull is not the net force (3). Why the nudge test works: a constant velocity means a net force close to zero, as far as the check can tell (3, v13). Why one variable per series: changing both hides the effect (4, v14's A2). Why repeat: one run can't show the spread (5). Why half the range: it is a first estimate of the repeat scatter, judged from the spread, not the whole uncertainty (5). Why net force goes along the bottom: it is the variable chosen (6). Why judge by bars: the dots scatter by about the bar size, so a line through every bar is consistent with the data (6), with the other limits bounded separately (8). Why plot 1/m: a curve can't be tested by eye; the axis change straightens it, as v10's t² did (7). Why the conclusion is limited and conditional: untested range; systematic error from the tilt, bounded by the no-pull check at each load (8). Why each risk control (9).
- **Worked-example rule:** each gradient has its task (the read point), the rise-run triangle on the graph and one row per cue, with the independent comparison (balance, sensor) as a gold tag beside its result; one series per scene.
- **Response opportunities:** one 3 s prediction (3, answered by reasoning from v13, not reported as an observation); two unseen HSC-style questions with every needed value and condition on the card (A1: axes stated, the three gradients, the comparison value, three demands; A2: the fixed force, the table, two demands), 10 s each with "Pause here to answer.", no graph on either card. One crossed mistake each; feedback about 28 s and 31 s.
- **Avoided double teaching:** variables, mean, range and systematic error are recalled with their v10 meanings in a clause each; v14's law is the prediction, not re-derived; v08's slope is a grey chip.
- **Syllabus coverage:** `cic1d80483`: conducts the investigation (2 to 5), illustrates the relationship between force, mass and acceleration with two fair-test series (6, 7) and evaluates it (8). FA0 parts as in the placement table.

## Safety facts check (self-check, 2 Nov; Codex to confirm)

- **Runaway trolley.** A school dynamics trolley (about 0.5 to 2 kg) moving at under 1 m/s can still bruise a foot if it rolls off a bench-height track, and the sensor on it can be damaged. Controls: a buffer or end stop fixed to the track; keep feet clear of the bench end; release only when the path is clear.
- **Falling hanging mass.** Slotted masses of tens to a few hundred grams fall when the trolley reaches the end stop or the string slips. Controls: keep the drop short (the mass reaches the floor or a sand tray within a short distance); sand tray or foam pad underneath; feet clear.
- **Set-up (teacher note in the footer).** Clamp the pulley and track firmly so neither can be pulled off the bench; route sensor leads so they can't be caught by the trolley. Codex to confirm against current NSW Department of Education science safety guidance for dynamics trolley investigations; record sources.
