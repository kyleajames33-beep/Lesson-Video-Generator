### 2. sorting the category
> First, stop treating it as one thing. **Complementary healthcare** means products and services used alongside normal medical care. If they're used instead of it, that's **alternative** care, and the risks are completely different. Sort the category into five groups. Things you swallow: vitamins, herbal products, sports supplements. Practitioner services, like naturopathy, massage, acupuncture and chiropractic. Mind-body practices, like yoga, tai chi and meditation. Health apps and websites. And gadgets sold with health claims. The **evidence base**, how much research backs each one, runs from good to almost none. And in Australia, an approved product has been checked for safety and that it contains what it says, not that it works.

### 3. as prevention: the strongest case
> Prevention is where it's used most, and where the case is strongest, because prevention is about habits you keep up. The evidence is reasonable for mind-body practices easing stress and long-term pain, and for tai chi and yoga improving balance and strength, which helps prevent falls in older people. Supplements are weaker. They correct a shortfall, but they don't add benefit once you have enough. And often the real ingredient is time. A practitioner who listens for an hour and follows up is giving attention, continuity and support to change, which genuinely work, and which a short appointment struggles to give. Two risks. Money spent on things that don't work isn't spent on things that do. And a product can push out something proven, like an immunity supplement taken instead of a vaccine.

### 4. treating the issue: the risk is the delay
> Using it to treat a health issue gives the best evidence for some things and the biggest harm for others. What decides it isn't the therapy. It's whether it's used instead of effective treatment, or alongside it. The claims that hold up are narrow, like some manual therapies for some back pain, or mindfulness for chronic pain. Broad claims, to detox, boost immunity or cure serious disease, aren't supported. A treatment that works for everything works for nothing in particular. And the serious harm is rarely the therapy itself. It's the delay. Choosing an alternative for a serious, treatable illness uses up the window when treatment works. Cancer is the best-studied case, and delayed treatment there means worse survival.

### 5a. as a supplement: the danger is the secret
> Most often, it's used alongside medical treatment. Think of someone having chemotherapy who uses acupuncture for nausea, or someone with back pain using medicine, physio and yoga together. It can help, because medical treatment often targets the disease, while anxiety, pain and sleep get less attention, and feeling in control helps people stick with treatment. The real issue is telling. Many people don't tell their doctor, often because they expect to be dismissed. That matters, because of **interactions**, where one substance changes how another works. Some herbal products strengthen blood thinners, change how the liver handles drugs, or raise bleeding risk in surgery. The danger isn't the product. It's the secret.

### 6. the use grid: judge the use, not the label
> So put it on a grid with two questions. Is it used alongside medical care, or instead of it? And has the person told the people treating them? Alongside and told is the safest. Alongside but not told is the common risk, interactions. Instead of, but told, at least means someone knows what's being given up. Instead of and not told is the most dangerous, because nobody knows to step in. All of this assumes the problem needs treatment. For a mild one that clears up on its own, instead of matters much less. To explain this well, judge specific uses, not the whole category. Take people's reasons seriously: rushed care, wanting control, culture and belief. Be precise about the risks: delay, interactions and cost. And notice what popularity says about the system: people want time and attention, and they'll pay for it.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing. No figures.
- **Regulation (2).** TGA listed (AUST L) medicines: assessed for safety and quality, efficacy claims not individually evaluated before listing; registered medicines fully assessed. Chiropractic, osteopathy and Chinese medicine (including acupuncture) practitioners are nationally registered; naturopaths and many massage therapists are not.
- **Prevention evidence (3).** Mind-body interventions for stress, anxiety and chronic pain (moderate evidence); tai chi and balance exercise reduce falls in older people (Cochrane); digital interventions for physical activity, sleep and smoking cessation (modest effects when well designed); routine multivitamin supplementation in well-nourished adults shows little benefit (US Preventive Services Task Force).
- **Treatment evidence (4).** Spinal manipulation for some low back pain (small effects); mindfulness-based approaches for chronic pain and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for depressive relapse; alternative-medicine-only choice for curable cancers associated with worse survival (Johnson and colleagues, 2018); hepatotoxic herbs, adulterated products.
- **Interactions (5).** St John's wort induces liver enzymes (CYP3A4); ginkgo, fish oil and others with anticoagulants raise bleeding risk; non-disclosure common in surveys of complementary medicine users.
- **Blood pressure (8d).** Untreated hypertension raises stroke and heart attack risk.
