Main question: *A flask starts with only reactant. How can it end up as a mixture that never changes again?*
Entry knowledge (C1): reversible reactions run both ways, dynamic equilibrium means equal non-zero rates in a closed system, equal rates do not mean equal amounts. Also: rate means how much changes per second, and more particles available means more events per second.
Stops after: explaining how forward and reverse rates change from a reactant-only start until they are equal, so the concentrations stop changing; and reading both graph types.
Handoff: C3, what a flask already at equilibrium does when it is disturbed. K, expressions, catalysts and collision investigation are not taught here.

### 1. hook (flask with only reactant)
> Here is a sealed glass flask, heated to about two hundred and fifty degrees Celsius and held there, hot enough that everything inside stays a gas. We fill it with just one gas, phosphorus pentachloride, written P C L five, and nothing else. This reaction is reversible. A P C L five molecule can **split** into phosphorus trichloride, P C L three, and chlorine, and those two can **join** back up into P C L five. But right now the flask holds only P C L five, so there is nothing yet to run backwards. So how does a flask that starts with only reactant end up as a mixture that stays exactly the same? To find out, we'll follow it from the moment it's **sealed**.

Visual: painted hot flask (if delivered, else coded flask) with the stopper pressed in on "sealed". On "split" the equation chip PCl5(g) ⇌ PCl3(g) + Cl2(g) writes on with the forward arrow lit blue; on "join" the reverse arrow lights amber. Heading "Starting with only reactant" writes on at the start. A small tag "sealed, about 250 °C, all gas" appears with the flask. A small stopwatch icon starts at 0 on "sealed".

### 2. start (stage 0)
> At the very beginning, the flask holds 0.20 moles of P C L five in every litre. That is its **concentration**, and there is no P C L three or chlorine at all. In the equation, the reaction going left to right, here splitting, is the **forward** reaction. The one going right to left, joining, is the **reverse** reaction. Every P C L five molecule is a candidate to split, and some do every second, so the forward reaction can begin straight away. On a made-up scale, where bigger means more splits each second, the forward rate starts at **fifty**. The reverse reaction can't start yet. To rejoin, a P C L three molecule has to meet a chlorine molecule, and neither exists. So the reverse rate is exactly **zero**.

On "forward" the blue arrow in the equation pulses with the word "forward" beside it; on "reverse" the amber arrow pulses likewise. Right side: two rate bars labelled "rate (made-up units)". ### 3. forward falls, reverse starts (stage 1)
> A little later, some of the P C L five has split. The flask now holds 0.16 moles per litre of P C L five, and 0.04 moles per litre of each product. The forward rate has dropped from fifty to **forty**. Each P C L five molecule has the same chance of splitting in any one second, so with fewer molecules in the flask, fewer split. Meanwhile, the reverse reaction has **started**. P C L three and chlorine now exist, so now and then one of each **meets** and joins back into P C L five. That's a reverse rate of **eight**. But **forward is still bigger than reverse**, so more P C L five splits each second than is rebuilt, and its concentration **keeps falling**, but more slowly.

### 4a. closing in (stage 2)
> Later again, P C L five is down to 0.14 moles per litre, and each product is up to 0.06. The forward rate has slipped to **thirty-five**, because there is even less to split. The reverse rate has climbed to **seventeen**, because with more P C L three and chlorine in the same space, they find each other more often. The **gap** between the two rates is shrinking. While the forward rate is bigger, P C L five is lost faster than it is made, so it is still falling, just more slowly again.

Visual: flask shows 14 navy, 6 violet, 6 green. Rate bars move to 35 and 17. On "gap" a pen bracket spans the difference between the bar tops and the net arrow shrinks to 18. ### 4b. equal (stage 3, with colour evidence)
> Then comes the moment they are **equal**. At 0.12 moles per litre of P C L five and 0.08 of each product, both rates are **thirty**. Now P C L five is made as fast as it is lost, so its concentration stops changing. That is why the P C L five never runs out. Both reactions are still running at thirty, and their effects cancel. That is the dynamic equilibrium from last time. You can even see it. Chlorine is a pale yellow-green gas, while P C L five vapour is pale and nearly colourless, so the flask slowly turns **yellow-green**, then stops getting any darker, because the chlorine concentration has stopped changing.

### 5. graphs (both views of the same journey)
> Here is the whole journey as graphs. First, the concentrations. P C L five **falls**, then flattens. P C L three and chlorine rise together, and flatten too, because they are made in equal numbers. Notice they flatten at 0.08 moles per litre, below the P C L five at 0.12. The concentrations don't have to match. Now the rates. The forward rate starts high and **falls**. The reverse rate starts at zero and **rises**. Where the two lines meet, the concentrations stop changing, and from there both rates stay **level**. Check the height of that flat line: it is thirty, not zero. A common slip is to say the reaction stops at equilibrium. Neither rate drops to zero.

### 6. towns and aid

### 8. feedback
> At the start, the **forward** rate is zero, because there is no P C L five yet to split. Only the reverse reaction can happen: P C L three and chlorine meet and join. So P C L five **rises** from zero to 0.12 moles per litre, while P C L three and chlorine fall from 0.20 to 0.08, the **same mixture** as before. That happens because the total phosphorus and chlorine, the flask size and the temperature are the same. Here is how the rates change. The reverse rate **falls**, because those two are being used up, and the forward rate rises, because P C L five is building up. So the aid still holds: the reverse rate was the faster one, and it fell. And yes, both reactions are **still going**. If you said the reverse rate is zero, check what is in this flask: there is no P C L five, so the forward rate is zero. If you said forward falls and reverse rises, check which side is full: the rate with more to work on starts high. If you said the reaction stops, remember the concentrations stop changing, but the rates stay equal and above zero.

### 9. memory and handoff
> So whenever a question asks how a reaction reaches equilibrium, tell the story in order. Start from one side only: that side's rate is at its highest and the other rate is zero. As that side is used up, its rate falls. As the other side builds up, its rate rises. When the two rates are equal, the concentrations stop changing, but neither rate is zero. Next, we'll take a flask that is already at equilibrium and **disturb** it, to see what it does about it.

Visual: five story steps write on one by one with small rate-bar icons (start, falls, rises, equal, steady). The aid is not repeated here (spoken once in scene 6, shown again in scene 10). At "disturb" the final steady-state flask shakes slightly and a "Next: what a flask does when disturbed" chip appears. If the voiced preview feels long, trim this scene to the decision rule.

### 10. key notes (quiet)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- If a reversible reaction starts with only reactants, the forward rate starts at its highest and the reverse rate starts at zero.
- As reactants are used up, the forward rate falls. As products build up, the reverse rate rises.
- In a closed system at steady temperature, the rates become equal and not zero, so the concentrations stop changing. This is dynamic equilibrium.
- Equal rates do not mean equal amounts. The reactions have not stopped.
- Starting from only products, the reverse rate starts highest and the forward rate starts at zero. The rates still become equal.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen, shown again here after its single spoken use in scene 6): *The faster rate falls, the slower rate rises, they meet and stay level.*

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Forward rate falls because each molecule has the same chance of splitting and fewer are left; reverse rate rises because more products make meeting more frequent; the concentration of each substance is the net result of the two rates (stages 1 and 2, the equal moment, the net arrow); equal rates give steady concentrations. Nothing is only named.
- **Misconceptions addressed:** "the reaction stops at equilibrium" (graph scene, feedback), "equilibrium means equal amounts" (unequal final mixture, aid callback), "a reaction that only has reactants has no reverse reaction" (hook, stage 0), "forward rate falls to zero" (graph reading), "the reactant should run out" (net-change link, 4b), "the reverse rate is zero again" and "forward always falls" (named in feedback).
- **Worked-example rule:** one stage at a time. The table keeps the last two rows only; each number appears on its spoken word with its units (made-up rate units, mol/L for concentrations); scene 4 is split so the equal moment is its own stage.
- **Response opportunity:** unseen case (products only start), all question demands and conditions (same size, same temperature, sealed) visible during the hold of about 35 seconds, nothing answer-bearing until the hold ends.
- **Avoided double teaching:** C1's closed-system and static/dynamic rules are used, not retaught (a one-line callback "the dynamic equilibrium from last time"; the conditions "sealed, steady temperature" stated once in the hook and once with the aid).
- **Out of scope by design:** K or equilibrium expressions, Le Chatelier, catalysts, activation energy, enthalpy or entropy values, quantitative rate laws, random fluctuation of rates.

## Memory aid check

"The faster rate falls, the slower rate rises, they meet and stay level" assumptions: closed system (sealed flask), steady temperature, a simple one-step reversible reaction, and a start away from equilibrium on either side. "Faster" means faster at the start. It works for the reactant-rich start (forward falls, reverse rises) and for the products-only attempt (reverse falls, forward rises), and the scene says so aloud, with the swap diagram, before the attempt. It fails (is not true) when: the flask starts already at equilibrium (neither rate changes), or when temperature or concentrations are changed after equilibrium (c03/c04). The words "stay level" mean the rates, not the concentrations of each substance (those are level too, but at different heights). "Equal rates, not equal amounts" is from the aid bank (Static and dynamic equilibrium); its science holds because the final PCl5 and products differ (0.12 and 0.08). For the bank's "Reading rate-time graphs" shortcut: not used here (disturbances are c03/c04).
