- **Owner brief served:** "Students know the content but don't know how to write the answer." Here the content is two inheritance patterns, two acids and two chromosome terms that students have already met. The skill is the shape: the same feature on both sides, in one sentence.
- **Frequency data used (`research/exam-skills/verb-frequency.md`, Section II written responses in the 10 NESA Chemistry and Biology papers, 2021 to 2025):**

The narration scopes it: "In the last five years of Chemistry and Biology written questions ... Contrast and distinguish didn't, but they're still glossary words, so know them." The screen carries the counting tag.
  - Author note, not learner copy: the 2019 Chemistry paper (outside the pack's range) has a compare question (Q30, enthalpy and entropy for two magnesium salts), so Chemistry does use the verb. It is not quoted or used.
  - **Spoken:** "compare questions were worth three to six marks" (hook) and "compare appeared seven times" (scene 2). **On screen:** "compare: 7 question parts, all Biology, 3 to 6 marks" with the grey tag "NESA glossary key words only; HSC Chemistry and Biology papers, Section II, 2021 to 2025, counted by question part".

The hook's "two separate descriptions" wording is the pack's own plain-terms label.

The video's tip, "give at least one similarity and one difference, unless the question narrows it", is HSCScience advice, labelled as a tip in key note 1. It rests on a pattern in the guidelines, stated as a pattern:

Only the form is described, so no NESA wording or topic is reproduced. Spoken in scene 9 as "In a table question, comparing all the features correctly reached three marks, and comparing most of them reached two." The topic is not named.

  - **Stimulus:** two crosses in one small table. Snapdragons: red-flowered x white-flowered, all offspring pink. Cattle: red x white, all calves roan (red hairs and white hairs mixed). Rows: "offspring genotype", "offspring against parents", "how the two alleles show". It is a planning step, not answer copy; the marks come from the sentences. ### 2. three-verbs
> Here's what NESA's glossary says, in short. **Compare** means show similarities or differences. **Contrast** means show differences, or opposites. **Distinguish** means show how things differ. So contrast and distinguish want differences. Compare can take both, and the safe habit is to give at least **one similarity and one difference**, unless the question narrows it. In the last five years of Chemistry and Biology written questions, compare appeared seven times. Contrast and distinguish didn't, but they're still glossary words, so know them.

### 4. read-it
> Run the routine first. The verb is **compare**, so you need what's the same and what differs, about the same feature. Three marks suggests about **three paired points**. Underline the givens. **True-breeding** tells you each parent has two identical alleles. The parents differ, so every offspring gets one of each: heterozygous. **Pink**, and **red and white hairs**, are the two results you'll set side by side. There's no constraint phrase, so any correct inheritance reasoning counts.

### 5. pair-table
> Here's the trick. Before you write, sketch a tiny table: one row per **feature**, one column for each case. Row one, the offspring's genotype: both crosses give heterozygotes. Row two, the offspring against their parents: in both, they look different from **both parents**. Row three, how the two alleles show. In snapdragons, the colour is **in between**. In cattle, **both** colours show, hair by hair. Now every row lines up one feature across both cases.

### 6. table-to-sentence
> Now turn each row into one sentence, with a comparison word. Row one: **in both** crosses, the offspring are heterozygous, because each true-breeding parent gives a different allele. Row two: **both** heterozygotes differ from both parents, so neither allele is dominant. Row three: snapdragons show incomplete dominance, an in-between pink, **whereas** cattle show co-dominance, with both colours fully expressed in separate hairs. Three paired sentences: in our marking guide, three marks.

### 7. comparison-words
> These little words do the comparing for you. For similarities: **both**, in both, similarly. For differences: **whereas**, while, but, unlike, and words ending in er, like larger or slower. But a comparison word can't rescue a mismatched pair. "Snapdragons are pink, whereas cattle have hair" uses whereas, but it compares **colour with hair**. Same feature, both sides, one sentence.

### 8. weak-vs-strong
> Now here's a weaker answer. "Snapdragons show incomplete dominance, and their offspring are pink. Cattle show co-dominance, and the calves are roan, with red and white hairs." Every word is right. But it's **two descriptions**. There's no similarity at all, and the difference is left for the marker to find. In our marking guide it earns **one** of three, for the correct pattern in each case. The fix isn't more science. It's **pairing**.

### 9. what-guidelines-show
> NESA's marking guidelines point the same way. In one recent three-mark compare question, the sample answer was only **two sentences**: a similarity, and a difference, each about the same feature of both things. In a table question, comparing **all** the features correctly reached three marks, and comparing most of them reached two. So the marks followed **complete pairing** of the features asked about, not longer descriptions. The criteria are set question by question, but pairing turns up again and again.

### 15. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, hold about 50 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Compare: show similarities or differences. Contrast: show differences or opposites. Distinguish: show how things differ (NESA glossary, paraphrased). Tip: for compare, give at least one similarity and one difference unless the question narrows it.
2. Write in pairs: the same feature for both cases, in one sentence. Two separate descriptions do not compare.
3. Table-to-sentence: one row per feature, one column per case, then one sentence per row.
4. Comparison words: both, in both, similarly; whereas, while, but, unlike, larger than.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Same feature, both sides, one sentence.*

## Questions used and model answers

All questions are written for HSCScience. None is copied from or adapted from a specific NESA question; the comparison lists are in Decisions. Mark tallies follow the HSCScience illustrative guide.

**Worked question (Biology, compare, 3 marks).** "Two crosses were carried out. All the parents were true-breeding. [Table] snapdragon: red flowers x white flowers, all pink flowers; cattle: red coat x white coat, all roan (a mixture of red hairs and white hairs). Compare the inheritance of flower colour in the snapdragons with the inheritance of coat colour in the cattle. (3 marks)"
- Annotated reading (scene 4): verb "Compare" (highlighter); "(3 marks)" (badge: about three paired points); givens "true-breeding", "all pink flowers", "a mixture of red hairs and white hairs" (underlines); no constraint phrase.
- Pair table (scene 5): offspring genotype (heterozygous | heterozygous); offspring against parents (different from both parents | different from both parents); how the alleles show (in between, pink | both colours, separate hairs).
- Model answer (3 paired points):
  1. In both crosses the offspring are heterozygous, because each true-breeding parent passes on a different allele.
  2. Both heterozygotes have a phenotype unlike either parent, so in both cases neither allele is dominant.
  3. Snapdragons show incomplete dominance (an intermediate pink phenotype), whereas cattle show co-dominance (both alleles fully expressed: red hairs and white hairs).
  - Also accepted (author note): "If the offspring were crossed, both would give a 1 : 2 : 1 ratio, because the heterozygote looks different from both homozygotes." "In snapdragons one working allele makes less pigment in every cell, whereas in roan cattle each hair shows one allele's colour."
- Weak answer shown: "Snapdragons show incomplete dominance, and their offspring are pink. Cattle show co-dominance, and the calves are roan, with red and white hairs." Illustrative 1 of 3, for the correct pattern in each case. It loses 2: no similarity, and the difference is never stated as a pair.

**Drill 1 (Chemistry, compare, using the data, in terms of acid strength, 3 marks).** "Two acid solutions, each with a concentration of 0.10 mol L-1, were tested at 25 °C. [Table] Electrical conductivity: HCl high, CH3COOH low. Reaction with magnesium ribbon: HCl rapid bubbling, CH3COOH slow bubbling. Volume of 0.10 mol L-1 NaOH(aq) needed to neutralise 20.0 mL of the acid: 20.0 mL for each. Using the data, compare the two acid solutions in terms of acid strength. (3 marks)"
- Model answer:
  1. Similarity: both need 20.0 mL of NaOH, because both are monoprotic acids at the same concentration, so they contain the same number of moles of acid. As OH- removes H3O+, the weak acid's equilibrium keeps shifting to ionise more until all of it has reacted.
  2. Difference: HCl is a strong acid that ionises completely, whereas CH3COOH is a weak acid that ionises only partially (about 1% at this concentration), so [H3O+] is much higher in the HCl solution.
  3. Difference (linked to the data): the HCl solution therefore has more ions and more H3O+, so it has higher conductivity and reacts faster with magnesium, whereas the CH3COOH solution has low conductivity and bubbles slowly.
- Crossed habit: "HCl is a strong acid that reacts quickly. Ethanoic acid is a weak acid that reacts slowly." (two descriptions; no similarity; the equal titre is unused).

**Drill 2 (Biology, distinguish, 2 marks).** "The diagram shows one pair of chromosomes in a body cell after DNA replication. [Diagram: a duplicated homologous pair, with brackets labelled 'homologous chromosomes' and 'sister chromatids'.] Distinguish between homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids. (2 marks)"
- Model answer:
  1. Homologous chromosomes are a pair, one inherited from each parent, whereas sister chromatids are two identical copies of one chromosome, made by DNA replication and joined at the centromere.
  2. Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes at the same loci but may carry different alleles, whereas sister chromatids carry the same alleles.
  - Also accepted (author note): homologues separate in meiosis I, whereas sister chromatids separate in mitosis and meiosis II.
- Crossed habit: "Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes. Sister chromatids are copies of a chromosome." (two definitions; no feature in which they differ).

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** In the content: why both crosses give heterozygotes (true-breeding parents each give a different allele), why neither allele is dominant (the heterozygote looks like neither parent), why the two acids need the same volume of base (same moles; the weak acid keeps ionising), why HCl conducts and bubbles faster (complete ionisation, more H3O+), why homologues can differ in alleles (one from each parent) and sister chromatids do not (copies made by replication). In the skill: why two descriptions lose marks (the marker has to do the comparing), why a comparison word cannot rescue unmatched features, and why marks follow complete pairing of the requested features rather than length.
- **Science (to check):**
  - Snapdragon red x white gives all pink (incomplete dominance); red x white cattle give all roan (co-dominance); both F1 heterozygous (B10).
  - 0.10 mol L-1 CH3COOH, Ka 1.8 x 10^-5: [H3O+] about 1.3 x 10^-3 mol L-1, about 1.3% ionised (C05 author note a). The card gives no pH. "About 1%" is the spoken rounding; the screen says "about 1%".
  - Equal titres: 20.0 mL of 0.10 mol L-1 HCl or CH3COOH needs 2.0 x 10^-3 mol NaOH, 20.0 mL of 0.10 mol L-1 NaOH, 1 : 1 for both.

  - Sister chromatids carry the same alleles; crossing over in prophase I can exchange segments between non-sister chromatids, after which sister chromatids may differ.
