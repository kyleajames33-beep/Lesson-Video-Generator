- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Toxins: cycasin is methylazoxymethanol (MAM) beta-D-glucoside; macrozamin is MAM beta-primeveroside (the same MAM part on a two-sugar unit). Both are water-soluble azoxyglycosides. Cycasin water solubility is listed at about 57 g/L (Wikipedia, PubChem-derived; not shown on screen). Ladd et al. 1993, cited in Asmussen 2011, measured macrozamin in Macrozamia and Bowenia kernels and the *M. riedlei* sarcotesta (up to about 5 percent of fresh weight in Bowenia). Cycads also contain BMAA, a water-soluble non-protein amino acid; not mentioned (keeps the video on the two named glycosides). (b) Starch: starch granules do not dissolve in cold water (amylose and amylopectin chains are packed tightly in semi-crystalline granules held by many hydrogen bonds between chains; heating in water gelatinises them). The bag's weave also retains the solid pieces; the narration's reason is the solubility one, which is the chemistry point. (c) The equilibrium model: treat it as a distribution of dissolved toxin between the water inside the seed pieces and the surrounding water, toxin(seed) ⇌ toxin(water), with K roughly the ratio of the two concentrations. In a small fixed volume the net transfer stops when that ratio is reached, with toxin still in the seed; the surrounding water is nowhere near saturated, so the site's "saturates" is wrong. A large volume reaches the same ratio with far more toxin moved out, which is why still pools and waterholes also work (Asmussen 2011 records "a stream or waterhole" and "still or running water"; Beck records still-water storage for up to five months). Running water keeps the outside concentration near zero, so Q stays below K and net movement continues; the net rate falls as the seed's toxin runs down, so there is no constant "maximum rate". The video says "Q below K" with no number. (d) Hydrolysis: the Australian Curriculum teacher background says water slowly hydrolyses cycasin into glucose and MAM, and that the water-soluble MAM is then removed; North Queensland rainforest Aboriginal peoples sped this up with more surface contact and higher temperature. A beta-glucosidase in the seed (and in the gut) also catalyses it. Not in learner copy; the narration avoids saying the toxin is only removed unchanged. (e) Crushing: Nungalinya ("cracked kernels"), Wreck Bay account (no detail), Asmussen (pounded or crushed kernels in most prolonged-leaching accounts). Surface area and diffusion path: standard rate reasoning, Year 11. (f) Other methods, from Asmussen 2011 and Beck 1992: brief leaching (roasted and cracked, then about a day in still or running water), prolonged leaching (more than a day, often several days, still or running water), very prolonged leaching (30 to 150 days, Bloomfield River and Lower Tully, Queensland), roasting then extended leaching (Wide Bay and Newcastle accounts, including Gaiarbau, a Dungidau elder of Kilcoy), ageing (seeds left or buried for months; Cycas ageing restricted to the Northern Territory in the reviewed accounts; at Donydji, northeast Arnhem Land, aged *Cycas angulata* seeds were most regularly eaten), and prolonged leaching then burial of the *M. riedlei* outer flesh in south-west Western Australia (Noongar). Some sources call ageing or burial "fermentation"; Asmussen and Beck use "ageing" and do not give a mechanism, so the video says "aged" and gives no mechanism. Asmussen found the choice of method partly related to water availability (statistically significant for Cycas, not for Macrozamia); the narration says "partly depending on how much water was around". (g) Durations: the Nungalinya account says "several days"; the Wreck Bay quote gives none. No duration is presented as making the food safe. (h) Language words and names: the Nungalinya page attributes its account to Yurranydjil Dhurrkay; the learner copy credits Nungalinya College only, pending the cultural reviewer's advice on naming individuals (some communities avoid naming people who have died). (i) Pronunciation: "cycad" SY-kad; "Cycas" SY-kas; "Macrozamia" mak-roh-ZAY-mee-uh; "cycasin" SY-kuh-sin; "macrozamin" mak-roh-ZAY-min; "Nungalinya" NUNG-uh-LIN-yuh (confirm with the college); "O H groups" as "oh-aitch groups"; "Q" and "K" as letters. (j) The Wreck Bay quote on screen is verbatim from the ANBG page ("The old fellas used to eat them but they would put them in the creek and let the water wash them."); narration paraphrases it as "the old people".

Main question: *How does water take the toxin out of a cycad seed but leave the food behind?*
Entry knowledge (C11, C8 and Year 11): dissolving as a two-way process with equal rates at equilibrium (C11); Q against K, removing a product (C8); hydrogen bonding between OH groups and water, surface area and rate (Year 11).
Stops after: naming cycasin and macrozamin and explaining why they dissolve in water; explaining why starch stays; explaining why crushing speeds leaching; explaining with two-way movement and Q against K why running water removes more toxin than a small unchanged volume of still water; describing the practice as empirical knowledge developed and held by named communities, with methods that vary by place. No preparation method, no safe duration, no hydrolysis mechanism, no Ksp, no ionic dissolving recap.
Handoff: solubility rules and what happens when two ionic solutions are mixed (route chem-m5-c13).

### 1. hook (a food and a poison in one seed)
> Cycad seeds are packed with starch, which makes them a valuable food. They also contain **toxins** strong enough to make people seriously ill. Aboriginal communities in many parts of Australia worked out how to remove those toxins, and that knowledge is still held today. So how can **water** take the poison out of a seed, but leave the food behind?

### 3. why the toxins dissolve and the starch doesn't
> Here's the chemistry. In Cycas seeds the best-known toxin is **cycasin**, and in Macrozamia seeds it's a close relative, **macrozamin**. Both molecules have the same toxic part joined to a **sugar part**, and the sugar part is covered in **O H groups**. Those O H groups form **hydrogen bonds** with water, so both toxins dissolve well in water. Starch is different. It's packed into **solid grains** that don't dissolve in cold water, so the food stays in the bag.

### 4. why crack or crush the kernels
> Why crack or crush the kernels first? A whole kernel only touches water on its outside, and toxin deep inside has a long way to travel. Crushing makes many small pieces, so far **more surface** touches the water, and the toxin has a **shorter path** out. So the toxin leaves **faster**.

Visual: a coded cross-section: on the left one whole kernel (oval) with red toxin dots throughout and a long arrow from the centre to the edge; on the right the same kernel broken into eight small pieces. On "more surface" the outlines of the pieces glow teal against the single outline of the whole kernel (tag "more surface in contact with water"). On "shorter path" short arrows from the centre of each piece to its edge (tag "shorter path out"). On "faster" red dots stream out of the pieces faster than from the whole kernel. Hold about 1 second.

### 5. in still water, the toxin moves both ways
> Now the idea from the dissolving video. Dissolved toxin moves **both ways**: out of the seed into the water, and from the water back into the seed. Put crushed seed into a small amount of still water, and never change it. At first, nearly all the movement is outward. But as the water fills with toxin, more moves back in, until the two rates are **equal**. Toxin is still moving, but there's **no net change**, and toxin is **left in the seed**.

Visual: the ported trough, left panel only, labelled "small amount of water, never changed": crushed seed pieces dotted red. On "both ways" a teal arrow (seed to water, "out") and a violet arrow (water to seed, "back") write with the equation strip "toxin in seed ⇌ toxin in water". Particles leave; as the water's dots increase, violet returns appear. On "equal" a small two-rate meter (C2 style) shows the teal and violet bars meeting (tag "rates equal"). On "no net change" particles only swap places, one out and one back. ### 6. running water keeps the forward direction ahead
> Running water changes that. The stream keeps **carrying dissolved toxin away**, so the water around the seed always holds very little. In equilibrium terms, removing the product keeps **Q below K**, so the forward direction keeps winning, and toxin **keeps leaving** the seed for as long as the water flows.

### 7. one idea, many methods
> Communities used this idea in different ways to suit local conditions. Some **roasted** seeds before leaching them. And in some places, partly depending on how much water was around, seeds were **aged** for months instead. These methods were developed by observing, testing and passing on the results over many generations. That's **empirical knowledge**: built from evidence.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the toxins dissolve: OH groups on the sugar part hydrogen bond with water (3). Why the food stays: starch grains do not dissolve in cold water (3). Why crushing helps: more surface, shorter path (4). Why still water leaves toxin behind: the return rate rises to equal the outward rate (5). Why running water keeps working: removing the dissolved toxin keeps Q below K (6). Why methods differ: local conditions such as water supply, not levels of understanding (7). Why it counts as empirical knowledge: observation, testing and transmission (7).

## Cultural sources and review

Every source used, what it supports, and its limits. Learner copy uses only claims marked "supports".

1. **Nungalinya College, "Cycad" page** (https://www.nungalinya.edu.au/cycad; published 3 April 2024, updated 30 May 2024; fetched 17 Oct 2026). Indigenous-led college in Darwin. Supports: *Cycas armstrongii*; the outer shell is cracked to reveal the kernel; "These kernels are highly poisonous so must be leeched of toxins before consumption"; "The cracked kernels are placed in a woven bag and left in fresh running water for several days"; "This process must only be performed by those with great skill and knowledge in this procedure"; present-tense description (living practice). The bread story is attributed to Yurranydjil Dhurrkay. Limits: names no toxin; the processing passage does not name the community the method belongs to (the page mentions the Wangurri clan in a totem context and a Larrakia plant name; neither is used, and the video says "an account shared through Nungalinya College", not a Nation).
2. **Australian National Botanic Gardens, Aboriginal plant use, *Macrozamia communis*** (https://www.canbr.gov.au/apu/plants/macrcomm.html; the domain is the Australian National Botanic Gardens plant use site; fetched 17 Oct 2026). Supports: the seed "is toxic and must be leached of poison before use"; the Wreck Bay community quote "The old fellas used to eat them but they would put them in the creek and let the water wash them" (Wreck Bay Community and Renwick 2000: 19); "Pounded seeds are washed in running water for some days" (Stewart and Percival 1997); the Dhurga seed name (not used). Limits: secondary compilation; one cited author says there is "no evidence that this treatment destroys or even reduces the poisons", and the page points to Beaton (1982) for evidence that processing lowers toxin levels. The video makes no claim about how much toxin a specific method removes.
3. **Asmussen, B. (2011). "There is likewise a nut...": a comparative ethnobotany of Aboriginal processing methods and consumption of Australian Bowenia, Cycas, Lepidozamia and Macrozamia species.** *Technical Reports of the Australian Museum, Online* 23(10): 147 to 163. DOI 10.3853/j.1835-4211.23.2011.1575 (full text read 17 Oct 2026). Peer-reviewed museum publication. Supports: Beck's classification of Cycas methods (brief leaching, prolonged leaching, ageing); prolonged leaching was the most commonly recorded method for both Cycas and Macrozamia; still or running water; a dilly bag "in a stream or waterhole" (Wide Bay); roasting then extended leaching (including Gaiarbau, a Dungidau elder of Kilcoy); ageing; prolonged leaching then burial of *M. riedlei* flesh in south-west Western Australia (Noongar); methods vary by region, partly with water availability; macrozamin content data (Ladd et al. 1993). Limits: many of the accounts it reviews are colonial-era European records, so they describe practice through outsiders' eyes; the video uses the review only for general method types and regional variation, not for any single community's practice.
4. **Beck, W. (1992). Aboriginal preparation of Cycas seeds in Australia.** *Economic Botany* 46: 133 to 147. DOI 10.1007/BF02930628 (abstract and secondary citations only; full text not read). Supports: fieldwork at Donydji outstation, northeast Arnhem Land, where aged *Cycas angulata* seeds are most regularly eaten; analyses of fresh seeds and seeds prepared at Donydji and in the laboratory indicate cycasin is effectively removed by all the traditional techniques, each with different storage and handling properties; a long history of use. Limits: full text not checked; the video does not quote it, only uses "aged for months" and "many generations".
5. **Australian Curriculum (v9), Science teacher background information AC9S10U07 E6** (https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/support-resources/background-information/science_teacher_background_information_AC9S10U07_E6; fetched 17 Oct 2026). Government curriculum body. Supports: cycasin is glucose bound to methylazoxymethanol; water hydrolyses it slowly, releasing the water-soluble toxic part; rainforest Aboriginal peoples of North Queensland sped this up by increasing contact surface and temperature. Limits: the page itself warns that its consulted works may contain outdated or culturally offensive material; used for author note d only; the Cook story is not used.
6. **Site lesson 15** (`C:/Users/kygs/hscscience/subjects/chemistry/year12/module5/lesson15.sa.html`). Supports the lesson scope, the still-versus-running framing, crushing, the knowledge-system framing and the cultural context box. Limits: unsourced claims corrected as listed under Decisions (65,000 years, testing small amounts, below detectable levels, validation framing, saturation and maximum rate).
7. **Chemistry facts:** cycasin and macrozamin structures and water solubility (Wikipedia "Cycasin", PubChem-derived, and the Cornell veterinary sago palm toxicosis summary found by search; Lythgoe and Riggs 1949 for macrozamin, not re-read); starch granule insolubility in cold water (standard). Limits: secondary; Codex to confirm against a primary or data book source.
