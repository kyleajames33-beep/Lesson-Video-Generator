- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Toxins: cycasin is methylazoxymethanol (MAM) beta-D-glucoside; macrozamin is MAM beta-primeveroside (the same MAM part on a two-sugar unit). Both are water-soluble azoxyglycosides. Cycasin water solubility is listed at about 57 g/L (Wikipedia, PubChem-derived; not shown on screen). Ladd et al. 1993, cited in Asmussen 2011, measured macrozamin in Macrozamia and Bowenia kernels and the *M. riedlei* sarcotesta (up to about 5 percent of fresh weight in Bowenia). Cycads also contain BMAA, a water-soluble non-protein amino acid; not mentioned (keeps the video on the two named glycosides). (b) Starch: starch granules do not dissolve in cold water (amylose and amylopectin chains are packed tightly in semi-crystalline granules held by many hydrogen bonds between chains; heating in water gelatinises them). The bag's weave also retains the solid pieces; the narration's reason is the solubility one, which is the chemistry point. (c) The equilibrium model: treat it as a distribution of dissolved toxin between the water inside the seed pieces and the surrounding water, toxin(seed) ⇌ toxin(water), with K roughly the ratio of the two concentrations. In a small fixed volume the net transfer stops when that ratio is reached, with toxin still in the seed; the surrounding water is nowhere near saturated, so the site's "saturates" is wrong. A large volume reaches the same ratio with far more toxin moved out, which is why still pools and waterholes also work (Asmussen 2011 records "a stream or waterhole" and "still or running water"; Beck records still-water storage for up to five months). Running water keeps the outside concentration near zero, so Q stays below K and net movement continues; the net rate falls as the seed's toxin runs down, so there is no constant "maximum rate". The video says "Q below K" with no number. (d) Hydrolysis: the Australian Curriculum teacher background says water slowly hydrolyses cycasin into glucose and MAM, and that the water-soluble MAM is then removed; the same page notes that more surface contact and higher temperature speed this up. A beta-glucosidase in the seed (and in the gut) also catalyses it. Not in learner copy; the narration avoids saying the toxin is only removed unchanged. (e) Crushing: Asmussen 2011 records pounded, cracked or crushed kernels in most prolonged-leaching accounts. Surface area and diffusion path: standard rate reasoning, Year 11. (f) Other methods, from Asmussen 2011 and Beck 1992, described in general terms only (Revision 2: named places, groups and individuals removed from these notes as well as from learner copy): brief leaching (roasted and cracked, then about a day in still or running water), prolonged leaching (more than a day, often several days, still or running water; the most commonly recorded method), very prolonged leaching (weeks to months), roasting then extended leaching, and ageing (seeds left or buried for months). Some sources call ageing or burial "fermentation"; Asmussen and Beck use "ageing" and do not give a mechanism, so the video says "aged" and gives no mechanism. Asmussen found the choice of method partly related to water availability (statistically significant for Cycas, not for Macrozamia); the narration says "partly depending on how much water was around". (g) Durations: "for days" (scene 2) is the general prolonged-leaching description in Asmussen 2011; "for months" (scene 7) is ageing. No duration is presented as making the food safe. (h) Names and language words: none used (owner decision, 18 Nov). ### 3. why the toxins dissolve and the starch doesn't
> Here's the chemistry. In Cycas seeds the best-known toxin is **cycasin**, and in Macrozamia seeds it's a close relative, **macrozamin**. Both molecules have the same toxic part joined to a **sugar part**, and the sugar part is covered in **O H groups**. Those O H groups form **hydrogen bonds** with water, so both toxins dissolve well in water. Starch is different. Its long chains are packed tightly into **solid grains**, held together by hydrogen bonds between the chains, so the grains don't dissolve in cold water, and the food stays in the bag.

### 4. why crack or crush the kernels
> Why crack or crush the kernels first? A whole kernel only touches water on its outside, and toxin deep inside has a long way to travel. Crushing makes many small pieces, so far **more surface** touches the water, and the toxin has a **shorter path** out. So the toxin leaves **faster**.

On "more surface" the outlines of the pieces glow teal against the single outline of the whole kernel (tag "more surface in contact with water"). On "shorter path" short arrows from the centre of each piece to its edge (tag "shorter path out"). On "faster" red dots stream out of the pieces faster than from the whole kernel. Hold about 1 second.

### 5. in still water, the toxin moves both ways
> Now the idea from the dissolving video. Dissolved toxin moves **both ways**: out of the seed into the water, and from the water back into the seed. Put crushed seed into a small amount of still water, and never change it. At first, nearly all the movement is outward.
