### 2b. q1-plan (verb, levels, baseline)

Varieties are the crop's **genetic** level. Surviving caterpillars show a genetic change in the pest.

### 6. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Plan any biotechnology judgement: one mechanism link (what the technology does), criteria (often given in the question), evidence from the stimulus for each, then a verdict with "to a ... extent" and "provided that ...". Assess: how far, which way. Evaluate: against criteria.
- Biodiversity: name the level (genetic, species, ecosystem) and the baseline in the question. Link the technology to biodiversity, not just to the organism or the harvest. A crop can raise species diversity in fields while narrowing the crop's genetic diversity. Rising pest resistance is a change in allele frequency, not lost diversity, but it can bring sprays back. Do not judge a level the data do not cover.
- Cloning adds genetic diversity only when the donors carry alleles the living population lacks. Count the donors, not the clones. Mitochondrial DNA comes mainly from the egg. Weigh low success and surrogate welfare against alternatives such as habitat protection, which cannot restore missing alleles.
- Ethics: say who carries each risk (animals, patients, other people), whether consent is possible, and which objections are about principle, which evidence cannot settle.
- Early results in a few patients show something can work, not that it is proven.

Memory aid (large): *No verdict without a reason, no reason without evidence.*

## Practice questions and model answers

1. (Q1, assess, 6 marks.) A farming district replaced conventional maize, sprayed with insecticide against caterpillar pests, with an insect-resistant maize that produces a protein that kills caterpillars feeding on it. Data (illustrative), before and ten years after: insecticide sprays per season 6 and 1; insect species recorded in maize fields 17 and 25; maize varieties grown in the district 15 and 4; farms planting refuge strips of non-resistant maize: not applicable, 3 in 10; caterpillars surviving on resistant maize in laboratory tests: not applicable, none until year 7 and rising since year 8. Assess the effect of this change on biodiversity in the district.
   Model: The change increased species diversity but reduced the crop's genetic diversity, so its effect on biodiversity is mixed. Species level: compared with sprayed conventional maize, sprays fell from 6 to 1 per season, so fewer non-target insects were killed, and the number of insect species recorded in maize fields rose from 17 to 25. Genetic level (crop): the number of maize varieties fell from 15 to 4, narrowing the crop's gene pool, so a new disease or pest could affect most of the district's crop. Genetic change (pest): more caterpillars now survive on the resistant maize, showing that resistance alleles are becoming more common in the pest population by natural selection; this is a change in allele frequency, not a loss of genetic diversity, but it threatens the species-level gain. Because only 3 in 10 farms plant refuges, resistance may continue to spread; if the maize stops controlling the pest, spraying could return and the species-level gain could be lost. The data give no information on ecosystem diversity, so no judgement is made at that level. Overall, the effect is mixed: species diversity has increased to a moderate extent, but crop genetic diversity has decreased. The species gain will last provided that refuges are widely planted (with resistance monitored), and breeding the resistance trait into more varieties would reduce the genetic loss. Classic mistake (weak answer): describes the crop's effect on caterpillars and harvests, with no biodiversity level, no data and no judgement ("a gain in harvest isn't a gain in biodiversity").
2. (Q2, evaluate, 7 marks.) A rare wild sheep species survives as one population of about 300 animals, all descended from 8 founders. A cell bank holds frozen skin cells taken from 10 wild sheep of this species that died 30 years ago; these 10 carried alleles no longer found in the living population. Scientists plan to use somatic cell nuclear transfer, with eggs and surrogate mothers from domestic sheep. In past cloning of this kind, about 2 of every 100 embryos transferred to surrogates produced a live lamb. Evaluate this plan as a way to protect the species. Refer to genetic diversity, animal welfare and one alternative.
   Model: In somatic cell nuclear transfer, the nucleus of a donor's body cell is placed in an egg whose nucleus has been removed, so the clone's nuclear DNA matches the donor. Genetic diversity: the living population's alleles come from only 8 founders; clones of the 10 frozen donors would carry alleles that have been lost, and if the clones survive and breed with living sheep, those alleles re-enter the gene pool, increasing genetic diversity and the population's capacity to cope with disease or change. The gain depends on the number of different donors, not the number of clones: many clones of one donor add only that donor's alleles. The clones' mitochondrial DNA comes from the domestic sheep's eggs, not the wild species. Animal welfare: with about 2 live lambs per 100 embryos, many domestic ewes undergo egg collection, embryo transfer and failed pregnancies, and cloned lambs may have health problems, so the welfare cost per lamb is high. Alternative: managed breeding among the living animals (equalising founder contributions) and habitat protection; these address the threats that reduced the population, which cloning does not, but they cannot restore alleles that have already been lost, which only the frozen cells hold. Judgement: the plan is worthwhile to a moderate extent as a small, monitored addition to habitat protection and breeding management, provided the clones survive, reproduce and are integrated into the herd; it is not a substitute for protecting the species' habitat. Classic mistake crossed: "clones are identical, so cloning can never increase genetic diversity". Other weak answer (author note, not shown): "more clones of one sheep give more diversity".
3. (Q3, assess, 6 marks.) Many thousands of people with kidney failure wait years for a kidney from a human donor, and some die while waiting. Scientists have gene-edited pigs so that their kidneys are more suitable for transplant into humans: three pig genes whose products trigger rapid rejection by the human immune system were inactivated, seven human genes that help control immune and clotting reactions were added, and copies of a virus built into the pig genome were inactivated. The pigs are raised in sealed, disease-controlled facilities. Since 2024, a small number of patients have received these kidneys, first with special permission and then in clinical trials. One patient's kidney worked for about nine months before it was removed; others are still being followed. Assess the use of gene-edited pigs as a source of kidneys for transplant. Refer to benefits and ethical issues.
   Model: Benefit: compared with waiting years for a human kidney, a supply of edited pig kidneys could be available when needed, so if successful the benefit would be large for the many people on waiting lists. Evidence: only a small number of patients have been treated, first with special permission and then in trials, and one kidney worked for about nine months before removal, so the benefit is promising but not yet established; long-term function and safety are unknown. Ethical issues: pigs are bred, confined in sealed facilities and killed for their organs, a cost to the animals; early patients accept high and uncertain risks, so fully informed, voluntary consent is essential; a pig virus could cross into a patient and spread to other people, a risk carried by people who did not consent, reduced (not removed) by inactivating the viral copies and by monitoring; some people object in principle to using animals as organ sources, which evidence cannot settle. Access and cost may also determine who benefits (extension). Judgement: the use of gene-edited pigs is justified to a moderate extent as carefully regulated trials, provided patients give informed consent, animal welfare standards are maintained and recipients are monitored for infection; it is not yet a replacement for human donor kidneys. Classic mistake crossed: "a kidney worked for nine months, so pig kidneys solve the shortage". Other weak answer (author note, not shown): "it's unnatural, so it's wrong" (no reason given).
