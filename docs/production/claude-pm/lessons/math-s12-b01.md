### 2. one variable: how many, usually?
> First, look at just the ice creams column. Each thing we measure, like the ice creams sold, changes from day to day, so it's called a variable. You already have the tools from Year Eleven, a dot plot, the mean and the median. The mean is seventy-seven ice creams a day, and the median is seventy-**five**. ### 4. which kind do you need?
> So which kind do you need? Ask what the question is about. A question about one quantity, its typical value or its spread, needs one variable. A question about whether two quantities go together needs pairs, measured on the same things. Let's sort four situations. The price of petrol at twenty service stations today, that's one variable. Each used car's age and its price, that's two, in pairs. The length of every song on a playlist, one variable. ### 5. which variable goes across?
> Back to the kiosk. When we draw this data, one variable goes across the bottom, on the horizontal axis, and one goes up the side, on the vertical axis. The one that may explain or predict the other is called the independent variable, or the explanatory variable. It goes across. The one that responds is the dependent variable, or the response variable. It goes up. Hot weather might make people buy more ice creams, but selling ice creams can't change the weather. And the kiosk could use tomorrow's forecast to predict its sales. So temperature is **independent**, and ice creams sold is **dependent**.

### 6. not the left column
> Careful. The variable written first, or in the left column, isn't automatically the independent one. Suppose the board had the ice creams written on the left. Temperature would still go across, because it's still the one that explains the other. And sometimes neither variable obviously drives the other. Take shoe size and reading speed for children. Older children tend to have bigger feet, and they also read faster, so the two go together. But bigger shoes don't make anyone read faster. Here, you choose by what you want to predict. If you want to predict reading speed from shoe size, then shoe size goes **across**.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 9; pack question 1, original, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "For each situation, decide whether the data are one-variable or bivariate. Give a reason. (a) A school canteen records the number of sausage rolls sold each day for a term, to plan how many to order. (b) The canteen records each day's top temperature and the number of cold drinks sold, to see whether hot days sell more drinks. (c) A rideshare company records the waiting time of every booking last Saturday."
Model answer: (a) One-variable: one measurement per day, and the question is about a typical amount. (b) Bivariate: two measurements on each day, kept in pairs, and the question is whether they go together. (c) One-variable: one measurement per booking. Classic mistake (crossed): "(c) bivariate: lots of numbers".

**Attempt 2 (scene 11; pack question 2 with the fare listed first, original, HSC style, 2 marks suggested).** "A rideshare app records, for each of ten trips, the fare charged ($) and the distance travelled (km). (a) Which is the independent variable? Justify your answer. (b) One trip is plotted at the point (8, 21). What does this point represent?"
Model answer: (a) Distance, because the fare is worked out from the distance, so distance is used to predict the fare; it goes on the horizontal axis. (b) Distance is across, so the point is a trip of 8 km that cost $21. Classic mistake (crossed): "fare: it's listed first".
