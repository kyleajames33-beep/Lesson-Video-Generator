### 2. correlation isn't causation
> First, two words. When two variables tend to move together, like these, we say they're correlated, or that there's a correlation. Causation is stronger. It means changing one variable actually makes the other change. A scatter plot can show a correlation. On its own, it can't show **causation**. It shows they move together, but not **why**. Think of a rooster that crows just before sunrise. The two happen together every morning, but the rooster doesn't make the sun come up.

### 3. a third variable drives both
> So why do these two move together? Think about a hot day. Hot weather makes people buy more ice creams, and hot weather also makes people buy more sunscreen. Temperature drives **both**. Ice creams don't make anyone buy sunscreen. A hidden variable like this, which drives both, is called a confounding variable, or a lurking variable. Just naming temperature isn't enough. Say how it drives each one. To find one, ask, what third thing could make both of these **change**?

### 6. when it really is causal
> But don't swing too far the other way. Some relationships really are causal. A rideshare app charges a booking fee, plus two dollars ten for every kilometre. So distance and fare can be described as causal, with distance as the independent variable, and fare as the dependent one. The data show the pattern, but it's the rule that shows the **cause**.

### 7. testing a cause: an experiment
> So how could we actually show a cause? With a controlled experiment. Change one thing on purpose, keep everything else as similar as you can, and see if the other thing changes. Say the kiosk wants to know if a lower price sells more ice creams. It picks days at random to charge four dollars or five dollars, so hot days and cool days end up spread across both prices. It won't split exactly evenly, but over many days, the weather evens out. If the four dollar days sell more, the price is the likely reason, because nothing else differs on **average**. Just watching what happens, like the kiosk's board, is an observational study, and it can't rule out a third **variable**.
