### 2. the only way in and out
> Everything a cell needs, like oxygen and glucose, gets in across its surface, the cell membrane. And every waste, like carbon dioxide, leaves the same way. Meanwhile, the inside, its volume, is where supplies are used up and waste is made. So surface area is the supply line, and volume is the demand. What matters is how much surface there is for each bit of inside. That's the surface-area-to-volume ratio, SA to V for short.

Visual: the giant Paramecium fades; `b6a-anonymous-cell-v1.png` (inspected: a plain rounded cell, a clear membrane line and empty pale cytoplasm) enters at the centre left. ### 3. the one centimetre cube, and a prediction
> Let's model a cell as a cube, because a cube is easy to measure. Start with sides of one centimetre. A cube has six faces, each one centimetre by one, so its surface area is six square centimetres. Its volume is one by one by one: one cubic centimetre. So SA to V is six to one, six square centimetres of surface for every cubic centimetre inside. Prediction: if the side doubles, which grows faster, the surface or the volume?

### 4. double the side

### 5. the pattern, and a rule to remember
> One more: three centimetre sides. Each face is nine square centimetres, so the surface area is fifty-four. The volume is twenty-seven. That's two to one. Here's a rule to remember: surface squares, volume cubes, and volume wins the race.

### 6. less surface for each part of the cell
> So why does the ratio matter? Each cubic centimetre of a cell's inside uses supplies and makes waste at about the same rate, whatever the size of the cell. In the small cube, each cubic centimetre is served by six square centimetres of membrane. In the big cube, only two. The big cube does have more surface in total, but much less surface for each part of its inside. So as a cell grows, materials can't move in and out fast enough to keep up with what its inside needs.

### 7. the second problem: distance
> There's a second problem: distance. Materials spread through a cell by diffusion, which is fast over tiny distances, but slow over long ones. Oxygen entering the one centimetre cube travels half a centimetre to reach the centre. In the three centimetre cube, it has to travel one and a half. In a giant cell, the middle would run short of oxygen and fill up with waste, long before supplies arrived.

That's why red blood cells are thin discs, and nerve cells are long and thin. And big organisms, like you, are built from many small cells.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Materials enter and leave a cell only across its surface (the cell membrane). The volume (the inside) uses supplies and makes waste. SA:V = surface area ÷ volume: the surface for each bit of inside.
- Cube: SA = 6 × side², V = side³, so SA:V = 6 ÷ side. Side 1 cm: 6 cm², 1 cm³, 6:1. Side 2 cm: 24 cm², 8 cm³, 3:1. Side 3 cm: 54 cm², 27 cm³, 2:1.
- As a cell grows, volume increases faster than surface area, so SA:V falls. Each part of the cell gets less membrane, so materials move in and out too slowly for its needs. The diffusion distance to the centre also increases.
- So SA:V limits cell size: cells stay small, have shapes that raise SA:V (flat discs, long and thin), or organisms are made of many small cells.

Memory: surface squares, volume cubes, and volume wins the race.

## Memory aid check

- **"Surface squares, volume cubes, and volume wins the race."** (The site's staged l02 hook, "always" dropped.) Assumptions: for objects of the same shape, surface area is proportional to length squared and volume to length cubed, so as length increases, volume increases faster and SA:V falls. Limits: it compares objects of the same shape (changing shape can raise SA:V at the same volume, which scene 8 shows straight after); for lengths below 1 unit the cube is smaller than the square, so "volume wins" is true only as size increases, hence "always" is dropped.
