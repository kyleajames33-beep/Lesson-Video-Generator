### 1. hook (from the WHO rung)
> Last time, the WHO definition gave health three dimensions: physical, mental and social. It's tempting to treat them as three separate scores. But think about a student who tears a hamstring. A scan would find one physical injury. Yet within two months, their friendships, their mood and even their recovery have all changed. So why doesn't an injury stay in the dimension it started in?

Visual: opens on v01's handoff frame (the meanings ladder with the WHO rung lit blue and "physical", "mental" and "social" glowing amber; the v01 heading "What is health?" at the top left). ### 2. the loop, step by step
> Follow it step by step. The torn hamstring means they can't train, so they stop seeing their team three times a week. That's a social change. Two months without the team, and without the thing they're good at, and their mood drops. That's a mental change. A low mood makes the rehab exercises harder to keep doing, so the physical recovery slows down. The change has travelled from physical, to social, to mental, and back to physical. It's a loop.

### 3. interaction, and the good loop
> That's what the syllabus means by dimensions interacting. A change in one dimension causes a change in another, and it can run back to where it started. So an answer that lists the dimensions one by one hasn't explained anything yet. Put a mechanism on every arrow. And the loop can run the good way too. Joining a social sports team lifts the social dimension, which supports mood, which makes it more likely the person keeps exercising. One change, three dimensions.

### 4. good health is relative
> So what counts as good health? It isn't one bar that everyone is measured against. A seventeen-year-old sprinter who's back to jogging after an illness would call their health poor. A seventy-year-old who walks two kilometres a day, and takes medication for blood pressure, would call the same fitness excellent. Both are right, because good health is judged relative to a person's circumstances, expectations and life stage. That's why someone can live with a chronic condition and still be in good health.

### 5. the health continuum
> To describe health that keeps moving, we use the health continuum. It's a line, not a switch, with optimal health at one end and death at the other. Everyone is somewhere on it, and nobody stays still. Now put two people at exactly the same point. One is three weeks into recovering from an injury, moving towards optimal health. The other has an untreated condition that's slowly getting worse. Their position is identical, but their situations are opposite.

### 6. direction, rate and a limitation
> So a position on its own doesn't describe anyone's health. You also need direction, which way they're moving, and rate, how fast. A broken wrist moves you sharply and briefly. A slowly developing condition moves you so gradually that nobody notices. The model has one big limitation, though. A single line can't show the dimensions moving in different directions at once, like a body that's recovering while mood is falling.

### 7. three timescales
> Health also changes on different timescales, and each one has different causes. Within a day or a week, it's sleep, food, stress or a passing cold, fast and mostly reversible. Across months and years, it's habits building up, a condition developing, or a job changing, slow enough to be invisible while it happens. And across a whole life, life stage changes what health even means. The slow timescale matters most, and it's the one noticed least.

They move to cheaper, more crowded housing, sleep gets broken, and the food gets cheaper. Health changes without anyone deciding anything about health.

### 9. circumstances: the range of choices

So health is dynamic: the dimensions interact, people move along the continuum, and the conditions around them keep shifting.
