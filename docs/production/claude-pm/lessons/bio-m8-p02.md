## Decisions

- **Route changes applied.** Change 6 keeps p02 as plant water loss "with a potometer or a stomatal count". This design uses **both**, with different jobs: the potometer is the controlled comparison (the route start), and the stomatal count is a short contrast that answers a different question (where water can leave), which sharpens the stop ("interpret water-loss evidence and limitations"). Change 10 places s07 after p02 in the Biology playlist, so the handoff is **s07**, not b06 (the route JSON's `next` still says bio-m8-b06; root to update the playlist order when s07 is added to the Biology route, as b05 did for g03). This video uses s07's three questions and its "repeats help reliability" rule at the level the attempts need, and leaves the target picture, the word-feature-fix rail and the marking-guideline evidence to s07.
- **Main teaching move:** a measurement you cannot see. Water vapour leaving a leaf is invisible, so the video measures what the shoot takes in instead, explains why that is a fair estimate (b05: loss at the leaves pulls water up the stem), builds the fair test step by step, reads the data, and then names what the bubble cannot tell you. The general move students keep (shared with bio-m7-p05's "claim only what the plate measured"): claim only what you measured, here uptake by this shoot. Memory line: **"The bubble measures what goes in, not what goes out."**
- **The investigation.** A bubble potometer comparison. Aim: find out how air movement affects the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot. IV: air movement (desk fan off, fan on at a fixed distance and speed). DV: rate of water uptake as bubble distance per minute (mm min⁻¹), timed over 5 minutes. Controlled: the same shoot for both conditions (so species, leaf area and stem diameter cannot change), room temperature, light, humidity (same room, same session), fan distance and setting, timing and the reading edge of the bubble. Control: still air (fan off), the comparison condition. Repeats: three 5-minute readings per condition, bubble reset with the reservoir between readings, conditions alternated (still, fan, still, fan, still, fan) with a few minutes' settling after each switch, temperature and light checked each trial, and more shoots before any general claim (Revision 2). Set-up: cut the shoot under water and fit it under water (air in the xylem can block water movement), seal joints with petroleum jelly, introduce one small bubble by lifting the capillary's far end from its beaker for a moment and returning it (Revision 2), blot the leaves dry, let the shoot settle about 5 minutes.
- **Why the IV is wind.** The site's card 9 example is a fan ("a shoot in still air moves the bubble about 4 mm per minute, while the same shoot in front of a fan manages over 9"), b05 explained why moving air speeds water loss, and the attempts then transfer to humidity (a bag over the leaves, attempt 1) and to an uncontrolled temperature (attempt 2), so three of the site's four factors appear without three separate experiments. Light is left out: its effect works through stomatal opening, which b05 taught only for ABA closure.
- **Constructed data (labelled "example data" on screen).** Still air: 21, 19 and 20 mm in 5 min (4.2, 3.8 and 4.0 mm min⁻¹; mean 4.0). Fan: 47, 46 and 48 mm in 5 min (9.4, 9.2 and 9.6 mm min⁻¹; mean 9.4). Matches the site's "about 4" and "over 9". Checked under "Numbers to verify". The hook says "about four millimetres a minute" and "more than twice as fast" (9.4 / 4.0 = 2.35).
- **Stomatal count (scene 10).** Clear nail varnish peel, lifted with clear tape onto a slide, viewed at high power. Example counts (Revision 2: illustrative equal-area counts per field, at one magnification, from several random non-overlapping fields on a few leaves of one species): lower surface about 20 stomata, upper surface about 3 (labelled on screen; the painted matched pair shows about 20 and 3). The count shows where water vapour can leave (b05: "mostly on the underside"); a count alone does not measure the water-loss rate. An imprint can sometimes preserve pore aperture, so the design no longer claims a peel can't show opening (science S2). No density calculation is spoken (author note in Numbers; a density per mm² needs the field area and belongs to a data question, not this video).
- **What the bubble cannot tell you (scene 9; the route stop).** Uptake is not loss (a small fraction is used in photosynthesis or stays in the cells; over a short reading the two are close); one cut shoot with no roots is not a whole plant in soil; a desk fan is not field wind. Narrow conclusion: "with moving air, this shoot took up water faster than in still air."
- **VRA (scene 11), in s07's words.** Validity: "does it measure what it was meant to?" Yes for "does moving air change this shoot's uptake rate?", with the condition that uptake is close to, but not the same as, water loss. Reliability: "if you repeat it the same way, do the results agree?" Yes, within about 0.4 mm min⁻¹ (said "about half a millimetre per minute"), against a gap of 5.4. Accuracy: "how close is it to the true value?" A slow leak or reading different edges of the bubble shifts readings; more repeats cannot fix a leak (s07's "repeats help reliability, but they can't fix an error that pushes every trial the same way").
- **Risk table (scene 12).** Three spoken rows, each with hazard, harm and a control for this practical: a sharp blade (cuts: cut on a tile, away from fingers); the fan near water (electric shock: keep fan and cord away from the water, dry bench, mop spills); nail varnish (flammable and its vapour irritates: a small amount, a ventilated room, no flames). Teacher-note rows, not shown: the glass capillary (breakage: the teacher assembles and clamps it; report breaks, do not pick up glass by hand); plant choice (use a non-toxic, non-irritant shrub; avoid plants with irritant or toxic sap); petroleum jelly (low hazard). The shown table shows only the row being spoken (p05's Revision 2 rule); earlier rows collapse to one-word grey chips.
- **Site alignment (L05 card 9, kept in substance).** "Transpiration itself is not a homeostatic response; it is the physical process the plant has to manage" (scene 2's framing, unspoken); "A potometer measures the rate of water uptake by a cut shoot, which closely approximates transpiration because most absorbed water is transpired" (scenes 2, 3, 9); "An air bubble in a capillary tube moves as the shoot draws water, and the distance travelled per minute under each condition is the dependent variable, with all other factors controlled" (scenes 3, 5); "a shoot in still air moves the bubble about 4 mm per minute, while the same shoot in front of a fan manages over 9" (hook, results); "The limitation worth quoting is that the potometer measures uptake, not transpiration directly: a small fraction of the water is used in photosynthesis or retained for turgor" (scene 9, said "stays in the cells"); the HSC exam move "Wind increases transpiration because it removes the humid boundary layer at the leaf surface" (scene 8's last sentence, said plainly as b05 did); the book note "A potometer measures water uptake (approximately transpiration) by timing bubble movement" (key note 1). Not used: the four-factor list as a lesson (b05 already explained why dry, windy air speeds loss; attempts carry humidity and temperature); light and the guard cell pumps (site error 1 below).
- **Site errors and gaps found (this read; `../site-corrections-m8.md` checked: L05 entries exist for card 10, the darkness misconception, the book notes, the fish card and the guard cell notes, none for card 9).** Root to forward to Codex for that file.
  1. L05 card 9: "Higher light intensity activates the guard cell K⁺ pumps, widening the pores." Light (especially blue light) activates the guard cells' proton pumps, and the resulting gradient drives K⁺ uptake through channels; there is no K⁺ pump in this account. Same family as the logged guard cell note ("The active step is K⁺ transport"), at a new location. Proposed: "Higher light intensity switches on the guard cells' proton pumps, which drive potassium ions in, so the pores open wider."
  2. L05 card 9: "In a typical school result, a shoot in still air moves the bubble about 4 mm per minute, while the same shoot in front of a fan manages over 9." Bubble distance per minute depends on the capillary's bore and the shoot's size, so there is no "typical" mm per minute that transfers between set-ups. Proposed: "In one school result, ..." and add "To compare different potometers, convert distance to volume (πr² × distance moved)." Method note; Codex to judge whether it belongs in the errors table or the method notes.
  3. L05 card 9: "Wind strips away the humid boundary layer clinging to the leaf surface, keeping the gradient at its maximum." Wind thins the boundary layer and steepens the gradient; "at its maximum" overstates (the gradient still depends on air humidity and leaf temperature). Proposed: "keeping the gradient steep." Minor.

### 2. why uptake tracks loss
> Recall how plants lose water. Water evaporates inside the leaves and diffuses out through the stomata, and more water is pulled up the **xylem** to replace it. So the faster a leafy shoot loses water, the faster its stem draws water in. Measuring what goes **in** lets you estimate what comes out.

### 3. the potometer
> That's the job of a **potometer**, a device that measures how fast a shoot takes up water. The cut shoot is sealed into a tube of water, joined to a narrow glass tube with a scale. As the shoot draws water in, the water column moves towards it, carrying a small air **bubble** along. The distance the bubble moves in a set time gives the **rate** of uptake. A **reservoir** lets you push the bubble back to the start.

### 4. airtight, settled

Then lift the narrow tube's end out of its water for a moment, so one small **bubble** enters, far from the shoot. Let the shoot **settle** for about five minutes before timing.

### 5. aim and variables
