- **Site corrections (quoted from site lesson 18, read 3 Nov; for `../site-corrections-phys-y11.md`; none already logged for this lesson):**
  1. Intro: "connect a voltmeter the wrong way and it reads almost nothing." A voltmeter connected in series reads close to the full supply voltage: its very high resistance lets almost no current flow, so the other components have almost no voltage across them (V = IR) and nearly all the supply voltage is across the meter. Suggest: "connect a voltmeter the wrong way and the circuit stops working, while the meter shows nearly the whole supply voltage".
  2. Card 3: "Put a voltmeter in series and its near-infinite resistance blocks the path, so almost no current flows and the circuit barely works, giving a meaningless reading." The reading is not meaningless; it is close to the supply voltage, not the voltage of the component the student wanted. Suggest: "giving a reading close to the supply voltage instead of the component's voltage".
  3. Q2: "A voltmeter of resistance $10\ \text{k}\Omega$ is connected across a $100\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor in a circuit. Explain why the voltage it reads will be significantly lower than the voltage that was across the resistor before the meter was connected." Two issues. (a) The model answer combines the two in parallel (about 9.1 kΩ) and reasons about how a series circuit shares the supply voltage, which lessons 19 to 21 teach after this lesson. (b) The question does not say what else is in the circuit: if the 100 kΩ resistor were connected straight across an ideal supply, the reading would not drop at all; it drops only because the resistor shares the supply with other series resistance. Suggest: state "in series with a second 100 kΩ resistor across a 12 V supply" and move the question to lesson 21's practice (or tag it "after lesson 21").

- **Author notes (not learner copy):**
  (a) Scenes 3 to 7 use a fixed 12 Ω resistor (root decision 30, Revision 2), because v14 taught that a lamp is non-ohmic; the lamp appears only where lighting or darkness shows the effect (1, 2, 8 to 10). A cold filament has a much lower resistance than a lit one; in scene 10 and A2 the current is so small that the lamp's voltage is negligible whichever value is used (0.60 µA × 12 Ω = 7.2 µV).
  (b) Ammeter: 0.10 Ω is typical of a school digital meter's current range (a fused shunt). With the meter in the loop, I = 6.0 ÷ 12.1 = 0.496 A, which still displays "0.50 A" to two significant figures; the meter's share is 0.50 × 0.10 = 0.050 V. Scene 4 states the share, not the sum of resistances (v20 owns R_series = ΣR); the slider shows the trend only, with no values between "0.10 Ω" and its top, tagged "how resistances in a line combine: next video".
  (c) Voltmeter: 10 MΩ is typical of a digital meter's voltage range. The current through it, across 6.0 V, is 6.0 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁷ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ A, against the lamp's 0.50 A (a ratio of about 1 to 830 000). The supply current change is negligible; how a low-resistance voltmeter lowers a reading is not taught (site correction 3).
  (d) Short circuit: 6.0 ÷ 0.10 = 60 A is the ideal-supply figure; a real bench supply current-limits or cuts out, a battery's internal resistance limits it and heats the battery, and a school meter's fuse blows. The tag says this in one line. The lamp has almost no voltage across it (parallel with 0.10 Ω), so it goes dark.
  (e) Voltmeter in series: I = 6.0 ÷ (1.0 × 10⁷ + 12) ≈ 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ A; the lamp's voltage is about 7.2 × 10⁻⁶ V, so the voltmeter reads 6.0 − 0.0000072 ≈ 5.999993 V, which a three-digit display shows as "6.00 V". Spoken "almost six volts".
  (f) A2 circuit: battery, voltmeter (10 MΩ, in series), then the lamp with the ammeter (0.10 Ω) across it. I ≈ 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ A, almost all through the ammeter (0.10 Ω against the lamp's cold resistance of about 1 Ω), so the ammeter shows 0.00 A on any school range and the lamp current is negligible. The voltmeter reads about 6.0 V. Nothing is damaged. Typical meter resistances are not given on the card, because "very low" and "very high" are what the question tests.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why an ammeter goes in series: current is the charge passing a point, so the charge must pass through the meter; one path means the same coulombs pass through it and the lamp (2, 3). Why its resistance must be tiny: it sits in the only path, so its resistance would cut the current it measures (4). Why a voltmeter goes in parallel: voltage is energy given up between two points, so it must touch both ends (2, 5). Why a plain wire reads zero: no energy is given up along it (5). Why its resistance must be huge: it is a second path, and charge through it is not through the lamp (6). Why an ammeter across a lamp is dangerous: its low resistance lets charge bypass the lamp, so a huge current flows (8). Why a voltmeter in series reads nearly the supply voltage: almost no current, so the lamp takes almost no voltage (10).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** placement does not matter (1 to 3; site misconception 1); a good meter has a big resistance so it is safe (4, chip crossed; site misconception 2); a voltmeter in series reads almost nothing (9, 10, crossed; site corrections 1 and 2); the ammeter always shorts out when across a part, even with a voltmeter in series (A2 crossed).

**Attempt 1 (scene 12; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** Diagram: a battery, a resistor R and a lamp L in series; W a meter position in a gap in the loop between R and L; X a meter position on leads across L; Y a meter position on leads across R. "A battery, a resistor R and a lamp L are connected in series. The diagram shows three positions, W, X and Y, where a meter could be connected. A student wants to measure the current through L and the voltage across L. (a) Identify where the ammeter and where the voltmeter should be connected. (b) Justify each choice, referring to the resistance of each meter."
Model answer: (a) Ammeter at W; voltmeter at X. (b) Current is the rate of charge passing a point, so the charge must flow through the ammeter. At W it is in series with L; there is only one path, so the current through it is the current through L. It has a very low resistance, so adding it to the path hardly changes that current. Voltage is the energy given up per coulomb between two points, so the voltmeter must be connected across L's two ends: in parallel at X. It has a very high resistance, so it draws almost no current and hardly changes the circuit. (Y would give the voltage across R, not L.) Classic mistake (crossed): "ammeter at X" (an ammeter across L would short-circuit it).

**Attempt 2 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** Diagram: a 6.0 V battery, a voltmeter in series in the loop, and a lamp with an ammeter connected across it. "A student connects a 6.0 V battery, a lamp and two meters as shown. By mistake, the voltmeter is in series with the lamp and the ammeter is connected across the lamp. (a) State whether the lamp lights. (b) Predict the reading on each meter, and explain your predictions."
Model answer: (a) The lamp does not light. (b) The voltmeter is in the only path from the battery, and its very high resistance allows almost no current to flow. So the ammeter reads about 0 A, even though it is across the lamp (there is no large short-circuit current, because the voltmeter limits it). With almost no current, the lamp and ammeter have almost no voltage across them (V = IR ≈ 0), so nearly all of the battery's voltage is across the voltmeter: it reads about 6.0 V. Classic mistake (crossed): "the ammeter short-circuits the battery and burns out".
