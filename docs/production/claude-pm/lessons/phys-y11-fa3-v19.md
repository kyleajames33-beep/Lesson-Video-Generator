### 1. hook (which meter needs the huge resistance?)
> So, the ammeter goes in series and the voltmeter goes in parallel. Let's see why, with just one lamp. An ammeter measures current, and a voltmeter measures voltage. Here's a clue to both. One of these meters is built with almost no resistance, and the other with a huge resistance, millions of ohms. Which one needs the huge **resistance**?

### 2. through, and across
> The answer comes from what each meter measures. Current is how many coulombs pass a point each second. So to count them, the charge has to flow through the meter itself. Voltage is different. It's the energy each coulomb gives up between two points, like going in one side of the lamp and out the other. So a voltmeter has to touch both of those **points**.

Visual: camera continue. `LoopFlow`: navy dots drift round the leads. On "pass a point each second" v11's `ChargeGate` appears on the lead beside the lamp, ticking (tag "coulombs per second, from the current video"); on "through the meter itself" a grey word "THROUGH" stamps under the ammeter. On "between two points" two small dots mark the lamp's two ends, each with a hollow gold `EnergyPerCoulomb` tag: "in: 6 J per coulomb", "out: 0 J per coulomb" (tag "a volt is a joule per coulomb"); a bracket joins them, "6 J given up per coulomb". On "both of those points" a grey word "ACROSS" stamps under the voltmeter.

### 3. the ammeter joins the path
> So the ammeter goes in the path. Swap the lamp for a twelve ohm resistor, break the circuit beside it, and connect the meter into the gap. There's still only one path, so every coulomb through the resistor goes through the meter too. That's a series connection. Six volts across twelve ohms, so the meter reads half an **amp**.

### 4. why almost no resistance
> But now the charge has to push through the meter as well. If the meter had a big resistance, the whole loop would resist more, so less current would flow. The meter would change the very current it's trying to measure. So an ammeter is built with a tiny resistance, ideally zero. A good one has about a tenth of an ohm. At half an amp, it takes only point oh five volts of the six. The resistor hardly **notices**.

Why does an ammeter go in the path of the current, while a voltmeter goes across a component? And why does one need almost no resistance while the other needs millions of ohms? Watch charge flow through an ammeter and past a voltmeter, see what changes when each meter's resistance is wrong, then find out what really happens when you swap them: one makes a short circuit, and the other reads nearly the whole supply voltage. Two exam-style questions at the end.
