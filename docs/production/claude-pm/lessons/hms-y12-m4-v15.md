### 2. one system, and how it reaches injury
> The syllabus puts sleep, nutrition and hydration in one dot point because they work as one system. Each one drags down the others. A late finish forces a late meal. The late meal disturbs sleep. Short sleep makes the next session worse and shifts food choices toward quick energy. Mild dehydration raises effort and blurs concentration. So the athlete starts each evening more tired than the last. That's why three specialists each see a different symptom of one problem. Now, how does that reach injury? Short sleep slows reactions and weakens attention. Low fuel brings **fatigue** on earlier, so technique breaks down sooner. Dehydration lowers stroke volume and raises effort and temperature. These rarely cause an injury directly. They create the conditions for one: an athlete a fraction slow, a little less accurate, whose technique fades earlier than it should.

### 3. guidelines, and what a guideline is
> First, the guidelines. A **guideline** is a population default, a sensible starting point for most people, not an individual prescription. For sleep, teenagers are generally advised to get around eight to ten hours a night, at consistent times. Athletes in heavy training often need the top of that range. Two things people get wrong. Quality isn't the same as duration: nine broken hours aren't eight unbroken ones. And **sleep debt**, the shortfall that builds up across nights, isn't repaid by one big sleep-in. For nutrition, follow the national dietary guidelines, but with more energy to match training, carbohydrate timed around sessions as you saw last time, and enough iron and calcium. For fluid, drink steadily through the day, with pale urine as a rough guide, and set training-day fluid from the sweat rate.

### 4. planning backwards from the fixed points
> Knowing a guideline changes nothing. **Planning** turns it into a day. Start from what can't move: school starts, training times, competition. Then work backwards. If the athlete must wake at six thirty and needs nine hours, lights out is nine thirty. So the post-training meal has to be eaten by about nine fifteen. Training ends at nine, so that meal has to be made before training. So the real decision isn't at ten at night. It's in the afternoon, when someone has the time and energy to cook. Fluid refills go at set points too. That answers the hook. The one change is the late night: a prepared meal and a fixed lights-out. Sleep and the next day's sessions improve first.

### 5. routines, not willpower
> **Routines** are what make the plan happen without a fresh decision every night. A wind-down: the same thirty minutes before bed, screens away, lights low, a cool, dark room. That helps an athlete who is still wired after evening training. Consistent sleep and wake times across the whole week, weekends included, within about an hour, so the body clock stays steady. Food prepared in advance, so a meal is waiting at nine fifteen instead of a takeaway at ten. Fluid carried, not looked for: a filled bottle in the bag every day. The point is that willpower runs out by nine at night. A plan that needs it every night will fail. Telling an athlete to try harder to sleep earlier isn't a fix. Changing the structure of the day is.

### 6. monitoring, and what each measure can't see
> And **monitoring** tells you whether the plan is working. Five cheap measures, each with a blind spot. A sleep diary shows duration and consistency, but people overestimate how long they slept. A daily **wellness** rating of sleep, soreness, fatigue, mood and stress shows trends, but it drifts if athletes think it affects selection. Morning resting heart rate rising is an early warning, but it can't tell fatigue from illness. Body mass before and after sessions shows fluid loss, as you saw last time. Three rules. A trend beats a single reading. Cheap measures are the ones that survive. And every measure needs a decision attached, or it's just a ritual.
