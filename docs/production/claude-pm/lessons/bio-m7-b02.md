### 2. two old ideas, and what germ theory needed
> Two older ideas stood in the way. **Spontaneous generation** was the idea that living things can arise from non-living matter, like microbes from broth. **Miasma** was the idea that disease comes from bad air, given off by rotting matter. **Germ theory** says something different: infectious diseases are caused by microbes and other pathogens, and microbes come only from other microbes. To prove it, scientists needed two things. Show that microbes don't arise from the broth itself. And show that a specific microbe causes a specific disease.

### 8. give it, find it again, and why all four
> Third, he gave the pure culture to healthy guinea pigs, and they developed tuberculosis. Fourth, he recovered the same bacterium from those sick animals. Why all four? Finding a microbe in sick hosts only shows it's there alongside the disease. It might have moved in after the disease began. Giving the pure microbe to a healthy host, and seeing the disease appear, shows that the microbe causes it. Recovering it again rules out a different microbe sneaking in.

### 9. where the postulates struggle
> These four steps are called **Koch's postulates**, and their logic is still the standard, but they don't fit every pathogen. Viruses can't be grown on their own, because they need living cells. Some bacteria, like the one that causes leprosy, still can't be grown in a lab. Some healthy people carry a pathogen without getting sick, which breaks the first step. And for a disease that only infects humans, deliberately infecting a healthy person isn't ethical. Today, scientists often detect a microbe's DNA instead of growing it.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Germ theory: infectious diseases are caused by pathogens, and microbes come from other microbes. It replaced spontaneous generation (life from non-living matter) and miasma (disease from bad air).
- Pasteur's swan-neck flasks (around 1860): boiled broth, open S-shaped neck; air reached the broth, but dust and microbes settled in the bend, so the broth stayed clear. Breaking the neck let particles in and the broth went cloudy. IV: whether particles could reach the broth; DV: growth (cloudiness); controlled: broth, boiling, air.
- Koch's postulates (tuberculosis, 1882): 1. the microbe is found in every case of the disease (not in healthy hosts); 2. it is grown in pure culture; 3. the pure culture causes the disease in a healthy host; 4. the same microbe is recovered from that host.
- Step 1 shows association only; step 3 shows cause; step 4 confirms it is the same microbe.
- Limits: viruses need living cells; some bacteria cannot be cultured (leprosy); healthy carriers; infecting healthy people is unethical. DNA evidence is often used instead.

Memory: Find it, grow it, give it, find it again. Pasteur: where microbes come from. Koch: which microbe causes which disease.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (21 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; OpenStax Microbiology 1.1 and 3.1) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Pasteur's swan-neck flasks.** Experiments around 1859 to 1861; reported in 1861 (*Mémoire sur les corpuscules organisés qui existent dans l'atmosphère*); Alhumbert Prize 1862 (OpenStax Microbiology 3.1; Institut Pasteur history pages). Pasteur boiled broth in flasks whose necks he had heated and drawn into S-shaped curves; the necks stayed open; dust settled in the bend; the broth stayed clear; breaking the neck or tilting the flask so broth touched the bend led to growth. "Around 1860", "stayed clear ... for months", "within days": correct and deliberately not more precise (site error 2). Straight-necked flasks (scene 4): widely taught as a comparison; the facts list flags that Pasteur's recorded control was mainly the broken or tilted neck (site error 7). Science check to decide whether scene 4's straight-neck sentence should become "Open flasks without the curve" or stay.
- **The objection.** Supporters of spontaneous generation (for example Félix Pouchet; earlier John Needham against Spallanzani's sealed flasks) argued that boiling or sealing destroyed a "vital force" in air. "Argued that boiling or sealing a flask destroyed something in the air that life needed": correct as a plain summary.
- **Miasma and spontaneous generation.** Standard definitions (site card 1). Correct.
- **Koch and tuberculosis.** Koch announced the discovery of the tubercle bacillus on 24 March 1882 to the Berlin Physiological Society (the date now marked as World Tuberculosis Day; WHO). He stained bacilli in tissue (methylene blue with a Bismarck brown counterstain: blue rods on brown tissue), grew them on heat-coagulated blood serum, which took weeks because *M. tuberculosis* divides about every 15 to 20 hours, inoculated guinea pigs, which developed tuberculosis, and recovered the bacilli (Koch 1882, *Berliner Klinische Wochenschrift*; translation in Brock, *Milestones in Microbiology*). His lecture stated that one seventh of all human deaths were due to tuberculosis. "About one in seven deaths in Europe": correct as Koch's figure. "He didn't find it in healthy tissue": consistent with Koch's report that the bacilli were absent from non-tuberculous tissue. "German doctor": correct (physician).
- **Postulates.** Formulated from Koch's work and stated by Loeffler (1884) and Koch (1890). Four-step form as taught in NSW. "Find it, grow it, give it, find it again": matches the order.
- **Limits.** Viruses need living cells (b01). *Mycobacterium leprae* has not been grown in cell-free culture (it is grown in mouse footpads and nine-banded armadillos; WHO and CDC leprosy pages). "Still can't be grown in a lab": acceptable plain wording for "cannot be grown in laboratory culture"; science check may prefer "in laboratory culture". Healthy carriers: *Salmonella* Typhi and *Vibrio cholerae* carriers. Ethics of deliberately infecting healthy people: correct (human challenge trials exist under strict ethical approval for some diseases, so the narration says "deliberately infecting a healthy person isn't ethical" in the context of testing step three for a human-only disease; science check to confirm the wording is not over-absolute; alternative "is rarely ethical"). Molecular (DNA-based) criteria: Fredricks and Relman 1996, *Clinical Microbiology Reviews* 9: 18. Correct.
- **TB today (time-sensitive, not spoken).** WHO's *Global Tuberculosis Report 2025* estimates about 1.23 million deaths from TB in 2024 (WHO press release, November 2025; https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2025 and coverage at https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/tuberculosis/who-cites-progress-against-tb-globally-warns-about-reduced-funding , checked 21 Oct 2026). Not used in narration; recorded in case the learning review asks for a present-day hook line ("tuberculosis still kills more than a million people a year"). Recheck near recording if used.
- **Attempt 1.** Hypothetical data; one of five not developing disease is plausible (variable susceptibility, dose). Correct as a hypothetical.
- **Attempt 2.** *Chlamydia pecorum* is an obligate intracellular bacterium causing conjunctivitis (keratoconjunctivitis), urinary tract disease and reproductive disease in koalas; it is grown in host-cell culture (for example McCoy cells), not on cell-free media (Polkinghorne, Hanger and Timms 2013, *Veterinary Microbiology* 165: 214). Koala populations of Queensland, New South Wales and the ACT were listed as Endangered under the EPBC Act on 12 February 2022, and the koala is listed as endangered under the NSW Biodiversity Conservation Act (DCCEEW listing page). "Koalas in New South Wales are listed as an endangered species": correct (recheck near recording). Healthy koalas can carry *C. pecorum* without signs, noted in the model as an extra credit point.
