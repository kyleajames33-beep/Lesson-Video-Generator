### 2. progressive overload, and its two failure modes
> The body adapts to a stress it isn't used to. That change is **adaptation**. Once it's adapted, the same stress isn't new any more, so it stops driving change. That's the hook. The load was new for two months, then it wasn't. So the load has to rise, which is **progressive overload**. But it has to rise slowly enough to recover, because adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session. So the principle has two failure modes. Too little progression gives a **plateau**. Too much gives **overtraining**: fatigue builds faster than recovery, performance falls, and injury or illness follow. And here's the trap. On the way up, overtraining looks like the best progress of all, until it collapses.

### 3. the variables of overload, one at a time
> In Year 11 you met FITT: frequency, intensity, time and type. Each one is a way to progress. In aerobic training, that's more sessions, a faster pace, longer runs, or moving from continuous to intervals. In strength training, it's more sessions per muscle, a heavier load, more reps and sets, or moving from a machine to free weights. Aerobic overload usually rises through volume, and a common guide is about ten per cent a week. Strength overload usually rises through load, once the target reps are done with good technique. And change one variable at a time. If load, volume and frequency all rise in the same week and the athlete breaks down, you can't tell which change did it. So the smallest change for our gym-goer is a small load increase once the reps feel solid.

### 4a. thresholds: two scales
> Overload says how much more. A **training threshold** says more than what: the minimum intensity that produces a training effect for a system. In aerobic training, you met the two thresholds earlier. Below about seventy per cent of maximum heart rate, there's too little stimulus, however long you go. Above the lactate threshold, by-products build up and the work can't last. Strength has thresholds too, in per cent of one-rep max, the heaviest load you can lift once. Below about sixty-five per cent you mostly build endurance. For maximal strength in trained lifters, it's about eighty-five per cent. Beginners get stronger on much lighter loads, because their threshold is lower. Same idea, different units.

### 5. specificity has five dimensions
> **Specificity** means the body adapts to the exact stress you apply, so training should match the sport. The marks come from naming which way a session matches or misses. Five questions. Energy system: does it stress the system the event uses? A hundred-metre sprinter doing only long runs fails that. Movement pattern: does the exercise look like the sport's action? A cyclist training only on a seated leg machine misses that. Speed: is it done at the sport's velocity? A jumper doing only heavy, slow lifts gets stronger, but not quicker off the ground. And context: are the decision and the pressure there? But total specificity has a cost: little variety, weaknesses left unfixed, and the same tissue loaded again and again, which is how overuse injuries start. So say which dimension, not just "not specific".

### 6. applying them to aerobic and strength training, and evaluating
> The dot point asks you to evaluate how these apply to aerobic and strength training. They apply validly to both, but with different precision. In strength training, load, reps and sets are countable, so overload can be set exactly. In aerobic training, intensity is read from heart rate or effort, and heat, sleep, illness and hydration all shift it, so the same prescription gives a different stimulus on different days. To evaluate a program, use four steps: name the principle, quote the feature that shows it, predict the consequence, and recommend a change. A lifter adds ten kilograms to every lift every week. That's overload breached at the top: technique fails, fatigue builds, injury risk rises. Recommend small steps, only once the reps are solid.
