### 2. the first time
> Let's go back to the first infection. As you saw in the last two videos, only a few B cells and T cells fit the virus at first. They have to be selected and divide for days, while innate defences hold the virus back. So antibodies only appear in the blood about a week after infection, and they peak later. Meanwhile the virus spreads, and you get sick. This first reaction is the **primary response**.

### 3. what stays

So the memory is stored in cells, not in the antibody level.

### 4. the second time
> Now the same virus arrives again. This time, many fitting cells are already waiting, so the virus's antigens meet them quickly. Memory B cells need fewer divisions, and they turn into plasma cells fast. So antibodies rise within days, climb far higher, and stay up longer. This is the **secondary response**, and it often clears the virus before you feel sick.

### 5. reading the graph
> On a graph of antibody level against time, the primary response is a slow, low hump. The secondary response is a fast, tall peak. The scale is often logarithmic, so the second peak can be ten to a hundred times higher. But this only happens for the same antigen. If a different antigen arrives at the same time, it gets a primary response, slow and low, because no memory cells fit it.

### 15. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Primary response (first exposure): few fitting B and T cells must be selected and multiply; antibody appears after about a week, peaks low; illness often occurs while it builds. Most plasma cells then die, so antibody falls.
- Memory B and T cells are long-lived and far more numerous than the original fitting cells.
- Secondary response (same antigen again): short lag, much higher peak (often 10 to 100 times, log scale), lasts longer; often cleared before illness. A new antigen gets a primary response.
- Vaccination: antigen in a form that can't cause the disease (weakened live, killed, one protein, mRNA instructions) gives a primary response and memory without illness. A booster is a second exposure: secondary response, more memory.
- Active (own response, memory, long-lasting, slow to build): natural = infection; artificial = vaccine. Passive (given antibodies, immediate, no memory, fades in weeks to months): natural = mother's antibodies across the placenta and in breast milk; artificial = antibody injection, such as antivenom.

Memory: Second exposure: faster, bigger, longer. Active: you do the work. Passive: you're handed the antibodies.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (22 Oct) against the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Chickenpox reinfection.** After varicella, immunity usually prevents a second case of chickenpox; second cases are uncommon but occur. "Most people who've had chickenpox don't catch it again" and "usually don't get sick": correct as hedged. Author note, not learner copy: the virus stays latent in nerve cells and can reactivate years later as shingles, which is reactivation, not reinfection; leaving it out does not teach a wrong idea about this video's question. Science check to confirm that judgement.
- **Antibody level years later.** Antibody levels fall after the primary response as most plasma cells die; some long-lived plasma cells keep a low level (b07 site error 8), so "has usually fallen a long way" (not "to zero"). Correct.
- **Primary response timing.** Specific antibody is usually detectable about 7 to 10 days after a first exposure and peaks at about 2 weeks; illness often occurs during this period, while innate defences hold the pathogen back. "About a week after infection, and they peak later": correct (site error 4).
- **Memory cells.** Memory B and T cells are long-lived; some persist for decades (for example, smallpox vaccine memory has been measured decades after vaccination). Antigen-specific memory cells are many times more frequent than naive precursors (site error 5). "Stay in the body for years, many for decades" and "far more cells that fit this virus": correct as hedged.
- **Secondary response.** Shorter lag (about 1 to 3 days before the rise), higher peak (commonly cited as 10 to 100 times, on a log scale; the attempt uses about 40 times), more sustained, mainly IgG with higher affinity; memory B cells differentiate quickly into plasma cells. "Rise within days, climb far higher, and stay up longer" and "often clears the virus before you feel sick": correct as hedged.
- **Different antigen.** A new antigen given with a booster of a familiar one produces a primary response (classic experiment). Correct.
- **Vaccines.** Types: live attenuated (the varicella vaccine is a live attenuated Oka strain virus), inactivated, subunit or protein (including toxoids), mRNA. "Instructions that let your cells make that protein": correct for mRNA vaccines. "In a form that can't cause the disease": correct at this level (live attenuated vaccines can rarely cause mild illness, and are avoided in people with weakened immunity; not needed for this video's answers). Science check to judge whether "can't" needs "usually can't".
- **Boosters.** A further dose acts as a re-exposure, triggering a secondary response and increasing memory. Correct. "A vaccine prepares you before an infection. It doesn't cure an illness you already have": correct (post-exposure vaccination for slowly developing diseases such as rabies prevents illness before it starts; it does not cure established illness; site error 11).
- **Active and passive.** Active: own response, memory, long-lasting, slow onset; natural (infection) or artificial (vaccination). Passive: transferred antibodies, immediate, no memory, broken down over weeks to months. Maternal IgG crosses the placenta (mostly in the last trimester) and protects the newborn for the first months; breast milk antibodies (mainly IgA) protect the baby's gut. "Across the placenta and in breast milk": correct. "A week or more to build": correct (about 1 to 2 weeks for a primary response).
- **Antivenom.** Australian snake antivenoms (including brown snake antivenom) are made from the plasma of horses given small, increasing doses of venom (CSL Seqirus); some antivenoms elsewhere use sheep. "Made by giving an animal, often a horse, small doses of venom and collecting the antibodies it makes": correct. Antivenom is artificial passive immunity; the antibodies are cleared within days to weeks, so a later bite needs antivenom again. Codex to confirm the Australian brown snake antivenom source animal.
