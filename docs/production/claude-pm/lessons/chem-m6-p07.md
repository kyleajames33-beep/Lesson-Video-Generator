- **Site departure (deliberate, recorded):** the site's method titrates 10.00 mL of undiluted vinegar with 0.5000 mol/L NaOH. The site method stays valid and appears as the rejected alternative in scene 5 ("stronger base: corrosive"). - **Main teaching move:** the same vinegar gives two pH readings (2.46 neat, 2.96 diluted tenfold), and neither is an amount. Each number in a household analysis is a measurement of a particular solution, so the method has to decide which solution each measurement is made on: pH on the sample as it is; the amount on a dilution when the titre would not fit the burette, scaled back up. The general move students keep: before you measure, decide what each number will mean, then report each one in its own units, with its limits.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) the same vinegar gives two pH values, and neither is an amount (1); (2) two questions need two measurements: pH for how acidic, a titration for how much; clear, fast vinegar suits a direct titration (2); (3) pH describes the solution the probe sits in, so it is measured on the undiluted sample, with buffers either side (3); (4) the label lets you estimate the titre before you start; neat vinegar would need more than three burette fills (4); (5) dilute the sample, not strengthen the base: stronger base is corrosive, and dilution keeps the moles, so you multiply back by ten (5); (6) the base is standardised because it absorbs CO₂; the endpoint is the first faint pink that lasts 30 s (6); (7) concordant titres give the mean (7); (8) 1 : 1 ratio, moles in the aliquot, concentration of the dilution, times ten (8); (9) report as the label does, grams per 100 mL, and compare (9); (10) two results with two meanings, consistent through Ka; the titration counts every acid, so "as ethanoic acid" (10); (11) the limits of the result (11); validity, reliability, accuracy (12); risk (13); attempts and brief feedback (14 to 17); decision rule and handoff (18); key notes (19).

- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Molar mass: M(CH₃COOH) = 2(12.01) + 4(1.008) + 2(16.00) = 60.052 → 60.05 g/mol (HSC data sheet values). (b) Label estimate: the Food Standards Code (Standard 2.10.1) requires vinegar to hold at least 40 g/kg acetic acid; labels state "4% acidity" without a basis. At a density of about 1.005 g/mL, 4 g per 100 g and 4 g per 100 mL differ by 0.5 %, so the video treats the label as about 4 g per 100 mL ("about" in scenes 4 and 9) and names the density as a limitation (scene 11). The measured 4.083 g per 100 mL is 4.063 g per 100 g, above the 40 g/kg minimum, so the result ("close to the label", "consistent with the label") holds on either basis. So c ≈ 40 ÷ 60.05 = 0.666 → "about 0.67 mol/L"; a 25.00 mL neat aliquot needs 0.6661 × 25.00 ÷ 0.09940 = 167.5 mL → "about a hundred and seventy millilitres", 3.4 fills of a 50 mL burette. After tenfold dilution the estimate is 16.75 mL → "about seventeen". (c) Calibration: C16 scene 4 says the pH 7.00 buffer sets the zero point and the second buffer the slope, and buffers should lie close to the samples, ideally either side. Vinegar at about pH 2.5 lies outside a 4.00 and 7.00 calibration, so this design names pH 7.00 and 2.00 buffers (pH 2.00 buffers are sold by school suppliers; a pH 1.68 oxalate standard is the alternative). If a school has only 4.00 and 7.00, the fallback screen line is "pH 4.00 and 7.00: the vinegar reads just outside; note it as a limitation". Some school meters auto-recognise only 4.01, 7.00 and 10.01; if a pH 2.00 point cannot be entered, use the fallback line. Scene 3 says why this differs from C16's "pH four for acidic samples": vinegar reads below 4, so a pH 4 buffer would not bracket it. (d) Readings: probe readings 2.46, 2.45, 2.46 (mean 2.4567 → 2.46); the hook's diluted reading 2.96. Both match the Ka model (author note e). Measured at 25 °C (screen tag). (e) Ka check: with Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ (as C08 and C10) and c = 0.6799 mol/L, the quadratic gives [H₃O⁺] = 3.489 × 10⁻³ mol/L, pH 2.457 → 2.46 (0.51 % ionised; the approximation √(Ka c) gives 2.456, also 2.46); with the data sheet pKa 4.76 (Ka 1.74 × 10⁻⁵) it gives 2.465 → 2.46 or 2.47. Diluted tenfold (0.06799 mol/L): pH 2.960 with Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ (2.967 with 1.74 × 10⁻⁵), so the hook's "about half a unit" holds. Real vinegar readings can differ by a few hundredths because the probe responds to activity, not concentration, and vinegar holds small amounts of other substances; scene 10 says "about". (f) Label basis: grams per 100 mL is a mass per volume. A percentage by mass would divide the acid's mass by the vinegar's mass, which needs the density (site value 1.005 g/mL; G03's method); with 1.005 g/mL, 4.083 g per 100 mL is 4.083 ÷ 100.5 × 100 = 4.063 % by mass (not shown; scene 11 says "would also need the vinegar's density"). (g) Endpoint: phenolphthalein turns faint pink from about pH 8.2; the equivalence point for ethanoic acid with NaOH at these concentrations is near pH 8.7 (C13). The pink can fade as CO₂ from the air dissolves, so "lasts 30 s" is the endpoint rule (pre-design). Dissolved CO₂ in the distilled water used for the dilution is negligible at this precision (screen not needed). (h) Other household samples: juice (citric acid, three protons per molecule, reported as citric acid), soft drink (degas first, C16), wine (C16's AWRI pH 8.2 endpoint with a probe) and antacids (back titration, C15) each change one step of this routine. Scene 18's grey tag names them as one line, so the syllabus examples are acknowledged without new teaching. (i) Ammonia volatility (A2): over dilute aqueous ammonia, the vapour is much richer in NH₃ than the liquid (for about 1 mol/kg NH₃ at 25 °C, the NH₃ partial pressure is about 0.017 bar against water's 0.031 bar, while NH₃ is under 2 % of the liquid's molecules), so an open sample loses ammonia proportionally far faster than water; the concentration falls. The feedback says "much faster". (j) Titratable acidity: vinegar's acid is almost all ethanoic acid; small amounts of other acids (for example from the source material) also react, so the result is reported "as ethanoic acid". (k) Significant figures: the standardised NaOH 0.09940 mol/L (four), volumetric volumes 25.00 and 250.0 mL (four), titres to 0.01 mL (four), so concentrations and the grams per 100 mL to four significant figures; working keeps guard digits ("keep the digits; round at the end"); the label estimate and the expected titres are one- or two-figure estimates and are spoken with "about". (l) Pronunciation: "pipette" pi-PET, "burette" byoo-RET, "aliquot" AL-i-kwot, "titre" TY-ter, "phenolphthalein" fee-nol-THAY-leen, "ethanoic" eth-uh-NO-ik, "K a" as two letters; numbers spoken as written; formulas not spoken.

## Safety facts check (self-check, 21 Oct; science check to confirm)
