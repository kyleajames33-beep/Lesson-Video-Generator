### 3. the angles at O
> Each pair of neighbouring corners makes a triangle with O. Both bearings are measured from the same north, so the angle at O is just the difference between them. From A to B, a hundred and twenty-eight minus forty-one is eighty-seven degrees. B to C is eighty-six degrees, and C to D is ninety-one **degrees**. The last one, from D back to A, crosses north. From three hundred and five degrees up to north is fifty-five degrees, then on to forty-one, so the angle is ninety-six **degrees**. Subtracting straight across, three hundred and five minus forty-one, gives two hundred and sixty-four. That's the slip. Now the check. The four angles go all the way round O, so they must add to three hundred and sixty **degrees**. They do. But that only checks your subtractions, not the surveyor's **readings**.

### 4. worked example: the paddock's area
> Here's the exam-style task. Find the area of paddock A B C D, to the nearest square metre. Each triangle has two measured distances and the angle between them, so its area is a half a b sine C. Triangle A O B is a half times eighty-five times seventy times sine eighty-seven degrees, about two thousand nine hundred and seventy-one square **metres**. The other three are about three thousand three hundred and fifty-two, two thousand eight hundred and eighty, and two thousand five hundred and **thirty-six**. Keep them unrounded in the calculator, then add. The paddock is about eleven thousand seven hundred and thirty-eight square metres, about one point one seven **hectares**. Putting a bearing, like a hundred and twenty-eight degrees, into the area rule is the slip. Use the angle at O, eighty-**seven**.
