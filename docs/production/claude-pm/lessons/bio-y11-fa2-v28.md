Main question: *How does the hypothalamus regulate hormone secretion through the pituitary and target glands?* (route)
Entry knowledge: see above. Students have watched FA1 and FA2 v01 to v27.
Stops after: nerves send fast impulses to one place; hormones are chemical messengers secreted into the blood that reach every cell but act only on target cells with matching receptors; the hypothalamus, at the base of the brain, monitors the blood and receives nerve signals, and its nerve cells release hormones, so it links the nervous and endocrine systems; the pituitary hangs below it on a stalk; releasing hormones from the hypothalamus reach the anterior pituitary in tiny blood vessels in the stalk, and the anterior pituitary secretes its own hormones into the blood; many are tropic hormones, which target other glands (TSH the thyroid, ACTH the adrenal cortex, others the ovaries and testes), so the pituitary is a "master gland" that answers to the hypothalamus; the thyroid axis: TRH, then TSH, then thyroxine, which raises cells' metabolic rate (faster respiration, more heat); rising thyroxine is detected by the hypothalamus and pituitary, which secrete less TRH and TSH, and falling thyroxine has the opposite effect, so negative feedback holds thyroxine within its range; reasons for a chain (a small signal controls a large output; two control points; one gland reaches many); the posterior pituitary stores and releases hormones made in the hypothalamus (ADH); the pancreas and adrenal medulla are not pituitary-controlled; in a healthy loop low thyroxine goes with high TSH, so measuring both locates a fault.
Handoff: the pancreas, which reads blood glucose itself (route entry bio-y11-fa2-v29).

### 9. the posterior pituitary: made in one place, released in another

One of these, ADH, makes the kidneys take back more water. So the anterior lobe actually makes its hormones, and the posterior lobe passes on **others**.

### 10. not every gland takes orders
> One caution. Not every gland takes orders from the pituitary. Cells in the pancreas read blood glucose themselves, and respond directly. ### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Hormones: chemical messengers secreted by glands into the blood; they reach every cell but act only on target cells with matching receptors. Nerves: fast impulses to one place.
- Hypothalamus (base of the brain): monitors the blood and receives nerve signals; its nerve cells release hormones, so it links the nervous and endocrine systems.
- Anterior pituitary: releasing hormones from the hypothalamus arrive in tiny blood vessels in the stalk; it makes and secretes tropic hormones, which target other glands (TSH → thyroid; ACTH → adrenal cortex; others → ovaries, testes).
- Example axis: hypothalamus (TRH) → anterior pituitary (TSH) → thyroid (thyroxine) → body cells (metabolic rate up). Negative feedback: rising thyroxine reduces TRH and TSH; falling thyroxine increases them.
- Posterior pituitary: stores and releases hormones made in the hypothalamus (ADH). Not every gland is pituitary-controlled (pancreas; adrenal medulla, by nerves).

Memory: Anterior actually makes; posterior passes on. The product switches off its own supply.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (3 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Nerves and hormones (scene 2).** Nerve impulses are fast and travel to specific cells; hormones travel in the blood to all tissues and act only on cells with specific receptors. Correct.
- **Hypothalamus (scene 3).** At the base of the brain, above the pituitary; contains osmoreceptors, thermosensitive neurons and neurons sensitive to circulating hormone levels; receives input from many brain regions; neurosecretory cells release hormones (releasing and inhibiting hormones into the portal blood; ADH and oxytocin from axon terminals in the posterior pituitary). "checks the blood flowing through it, including its temperature, its water content and its hormone levels": water content is a simplification of solute concentration (osmolarity); acceptable at this level. "about the size of an almond" (site) is not spoken. Correct.
- **Anterior pituitary and portal vessels (scene 4).** Hypothalamic releasing hormones enter capillaries at the base of the hypothalamus (median eminence) and travel in the hypophyseal portal veins down the stalk to a second capillary bed in the anterior pituitary, which secretes its own hormones into the systemic circulation. "tiny amounts": releasing hormones are secreted in very small quantities and diluted little because of the portal route. Correct.
- **Tropic hormones (scene 5).** TSH targets the thyroid; ACTH targets the adrenal cortex (the outer layer); FSH and LH target the gonads. The pituitary sits in a bony hollow at the base of the skull (not spoken). "the thyroid gland in your neck": correct. "on top of the kidneys": the adrenal glands sit on the upper pole of each kidney. Correct.
- **Thyroid axis (scene 6).** TRH (thyrotropin-releasing hormone) → TSH → thyroxine (T4; with T3, the more active form, not named). Thyroid hormones raise basal metabolic rate, oxygen consumption and heat production in most tissues. Thyroxine contains four iodine atoms. Correct.
- **Feedback (scene 7).** Thyroid hormones inhibit TRH secretion at the hypothalamus and TSH secretion at the anterior pituitary. Correct.
- **Why a chain (scene 8).** Amplification: each step releases a larger quantity of hormone than the signal that triggered it (releasing hormones in small amounts; the thyroid's daily output of thyroxine is about 100 micrograms); "each step releases more hormone than the signal it received" is a standard simplification of cascade amplification, true for this axis in mass. Two control points: both the hypothalamus and pituitary respond to feedback. One gland reaching many targets. Science check to confirm the amplification wording, with fallback "so a tiny signal at the top controls a much bigger output at the bottom" (length-neutral).
- **Posterior pituitary (scene 9).** ADH and oxytocin are made in hypothalamic neurons (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei), transported down axons in the stalk, stored in axon terminals in the posterior pituitary and released into the blood when action potentials arrive. ADH increases water reabsorption by the collecting ducts. Correct.
- **Not every gland (scene 10).** Pancreatic islet cells respond directly to blood glucose (and are also influenced by nerves and gut hormones, not spoken). The adrenal medulla is stimulated by sympathetic preganglionic nerve fibres, not by a pituitary hormone. Correct.
- **Blood test (scene 11, 12c).** Primary hypothyroidism: low T4, high TSH. Primary hyperthyroidism (for example Graves' disease, toxic nodules): high T4, suppressed TSH. Taking thyroxine medicine in excess gives the same pattern, so the 12c stem says "takes no medicines". In Graves' disease antibodies stimulate the thyroid, so "making too much without TSH driving it" is correct. Correct.
- **Iodine (12a).** Iodine deficiency reduces thyroid hormone synthesis; reduced negative feedback raises TSH; chronic TSH stimulation enlarges the thyroid (goitre, a grey tag only). In mild deficiency TSH may stay within the reference range; the stem says "too little iodine for many months" and asks for the direction only. Science check to confirm the direction is safe as the marked answer.
