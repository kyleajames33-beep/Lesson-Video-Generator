### 2. optimal, not maximal
> **Arousal** is your general level of activation. Its link with performance is an inverted U. Too little, and an athlete is flat: slow to react and not producing force. Too much, and performance falls again. Attention narrows so far that cues are missed, muscle tension disrupts coordination, decisions get rushed, and fine control breaks down. In between is the **optimal arousal** for that athlete and that task. And the optimum moves. As you saw with skill types in Year 11, a simple, forceful, gross task like a lift tolerates high arousal. A fine, precise task like archery or a putt needs it lower, because tension and narrow attention hurt it first. Experienced athletes often cope with more, and very anxious athletes usually need less. So the powerlifter and the archer want the same thing, their own optimum, from opposite sides. And one fired-up team talk can't suit everyone.

### 3a. stress and eustress
> **Stress** is what happens when the demands of a situation are seen to exceed your resources. The word "seen" matters. Two players facing the same final-minute kick can feel very different stress, because they judge their resources differently. **Eustress** is the same arousal felt as excitement, not threat.

### 3b. anxiety and its two halves
> **Anxiety** is the unpleasant state stress can produce, and it has two halves. **Cognitive** anxiety is thinking: worry, doubt, attention on what might go wrong. **Somatic** anxiety is the body: a pounding heart, tense muscles, shaky breathing. They need different strategies. Give a worrier a breathing exercise, and their heart rate falls, but the worry keeps going. And the aim is never to remove anxiety completely. It's to manage it, and to read those body signals as readiness.

### 4a. two thought strategies: goals and rehearsal
> Match each strategy to a problem, and say how it works. **Goal setting** helps with stress over results you can't control. Set process goals, like completing your routine on every shot, which you control even in a loss. An outcome goal like "win" adds stress, because the opponent has a say. **Mental rehearsal** means imagining a successful performance in detail. It builds confidence and fills attention with the task, so it suits cognitive anxiety.

### 4b. relaxation, concentration and self-talk
> **Relaxation**, slow breathing and progressive muscle relaxation, lowers somatic arousal directly. But use it on a flat athlete and you push them further below their optimum. **Concentration** strategies give attention a fixed target, like a cue word. **Self-talk** can be instructional, naming the key cue, or motivational, reframing nerves as readiness. And all of these are rehearsed in training, not invented in the final minute.

### 5. individual and group sports, and participation
> Individual and group sports create different pressures. An individual athlete carries the result alone, and nobody covers an error. In a team, pressure comes from teammates, selection and a role others rely on. A team room also holds many optimal levels at once, so it needs a shared framework plus individual routines, and teammates themselves become a resource that an individual athlete doesn't have. And half of this dot point is about participation, not just performance. Fear of being judged or of letting a team down is one of the main reasons young people stop playing. So strategies for participation look different: process goals that show improvement without winning, a **mastery climate** where effort and progress are praised, and a clear role for every player.

### 6. investigating it honestly
> The verb is investigate, so evidence is part of the answer. Say what you claim, what evidence would support it, and what the evidence can't settle. Most evidence that a strategy works is self-report, athletes saying how anxious they felt, and people taught a strategy know what answer is expected. And in real sport, a strategy is rarely the only thing that changed.
