## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (22 Oct) against the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing; time-sensitive items are marked.
- **Myrtle rust arrival.** *Austropuccinia psidii* (formerly *Puccinia psidii*) first detected in Australia in April 2010 at a nursery on the NSW Central Coast (Gosford area); spread along the east coast to far north Queensland and Victoria within about three years; later Tasmania and the Northern Territory (both 2015); WA first detection June 2022, East Kimberley, on a paperbark in a wetland (DPIRD media release, 8 July 2022). **Time-sensitive:** narration names no state as still free (a 2024 source, Australasian Plant Pathology s13313-024-00974-8, says all states and territories except South Australia; recheck before voicing and do not narrate SA's status).
- **Origin and hosts.** Native range South and Central America (rust of guava, *Psidium*); the strain in Australia is the pandemic biotype, and its pathway into Australia is unknown, so the narration says "native to", not "came from" (science check finding 4). Infects plants in the Myrtaceae, including eucalypts, paperbarks (*Melaleuca*), tea tree (*Melaleuca alternifolia*) and lemon myrtle (*Backhousia citriodora*); attacks young, soft new growth; yellow powdery pustules. Spread by wind-borne urediniospores, and on infected plant material, clothing and equipment. "Many species had little resistance": susceptibility varies within and between species (site error 6).
- **Spores and water.** Rust urediniospores need free water (dew, rain) on the leaf surface to germinate (site L06 card 2; standard plant pathology); some rusts also germinate in near-saturated air. Revision 2 narration: "need a film of water on the leaf to germinate" (the absolute "only" removed; science check finding 5).
- **Rust and yield.** Rust fungi are biotrophs that draw nutrients from living host cells; pustules reduce green leaf area and photosynthesis and divert sugars, reducing grain fill. "Take sugars from the leaf and destroy green tissue, so the plant makes less food and fills less grain": correct as a summary.
- **Root-knot nematodes.** *Meloidogyne* juveniles induce giant cells and galls that disrupt xylem and phloem in the root; plants wilt and are stunted even in moist soil (site L06 card 2). "Squeeze the xylem ... the plant wilts even in moist soil": correct (phloem also disrupted; not stated).
- **Lemon myrtle and tea tree.** Lemon myrtle is highly susceptible; growers rely on fungicide, and untreated plantations have reported large yield losses (industry reports cite up to 70 per cent; the narration says "can lose much of their crop", not a figure). Tea tree is grown densely, which favours the rust (Queensland Government business.qld.gov.au myrtle rust page; farmonline reports). **Time-sensitive:** fungicide permits change; the narration says only "pay for fungicide".
- **Native guava.** *Rhodomyrtus psidioides*: formerly a common rainforest-margin shrub in NSW and Queensland; Critically Endangered, NSW and Commonwealth (NSW BioNet profile; NSW final determination); confirm the EPBC listing date on SPRAT before writing it anywhere; wild populations of mature trees have collapsed from repeated myrtle rust infection (Carnegie et al., Western Sydney University). Narration: "now critically endangered", with no act or year named (confirmed safe by the science check).
- **Eradication.** Eradication was abandoned in late 2010 to 2011; national management since. "Can't be eradicated": correct as the official position (Plant Health Australia; invasives.org.au).
- **FMD.** Picornavirus (*Aphthovirus*), small (about 25 to 30 nm), non-enveloped and icosahedral, so the house enveloped virus plate is not used for it (grey word tag only); infects cloven-hoofed animals (cattle, sheep, pigs, goats, deer, camelids partly); spread by direct contact, airborne virus, fomites (vehicles, boots, equipment), and feed, including swill; vesicles in the mouth, on the feet and teats; lameness, drooling, reduced feeding, milk yield and weight gain; low mortality in adults, higher in young animals (myocarditis) (WOAH; Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry FMD page). "Most adults survive": correct.
- **UK 2001.** Over six million animals culled (NAO 2002: "culled for disease control purposes or because of welfare problems resulting from the restrictions on animal movements"; Committee of Public Accounts): disease-control culls (infected premises, contiguous and dangerous contacts) plus welfare culls of stock stranded by movement restrictions (about two million under the Livestock Welfare Disposal Scheme, about a third of the total); most were not infected. Narration: "whole herds on infected and neighbouring farms were culled, and animals stranded by movement bans, short of feed and space, were killed for welfare" (science check finding 1). The dot grid's amber share is illustrative; no infected-animal count is stated. Costs: public sector over £3 billion and private sector over £5 billion, about £8 billion (NAO 2002); Thompson et al. (2002) estimated about £3.1 billion lost to agriculture and the food chain and £2.7 to £3.2 billion to tourism. Narration: "about eight billion pounds, and tourism lost about as much as farming and the food industry": correct. Countryside footpaths closed; exports banned.
- **Australia.** FMD-free; last outbreak 1872 (Victoria). **Time-sensitive:** Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry states "Australia is free from FMD" (checked 22 Oct 2026; recheck before voicing). Beef export share (checked 22 Oct 2026 by the science check): about 74 per cent of production in calendar 2024 (MLA-based reporting; up from about 67 per cent in 2022); about 80 per cent in 2024 to 25 (ABARES), forecast 82 per cent for 2025 to 26.
