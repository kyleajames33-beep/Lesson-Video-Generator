## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "How do we prepare a standard solution accurately?" The answer, in order: both n and V in c = n ÷ V must be accurately known (1); so the solid must be a primary standard (2, 3); calculate the mass (4); weigh and record what you have (5); dissolve (6); move every bit of solute into the flask (7); make up to exactly the mark (8 to 10); mix, then calculate the real concentration (11); every error has a direction (12).
- **Opening frame (reconciled by root, 5 November).** v12 opens on m2-v11's actual final frame (empty 250.0 mL flask, label "0.100 mol L⁻¹", top-pan balance "?.??? g", "exactly?" chip). Scene 1 rewrites the label to "0.1000" and cross-fades to the analytical balance on "accurately" (Revision 2: the cue word "precisely" became "accurately", brief 181 finding 1); the "exactly?" chip is rewritten to "accurately known" on "known accurately".
- **Hook (Revision 2 wording).** The four-figure label: an accurately known concentration has to come from an accurately known number of moles in an accurately known volume. "Exactly" is not used for measured quantities: every measurement has an uncertainty, and "precise" keeps the course meaning (agreement of repeated readings). A 3 s prediction comes later, at the step where students most often slip (scene 9: one drop over the mark).
- **Primary standard (site, all four properties with reasons; site trap 3).** Pure and stable in air (so the mass gives the moles); high molar mass (more grams per mole, so the balance's fixed uncertainty is a smaller share: 0.001 g in 2.657 g is about 0.04%, against 0.1% in 1.000 g, shown, not spoken); dissolves completely. NaOH fails (pellets take up water and carbon dioxide while weighed; the readout creeps up). Anhydrous sodium carbonate passes once dried in an oven and cooled protected from moisture in a desiccator (site lists it; Revision 2 wording, brief 181 finding 2; the drying step follows the teacher-approved method, author note b).
- **Atomic masses.** HSC data-sheet values (Na 22.99, C 12.01, O 16.00; root decision m2-1). MM(Na₂CO₃) = 105.99 g mol⁻¹, the site's 105.989 to two decimals; the site's 2.650 g target is unchanged.
- **The case (site worked example 1, turned into a preparation).** Target 250.0 mL of 0.1000 mol L⁻¹: n = 0.02500 mol; m = 2.650 g. The balance reads 2.657 g (illustrative); the student records and uses it (site: "use this value ... not the theoretical target mass"): n = 0.02507 mol, c = 0.1003 mol L⁻¹. On-screen note (Revision 2): "use the mass you actually weighed, not the round target". m2-v13 and m2-v14 continue with this 0.1003 mol L⁻¹ solution.
- **Weighing into a beaker.** The solid is weighed straight into the small beaker it will dissolve in (the existing balance plate shows exactly this), so no solid is left on a weighing boat. The site's weighing-boat version is equally valid; the beaker removes one transfer and matches the plate (author note c).
- **Volumetric flask accuracy.** "About a fifth of a millilitre" for 250 mL (Class A tolerance ±0.12 to ±0.15 mL by standard); measuring cylinder "a millilitre or two" (site). The site's "±0.1 mL or better" is noted (site note 1).
- **Error directions (route stop).** Solute lost (not rinsed, spilt) or volume over the mark: actual c lower than calculated. Volume short of the mark: higher. Flask wet with distilled water: no effect. Damp or impure solid: lower (attempt 2, a transfer case). Not mixed: samples vary (the top is dilute). Each direction is reasoned from c = n ÷ V, not memorised.

## Script and shots

Cue words in **bold** trigger the visual change. Colour key as in Decisions.

### 1. hook: a label you can trust
> Here's a bottle label: sodium carbonate, zero point one zero zero zero moles per litre. Four significant figures. How could anyone know a concentration that **accurately**? A solution like this is called a standard solution, one whose concentration is known accurately. And because c equals n over V, two things have to be accurately known: the moles of solute, and the volume of the **solution**.

Visual: opens on m2-v11's final frame exactly (`chem-y11-m2-v11.md` "Final frame": empty stoppered `m6-volumetric-flask-v1.png` with "250.0 mL" at its mark, label card "Na₂CO₃(aq), 0.100 mol L⁻¹", `y11c-top-pan-balance-v1.png` reading "?.??? g", amber "exactly?" chip, unit ladder small at the top right, "Next" chip); the "Next" chip fades. On "Here's a bottle label" the label card moves to the centre in front of the flask and rewrites "0.100" to "0.1000 mol L⁻¹". On "accurately" the four digits "0.1000" glow in turn (violet pen ring, violet hand tag "4 figures!") and the top-pan balance cross-fades to `m6-analytical-balance-v1.png` (the top-pan plate reads only to 0.01 g). On "known accurately" the amber "exactly?" chip rewrites in hand font to "accurately known (tiny uncertainty)"; gloss chip: "standard solution: a solution whose concentration is accurately known" (36 px). On "solution" the gauge writes at the right: "c = n ÷ V" (56 px) with two open padlocks, "n known?" (teal) and "V known?" (slate blue). Heading writes in the hand font: "Making a standard solution".

### 2. not just any solid
> So we start with a solid we can weigh, but not just any solid. Sodium hydroxide pellets look ideal, yet they soak up water and carbon dioxide from the air while they sit on the balance, so the reading keeps creeping **up**, and part of that mass isn't sodium hydroxide at all. The solid has to be very pure, and stable in air, so that its mass tells you its moles. A solid that passes these tests is called a primary **standard**.

Visual: board clears to the bench. The analytical balance with a watch glass on its pan (`m8-balance-watch-glass-v1.png`) at the left; coded white pellets sit on the watch glass. On "up" the coded readout (52 px) ticks "1.000 g → 1.003 g → 1.007 g" while small blue H₂O and grey CO₂ dots drift onto the pellets (rose hand tag "creeping up!", amber pen ring round the display; rose tag "mass ≠ moles"). A red cross over "NaOH" with a red hand tag "absorbs water and CO₂". On "standard" two criteria cards write: "1. very pure" and "2. stable in air", each with a "because" line: "so the mass gives the moles". Gloss chip: "primary standard: a solid pure and stable enough that its mass gives its moles".

### 3. two more tests, and our choice
> Two more tests help. A high molar mass means you weigh more grams for the same number of moles, so the balance's tiny uncertainty is a smaller share of the **reading**. And it has to dissolve completely in water. Anhydrous sodium carbonate, which has no water in its crystals, passes all four, as long as it's dried in an oven first, and cooled where it's protected from moisture, in a desiccator, before you weigh **it**.

Visual: cards 3 and 4 join: "3. high molar mass: so balance error is a smaller share" and "4. dissolves completely: so all of it is in solution". On "reading" the share bars draw: "0.001 g of 1.000 g = 0.1%" (long rose bar) and "0.001 g of 2.657 g = 0.04%" (short bar), with an amber hand tag "bigger mass, smaller share". Gloss chip "anhydrous: no water in the crystals". The four cards tick against "Na₂CO₃ (anhydrous), MM 105.99 g mol⁻¹". On "dried in an oven" the painted drying oven (`m8-drying-oven-v1.png`) appears small at the left; on "desiccator" it cross-fades to the painted desiccator (`m8-desiccator-v1.png`, uniform scale) with gloss chip "desiccator: a sealed jar with a drying agent, so the solid cools without picking up water" (30 px) and a teal pen ring round the drying beads. On "it" a small grey tag "teacher-approved drying method; weigh promptly" sits under the cards.

### 4. how much to weigh
> We want two hundred and fifty millilitres of zero point one zero zero zero moles per litre. First the moles. n equals c times V, with the volume in litres, so zero point one zero zero zero times zero point two five zero zero is zero point zero two five zero zero **moles**. Sodium carbonate's molar mass is one hundred and five point nine nine, so the mass to weigh is two point six five zero **grams**.

### 5. weigh it, and record what you have
> Now weigh it, on a balance that reads to a thousandth of a gram, straight into a small clean beaker. You don't need to hit two point six five zero exactly. Close is fine. The balance reads two point six five seven grams, and that's the number you record and **use**. The concentration comes from the mass you actually have, not the mass you were aiming **for**.

Visual: the balance plate returns with its own small beaker of white powder on the pan; coded display climbs "0.000 g → 2.402 g → 2.657 g" and stops. On "use" an amber notebook card writes "m(Na₂CO₃) = 2.657 g" (48 px) with a tick and an amber pen ring. On "for" the target "2.650 g" row dims to grey with the tag "target only", and a ghost chip "re-weigh until it reads 2.650 g?" is crossed in red, red hand tag "not needed: record what you have".

### 6. dissolve it first
> Next, add about fifty millilitres of distilled water to the beaker, and stir with a glass rod until every crystal has **dissolved**. Distilled water, because tap water carries other ions. And a beaker first, because it's easy to stir, and you can see that nothing is left **behind**.

Visual: the beaker (`m6-beaker-v1.png`, coded white crystals at the bottom, coded water fill to about 50 mL) moves to the centre; the wash bottle (`m6-wash-bottle-v1.png`) beside it with a coded stream. A coded glass rod stirs (rod only, no holder). On "dissolved" the crystals shrink and vanish; the particle lens opens: Na⁺ (violet) and CO₃²⁻ (navy) dots spread through the water. On "behind" the lens sweeps the beaker bottom: clear, tick and teal hand tag "all dissolved".

### 7. every bit into the flask
> Then pour the solution through a funnel into a two hundred and fifty millilitre volumetric flask, a flask made to hold one accurately known volume at its mark. But look at the beaker. Drops of solution still cling to the glass, the rod and the funnel, and those drops hold sodium carbonate. So rinse each one two or three times with distilled water, and let every rinse run into the **flask**. Moving every bit of solute across like this is called a quantitative transfer. Skip it, and some moles stay behind, so the real concentration ends up lower than you **calculate**.

Visual: the funnel (`m6-funnel-v1.png`) sits in the neck of the volumetric flask (`m6-volumetric-flask-v1.png`, stopper lifted off by code and set aside); the beaker tilts by code and a coded stream runs down the funnel; the flask fills to the bulb. Gloss chip "volumetric flask: holds one accurately known volume at its mark". On "cling" the particle lens shows a thin film on the beaker wall still holding a few dots (rose pen ring, rose hand tag "solute left here"). Three coded wash-bottle streams rinse beaker, rod and funnel in turn; on "flask" the last dots ride the rinse into the flask and the lens shows the film empty. Gloss chip "quantitative transfer: every bit of solute into the flask". On "calculate" the gauge's "n known" padlock clicks shut (teal), teal hand tag "every grain in"; a small rose card "rinses skipped → n lower → c lower" files to a waiting "lower" tray at the right edge.

### 8. make it up to the mark
> Now add distilled water until the level is close to the line on the neck. Then switch to a dropper, and add drop by drop until the bottom of the curved surface, called the meniscus, sits exactly on the **mark**, viewed at eye level. The neck is narrow, so a tiny volume moves the level a long way, and that's what makes the flask so accurate. A measuring cylinder can be out by a millilitre or two. This flask is good to about a fifth of a **millilitre**.
