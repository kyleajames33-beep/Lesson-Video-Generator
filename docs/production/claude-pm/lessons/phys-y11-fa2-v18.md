### 2. rays, the normal and the angles
> First, some names. A ray is a line with an arrow, showing which way the light travels. The dashed line is the normal. It's drawn at right angles to the mirror, at the point where the ray hits. The angle of incidence is between the incoming ray and the normal. The angle of reflection is between the reflected ray and the normal. Both are measured from the normal, not from the mirror. The normal is at right angles to any surface, even a curved one, so it's the line that works **everywhere**.

Visual: the beam simplifies to a coded ray with an amber-outlined arrowhead (gloss "ray: a line with an arrow showing which way the light travels"). On "the normal" the dashed line darkens with a right-angle mark at the mirror (gloss "normal: at right angles to the surface, where the ray hits"). On "angle of incidence" the 40° arc fills pale navy, labelled "θi, angle of incidence"; on "angle of reflection" an empty arc on the other side, labelled "θr, angle of reflection". On "even a curved one" an inset: a coded curved surface with three normals at different points, each at right angles to the surface there; on "everywhere" the inset fades.

### 3. the law
> Here's the rule. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence. So forty degrees in means forty degrees out, on the other side of the normal. At twenty degrees, it's twenty out. At sixty, it's sixty. And the incoming ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same flat plane, here, the paper. That's the law of **reflection**.

Visual: on "equals" a row at the right "angle of reflection = angle of incidence" and boxed "θr = θi (from the normal)". On "reflection" the law panel locks at the right edge.

### 4. light as a wave: wavefronts
> But why? To see the cause, think of light as a wave. A wavefront is a line joining points of a wave that are in step, like a line of crests. Rays point the way the wave travels, so they're at right angles to the wavefronts. In a ripple tank, a vibrating bar makes straight water waves. They hit a straight barrier at an angle and bounce off. The reflected wavefronts leave at the same angle they **arrived**.

Visual: board change: the ripple tank plate (`phys-y11-ripple-tank-v1.png`) three-quarter view, its straight vibrating bar at one side and a straight metal barrier set at an angle (gloss "ripple tank: a shallow tray of water; a vibrating bar makes waves we watch from above"). On "line of crests" the camera moves to a coded top view (tag "the pattern projected below the tank"): straight navy crest lines march from the bar (gloss "wavefront: a line joining points in step, like a line of crests"). On "right angles" amber-outlined ray arrows draw across the crests with right-angle marks. On "bounce off" the crests reach the barrier and reflect (`WavefrontReflect`); on "arrived" the angle between the incoming crests and the barrier and between the reflected crests and the barrier pulse with the same navy fill.

### 5. why the angles match
> Watch one wavefront. Its near end hits the barrier first and starts heading back out. Its far end still has this distance to go. The wave travels at the same speed before and after, because it's still in the same water. So while the far end covers this distance, the reflected wave from the near end covers exactly the same distance. That makes these two triangles identical. They share this long side along the barrier, they each have a right angle, and these sides are equal. So the wavefront leaves at the same angle it came in, and so do the rays. Light's wavefronts do the same at a **mirror**.

Visual: camera continue. All crests fade except one, AB (the letters small and grey). On "near end" A touches the barrier (a dot). On "this distance" a dimension line from B to the barrier at C pulses (tick mark). On "same speed" a grey tag "same medium, same speed". On "covers exactly the same distance" B slides to C while a wavelet circle grows from A to the same radius, AD, with the same tick mark; the reflected wavefront CD draws tangent to the circle. On "identical" the triangles ABC and CDA fill pale navy; on "long side" AC pulses; on "right angle" right-angle marks at B and D; on "these sides are equal" the matching ticks on BC and AD pulse. On "same angle" the two wavefront-to-barrier angles pulse equal; on "the rays" ray arrows draw at right angles to AB and CD, with the normal at the barrier, and θi and θr pulse equal (author note b). On "mirror" a small inset of the mirror diagram from 3 with the same equal arcs.

### 7. worked example: converting to the normal
> It's sixty-five degrees. Task: the ray makes twenty-five degrees with the surface. Find the angle of reflection. The law uses angles from the normal, and the normal is at right angles to the surface. So the angle of incidence is ninety minus twenty-five, sixty-five **degrees**. The angle of reflection equals it, so it's also sixty-five **degrees**. Check: a ray that skims close to the surface makes a big angle with the normal, so sixty-five makes **sense**.

Visual: on "sixty-five" the "65°?" chip ticks and "25°?" takes a scribble cross. Clear change to the calculation layout with the diagram small at the left. Task strip "Find θr". ## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A ray of light strikes a plane mirror, making an angle of 38° with the surface of the mirror. (a) Determine the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection. (b) Calculate the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray."
Model answer: (a) Angles are measured from the normal, which is at 90° to the surface, so θi = 90° − 38° = 52°; by the law of reflection θr = θi = 52°. (b) The incident and reflected rays are on opposite sides of the normal, so the angle between them is 52° + 52° = 104°. Classic mistake (crossed): "θr = 38°".

**Attempt 2 (scene 15; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested).** "At night, a wet road shows clear reflections of street lights, but a dry road does not. (a) Use the law of reflection to explain the difference. A labelled ray diagram may help. (b) State whether the law of reflection applies to the dry road."
Model answer: (a) A film of water fills the road's small bumps, giving a smooth surface whose normals all point the same way; parallel rays from a street light meet it at the same angle of incidence and leave at the same angle of reflection, so they stay parallel and a clear image of the light forms (specular reflection). The dry road's surface is rough, with tiny surfaces facing many directions, so the normals point in many directions; parallel rays leave in many directions (diffuse reflection) and no clear image forms. (b) Yes: at every point of the dry road, the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence measured from that point's own normal; only the directions of the normals differ. Classic mistake (crossed): "the dry road doesn't obey the law of reflection".
