### 2. naming sides and angles
> First, some names. A triangle with no right angle is called a non-right-angled triangle. We label its corners with capital letters, A, B and C. Each capital also names the angle at that corner. The side across from each corner gets the same letter, in lower case. So side a is opposite angle A, side b is opposite angle B, and side c is opposite angle **C**. A side and the angle opposite it belong together. Getting those pairs right is most of the **work**.

### 3. the sine rule in words
> Here's the sine rule. Take any side, and divide it by the sine of the angle opposite it. Do that for each of the three pairs, and you get the same number every time. Watch the meters. The corner moves, the triangle changes shape, and the three numbers change, but they always stay equal to each **other**. In symbols, a over sine A equals b over sine B equals c over sine **C**.

### 4. why it works
> Why does that happen? Drop a height from corner C, straight down to side c. On the left, there's a right-angled triangle, so the height is b times sine A. On the right, the same height is a times sine B. It's the same height, so b sine A equals a sine B. Divide both sides by sine A and sine B, and a over sine A equals b over sine **B**.

### 5. a matched pair, unknown on top
> To start the rule, you need a matched pair, a side and the angle opposite it, both known. Say angle A is forty degrees, angle B is seventy-five degrees, and side a is twelve. Find b. Twelve and forty degrees are the matched pair. Side b faces the seventy-five degree angle. So b over sine seventy-five degrees equals twelve over sine forty degrees. Put the unknown on top, then multiply both sides by sine seventy-five degrees. So b is twelve times sine seventy-five degrees, over sine forty degrees, about eighteen point zero **three**. Check, seventy-five degrees is bigger than forty, so b should be bigger than twelve. It **is**.

### 6. no pair yet: the third angle first
> Sometimes there's no matched pair yet. Angle A is fifty-two degrees, angle B is sixty-one degrees, and side c is twenty. Find a. Side c faces angle C, and C isn't given. But the angles of a triangle add to a hundred and eighty degrees, so C is a hundred and eighty minus fifty-two minus sixty-one, sixty-seven **degrees**. Now twenty and sixty-seven degrees are a matched pair. Side a faces fifty-two degrees, so a is twenty times sine fifty-two degrees, over sine sixty-seven degrees, about seventeen point one **two**.

### 7. worked example: the river
> Back to the river. Find the distance from Q to the tree, R. The five hundred metres faces the angle at the tree, which isn't given. So the third angle comes first. The angle at R is a hundred and eighty minus sixty minus eighty-five, thirty-five **degrees**. Now five hundred metres and thirty-five degrees are the matched pair. Q R faces the sixty degree angle at P. So Q R is five hundred times sine sixty degrees, over sine thirty-five degrees, about seven hundred and fifty-five **metres**.
