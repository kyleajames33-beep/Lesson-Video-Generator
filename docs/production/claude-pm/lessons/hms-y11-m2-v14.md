### 2. heart rate, in three parts
> Start with heart rate, because it changes in three parts. First, before you move: the **anticipatory rise**. Expecting the effort, the brain's sympathetic nerves and the adrenaline they release speed the heart up. That's the swimmer on the block, and it's useful, because blood's already flowing faster when the work starts. Second, at the start, a steep rise, as the parasympathetic brake on the heart comes off. Third, at a steady pace, a plateau, where supply meets demand. All of this is an **immediate response**, a change during one session that reverses afterwards. A lower resting heart rate after months of training is an **adaptation**, and answering one question with the other loses the marks.

### 3. ventilation, and what really drives it
> Next, **ventilation rate**: the air you move each minute, breathing rate times **tidal volume**, the size of each breath. At rest, that's about fifteen breaths of half a litre, around seven litres a minute. In hard exercise it can pass a hundred. At moderate effort, you mostly breathe deeper. Near maximum, depth tops out, so you breathe faster. And here's the surprise. Breathing doesn't rise because oxygen runs low. As you saw earlier, the brain's respiratory centre sets breathing, and it responds mainly to rising carbon dioxide, detected by chemoreceptors. But in the first seconds, signals from the brain and the moving joints give it a head start. At high intensity, rising acidity pushes it further. That's also why hyperventilating before a swim is dangerous. Carbon dioxide, not low oxygen, is what makes you want to breathe. Overbreathing blows it off without adding much oxygen, so the urge to breathe comes too late, and a swimmer can black out underwater.

### 4. stroke volume, and its ceiling
> Stroke volume is the blood the left ventricle pushes out each beat. It rises from about seventy millilitres toward one hundred and ten, because more blood comes back to the heart. That's **venous return**. Working muscles squeeze the veins, and deeper breathing helps pull blood back too. So the ventricle fills further, stretches more, and contracts harder. But at around forty to sixty per cent of maximum aerobic capacity, stroke volume stops rising. The heart's beating so fast that there's less time to fill between beats.

### 5. cardiac output, worked
> Earlier, you met **cardiac output**: heart rate times stroke volume. Now watch it in exercise. At rest, seventy beats of seventy millilitres is four point nine litres a minute. At moderate effort, one hundred and thirty times one hundred and ten is fourteen point three. At maximum, one hundred and ninety-five times one hundred and ten is twenty-one point five. Look at the last two rows. Stroke volume hasn't changed, yet output has risen by half. Once stroke volume stops, heart rate carries the whole rise. That's how the swimmer's heart keeps delivering more.

### 6. lactate, and the threshold
> Lactate is made all the time, even at rest, and removed all the time, by the heart, the liver and less active muscle. So blood lactate shows the balance, not just production. At rest it sits around one to two millimoles per litre. As intensity rises, production eventually outruns removal, and lactate starts to climb steeply. That point is the **lactate threshold**. In an untrained person, it's at about fifty to sixty per cent of maximum aerobic capacity. In a trained endurance athlete, it's higher, about seventy to eighty per cent. Two cyclists at the same power show why it matters. One has a lactate of two point one, the other six point eight. The first is below her own threshold, and can keep going. The second is above his, and has minutes left.

### 7. five responses, one demand
> These aren't five separate facts. They're one answer to one demand: get more oxygen to the working muscle, and carbon dioxide away. Ventilation brings more air in. Stroke volume and heart rate raise cardiac output, and blood is sent toward the working muscle. And lactate is the report card. Below the threshold, delivery is winning. Above it, it isn't, and the clock has started. Notice that stroke volume plateaus and lactate turns at different intensities.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Heart rate (1, 2).** Anticipatory rise via sympathetic stimulation and adrenaline; rapid onset from vagal withdrawal and sympathetic drive; steady state at constant submaximal work; the swimmer's 58 and 108 bpm (site, illustrative); resting heart rate falling with training as an adaptation (72 → 58 over 8 weeks, the site's example).
- **Ventilation (3).** Hyperventilation before underwater swimming lowers carbon dioxide (the main resting drive to breathe) without meaningfully raising oxygen stores, so blackout can occur before the urge to breathe returns (site L14 card 03). Ventilation = breathing rate × tidal volume; rest about 12 to 15 breaths × 0.5 L (6 to 7.5 L/min; the model uses 7); maximal values above 100 L/min in trained adults (adolescents often lower; the model's 107 is illustrative); tidal volume rises first, then frequency; rapid onset from central command and muscle afferents; CO₂ and, at high intensity, acidosis.
- **Stroke volume (4).** Rest about 70 mL, exercise about 100 to 120 mL; venous return via the muscle and respiratory pumps; Frank-Starling filling and stronger contraction; plateau at about 40 to 60% of maximum from reduced filling time in untrained people; continued rise in elite endurance athletes.
- **Cardiac output (5).** 70 × 70 = 4,900 mL; 130 × 110 = 14,300 mL; 195 × 110 = 21,450 mL; 21.45 ÷ 14.3 = 1.5.
- **Lactate (6).** Continuous production and clearance (heart, liver, less active muscle); rest about 1 to 2 mmol/L; lactate threshold about 50 to 60% of maximum aerobic capacity untrained, about 70 to 80% trained (root ruling 3; elite values can be higher); the cyclists' 2.1 and 6.8 mmol/L (site's WE b2).
