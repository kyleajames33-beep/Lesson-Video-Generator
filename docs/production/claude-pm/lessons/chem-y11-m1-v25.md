### 1. hook: same atoms, different everything
> Here's a puzzle. The tip of a pencil is mostly graphite, and it's soft enough to rub off onto **paper**. A diamond is hard enough to scratch glass. Both are made of nothing but carbon **atoms**. And sand, which is mostly silicon dioxide, is hard too, even though carbon dioxide, from the same group, is a gas you breathe **out**. The difference is in how the atoms are joined.

### 2. molecular or network
> Most covalent substances we've met so far are molecular. They're made of separate molecules, like carbon dioxide, with strong covalent bonds inside each molecule, and only weak attractions between the **molecules**. A covalent network is different. Covalent bonds join the atoms into one continuous structure, in layers or in 3D, right through the **crystal**. There are no separate molecules. So to melt it, you have to break covalent bonds, and that takes a lot of **energy**. A 3D network is also hard to scratch, for the same reason.

### 3. diamond: four bonds, locked
> Start with diamond. Each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds, to four other carbons, pointing to the corners of a **tetrahedron**, the shape we saw for methane. That pattern repeats in every direction, so a diamond is one giant 3D **network**. To scratch it, you'd have to break many strong bonds, so diamond is the hardest natural substance, a ten on the Mohs scale, which ranks minerals by which one scratches which. And all four outer electrons on each carbon are used in bonds, so none are free to move. Diamond doesn't conduct **electricity**.

### 4. graphite: three bonds and one free
> Graphite uses the same atoms differently. Each carbon bonds to only three others, in flat sheets of hexagons, like chicken wire. That leaves one outer electron on each carbon, and those electrons are delocalised, free to move along the whole **sheet**. So graphite conducts electricity along its **layers**. Between the sheets there are no covalent bonds, only weak attractions, so the sheets slide over each other easily. That's why graphite feels slippery, and why it rubs off as you **write**.

### 5. soft, but not easy to melt
> Graphite is soft, but it's not easy to melt. To turn graphite into a liquid or a gas, you'd have to break the strong covalent bonds inside every **sheet**. So graphite stays solid to well above three thousand degrees **Celsius**. Soft and slippery describes how the layers slide. It doesn't mean the bonds are **weak**.

### 6. allotropes
> Diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon. Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element, in the same physical state. Carbon has others too. In buckminsterfullerene, sixty carbon atoms join into a hollow ball, like a soccer **ball**. Each ball is a separate molecule, held to the next by weak attractions, so this form of carbon is soft, and it even dissolves in some **solvents**. Same element, different arrangement, different **properties**.

### 8. silicon dioxide: a network like diamond
> Hard, and it doesn't conduct. Those bonds link up in every direction, so silicon dioxide is a 3D covalent network, much like **diamond**. Quartz, a crystal of silicon dioxide, is a seven on the Mohs scale, hard enough to scratch glass. It melts at about seventeen hundred degrees **Celsius**, and its outer electrons aren't free to move through the crystal, so nothing can carry a current. The formula S i O two isn't one molecule. It's the ratio of atoms, one silicon for every two oxygens, across the whole **crystal**.

### 9. why carbon dioxide is a gas
> So why is carbon dioxide a gas? Carbon makes double bonds to two oxygens, and then it's finished. Each O C O unit is a separate molecule, and the molecules attract each other only weakly. Solid carbon dioxide, dry ice, turns straight to gas at about minus seventy-eight **degrees**. Silicon makes four single bonds instead, so the network never closes off. Same group, same formula pattern, but one is molecular, and the other is a **network**.

> So for carbon, count the bonds. Four bonds lock it, so it's hard and doesn't conduct. Three bonds leave one electron free, so the layers slide and it **conducts**. And for any substance, ask first: is it separate molecules, or a network?

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style; Revision 2).** "Silicon carbide (SiC) is used in cutting discs. In its structure, each silicon atom is covalently bonded to four carbon atoms and each carbon atom to four silicon atoms, in a continuous three-dimensional arrangement. Explain why silicon carbide is very hard and heat-resistant. (3 marks)"
Model answer: SiC is a covalent network solid: strong covalent bonds extend in three dimensions throughout the crystal, with no separate molecules (1). Scratching or deforming it requires breaking many strong, directional covalent bonds, so it is very hard (1). Breaking the structure down by heating also requires breaking these covalent bonds throughout, which needs a very large amount of energy, so it stays solid to very high temperatures (it decomposes near 2700 °C rather than melting; not required) (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "weak forces between SiC molecules".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style).** "Graphite is used to make electrodes and as a dry lubricant in locks. Diamond is used to tip drill bits. Explain these uses with reference to the structure and bonding of each allotrope. (4 marks)"
Model answer: In graphite each carbon is covalently bonded to three others in flat layers, leaving one delocalised electron per carbon (1); these electrons move along the layers, so graphite conducts electricity and can be used as an electrode (1). The layers are held together only by weak dispersion forces, so they slide over each other easily, making graphite a lubricant (1). In diamond each carbon is covalently bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional network, so it is extremely hard and suits drill bits (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "graphite is soft because its covalent bonds are weak".
