### 1. hook: same atoms, different everything
> Here's a puzzle. The tip of a pencil is mostly graphite, and it's soft enough to rub off onto **paper**. A diamond is hard enough to scratch glass. Both are made of nothing but carbon **atoms**. And sand, which is mostly silicon dioxide, is hard too, even though carbon dioxide, from the same group, is a gas you breathe **out**. The difference is in how the atoms are joined.

Visual: opens on v24's final frame (Decisions). On "paper" a coded grey pencil line draws beside the pencil plate. On "atoms" small lenses over the pencil and the diamond both show only charcoal "C" atoms, tag "both pure carbon". On "out" a grey CO₂ chip with a breath-like wisp of coded molecules drifts up beside the quartz plate, tag "same group: C and Si". The heading writes on: "Same atoms, different structures".

### 2. molecular or network
> Most covalent substances we've met so far are molecular. They're made of separate molecules, like carbon dioxide, with strong covalent bonds inside each molecule, and only weak attractions between the **molecules**. A covalent network is different. Every atom is joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds, in one continuous structure through the whole **crystal**. There are no separate molecules. So to scratch it or melt it, you have to break covalent bonds, and that takes a lot of **energy**.

Visual: board splits. Right lens: on "crystal" a network fragment draws outward bond by bond until it fills the lens, tag "covalent network: bonded throughout". On "energy" a chip writes "network: scratching or melting breaks covalent bonds", and a crossed chip "all covalent substances melt easily" fades in under the left lens (caption "only molecular ones").

### 3. diamond: four bonds, locked
> Start with diamond. Each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds, to four other carbons, pointing to the corners of a **tetrahedron**, the shape we saw for methane. That pattern repeats in every direction, so a diamond is one giant 3D **network**. To scratch it, you'd have to break many strong bonds, so diamond is the hardest natural substance, a ten on the Mohs scale, which ranks minerals by which one scratches which. And all four outer electrons on each carbon are used in bonds, so none are free to move. Diamond doesn't conduct **electricity**.

Visual: the diamond plate at the left; the lens shows one carbon with its four bonds drawing in turn, numbered 1 to 4; on "tetrahedron" a faint tetrahedron outline and "109.5°" (v23). On "network" the fragment grows to the turning diamond lattice. On the third sentence a coded scratch line tries to cross the diamond plate and stops (red stop mark); the Mohs bar draws with "diamond 10". On "electricity" the four bonds on the ringed carbon glow, tag "all 4 outer electrons in bonds: no free electrons", and the property table row writes "diamond: hardest; does not conduct; very high (above 3500 °C)".

### 4. graphite: three bonds and one free
> Graphite uses the same atoms differently. Each carbon bonds to only three others, in flat sheets of hexagons, like chicken wire. That leaves one outer electron on each carbon, and those electrons are delocalised, free to move along the whole **sheet**. So graphite conducts electricity along its **layers**. Between the sheets there are no covalent bonds, only weak attractions, so the sheets slide over each other easily. That's why graphite feels slippery, and why it rubs off as you **write**.

Visual: the pencil plate at the left; the lens shows one flat hexagonal sheet; one carbon is ringed, its three bonds numbered 1 to 3 and a fourth blue dot beside it. On "sheet" the blue dots from every carbon join a drift across the sheet. On "layers" two more sheets stack below at the wide spacing; the drift runs along each sheet, never across the gap, tag "conducts along the layers". On the fifth sentence grey dotted lines appear between sheets, tag "weak attractions between layers (dispersion forces: next video)"; the top sheet slides sideways. On "write" the pencil line thickens, and the table row writes "graphite: soft, slippery; conducts along layers".

### 5. soft, but not easy to melt
> Here's a trap. Graphite is soft, but it's not easy to melt. To turn graphite into a liquid or a gas, you'd have to break the strong covalent bonds inside every **sheet**. So graphite, like diamond, stays solid to well above three thousand degrees **Celsius**. Soft and slippery describes how the layers slide. It doesn't mean the bonds are **weak**.

Visual: the graphite stack turns; on "sheet" the in-sheet bonds glow ink-bright while the dotted interlayer lines stay grey, tags "strong: inside sheets" and "weak: between sheets". On "Celsius" the table's graphite cell writes "very high (above 3500 °C)". On "weak" a chip "graphite is soft, so its bonds are weak" fades in and is crossed (caption "weak between layers only").

### 6. allotropes
> Diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon. Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element, in the same physical state. Carbon has others too. In buckminsterfullerene, sixty carbon atoms join into a hollow ball, like a soccer **ball**. Each ball is a separate molecule, held to the next by weak attractions, so this form of carbon is soft, and it even dissolves in some **solvents**. Same element, different arrangement, different **properties**.

### 8. silicon dioxide: a network like diamond
> Hard, and it doesn't conduct. Those bonds link up in every direction, so silicon dioxide is a 3D covalent network, much like **diamond**. Quartz, a crystal of silicon dioxide, is a seven on the Mohs scale, hard enough to scratch glass. It melts at about seventeen hundred degrees **Celsius**, and every outer electron is held in a bond, so nothing is free to carry a current. The formula S i O two isn't one molecule. It's the ratio of atoms, one silicon for every two oxygens, across the whole **crystal**.

### 9. why carbon dioxide is a gas
> So why is carbon dioxide a gas? Carbon makes double bonds to two oxygens, and then it's finished. Each O C O unit is a separate molecule, and the molecules attract each other only weakly. Solid carbon dioxide, dry ice, turns straight to gas at about minus seventy-eight **degrees**. Silicon makes four single bonds instead, so the network never closes off. Same group, same formula pattern, but one is molecular, and the other is a **network**.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 52 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Covalent network: atoms covalently bonded throughout one continuous structure, no separate molecules. Scratching or melting breaks covalent bonds: very hard, very high melting point.
2. Diamond: each C bonded to 4 others (tetrahedral, 3D): hardest natural substance; no delocalised electrons, so does not conduct.

Explain why silicon carbide is very hard and has a very high melting point. (3 marks)"
Model answer: SiC is a covalent network solid: strong covalent bonds extend in three dimensions throughout the crystal, with no separate molecules (1). Scratching or deforming it requires breaking many strong, directional covalent bonds, so it is very hard (1). Melting also requires breaking these covalent bonds throughout the structure, which needs a very large amount of energy, so the melting point is very high (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "weak forces between SiC molecules".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style).** "Graphite is used to make electrodes and as a dry lubricant in locks. Diamond is used to tip drill bits. Explain these uses with reference to the structure and bonding of each allotrope. (4 marks)"
Model answer: In graphite each carbon is covalently bonded to three others in flat layers, leaving one delocalised electron per carbon (1); these electrons move along the layers, so graphite conducts electricity and can be used as an electrode (1). The layers are held together only by weak dispersion forces, so they slide over each other easily, making graphite a lubricant (1). In diamond each carbon is covalently bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional network, so it is extremely hard and suits drill bits (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "graphite is soft because its covalent bonds are weak".
