### 2. immediate management, and one mechanism to avoid
> First, the first forty-eight to seventy-two hours. For a soft tissue injury, use **RICER**. Rest: stop, because using it increases bleeding into the tissue. Ice, wrapped, for about twenty minutes at a time: it narrows blood vessels, which eases pain and may help limit swelling. Compression: a firm bandage, above and below the injury, leaving less space for swelling. Elevation: raise it above the heart to help drainage. And referral: get it assessed by a qualified professional, because a sideline check isn't a diagnosis. Now what to avoid: heat, alcohol, running and massage. They share one mechanism. Each increases blood flow to an area that's already bleeding, so swelling gets worse. For a suspected fracture or dislocation, it's different. Don't move the player unnecessarily, support the injury in the position you found it, and call for medical help.

### 3. rehabilitation: four stages, each earning the next
> Rehabilitation isn't a menu. It's a sequence, and the athlete moves on when they tolerate the current stage, not when the calendar says. First, **progressive mobilisation**: gentle movement in a pain-free range, started as early as the injury allows, so the joint doesn't stiffen. Then **graduated exercise**: stretching to restore full range, then conditioning to rebuild strength, power and endurance in the injured area. Then training: sport-specific skills, from controlled drills up to full-speed, unpredictable, contested work. And throughout, keep the rest of the body fit. As you saw earlier, fitness fades fast, so an athlete with an injured ankle can still train the upper body and the other leg, or cross-train. Heat and cold have a timing rule too. Cold early, mainly for pain. Heat later, once bleeding has stopped, to relax muscle and help movement before rehab work.

### 4. return to play: criteria, not a calendar
> The most common mistake is deciding by time. "Six weeks for a hamstring" is an average across many people, not a fact about this one. Decide against criteria. No pain, at rest or in sport movements. Full range, matching the other side. Strength close to the other side, measured, not guessed, because strength gaps are linked to re-injury. Skill under fatigue and pressure, because games aren't played fresh. **Psychological readiness**: confidence to do the movement that caused the injury, because a player protecting a limb moves differently. And clearance from a qualified professional. Back to the hook. No pain, full range and jogging tick some boxes. Measured strength, fatigue, confidence and clearance are still missing. So not yet. And re-injury risk is highest just after a return, so a **graded return**, with fewer minutes and no contact at first, beats all-or-nothing.

### 5. policy, and different sports
> Return-to-play decisions happen under pressure, by people who want a result, often with incomplete information. That's why there's a **policy**: it makes the decision in advance, while everyone is calm. The same principles then apply differently. In contact and collision sports, the tissue has to tolerate impact, and head injuries are a serious risk, so policies are stricter, with set stand-down periods after concussion and non-contact stages before full contact. And in school and community sport, there's usually no medical staff, less monitoring, and athletes who are still growing. So the policy should be more cautious, not more relaxed.

### 6. who decides
> Responsibility is shared, but not equally. The health professional assesses and clears the player, so the medical decision is theirs, partly because they have the fewest conflicts of interest. The athlete reports symptoms honestly and does the rehab, but wants to play and may play down symptoms, especially if young. The coach carries out the decision and manages the graded return, but doesn't override it, even with selection pressure. And the club or school sets and enforces the policy, and carries the duty of care. So when people disagree, the policy decides, and the medical assessment governs the medical question. A player's wish to play doesn't create clearance.

### 8d. feedback, item two
> No. Being knocked out means a suspected concussion, and under the national youth guideline there's no competition for at least twenty-one days after the injury, whatever any note says. A parents' note is consent, not clearance, so a doctor decides. The school's policy should require that clearance, then a graded return.
