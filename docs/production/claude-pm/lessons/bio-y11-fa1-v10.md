## Decisions

- **Main teaching move: a demonstration turns an invisible particle process into a change you can see or measure, and the particle model explains that change.** Every set-up is taught in the same fixed frame on screen, "Set up / Measure / Means", introduced in the hook. Students who keep that frame can outline any diffusion or osmosis demonstration; the site's Activity 1 asks exactly this (method, what you would measure, expected result).
- **The hook's surprise (scene 1).** A bag of sugar water in plain water gets heavier although nothing was poured in; colour enters a block of jelly with nobody stirring. Both are changes with no visible cause, which is the problem the video solves. The v09 definitions are not restated as a separate scene: each is applied where its demonstration is explained (scenes 4 and 6), with v09's title on a grey tag.
- **Diffusion demonstration: an agar jelly cube in dilute permanganate solution (scenes 2 to 4; Revision 2).** The open-beaker crystal drop was removed after the science check: a dense crystal that dissolves creates its own bulk currents, so still water and room temperature do not isolate diffusion (a 2025 experimental analysis, J. Chem. Educ. 10.1021/acs.jchemed.5c00262, says so; RMS diffusion distance in 20 minutes is only about 2 mm). The replacement is the single-size subset of the published Nuffield agar method (printed p. 210): one 1.0 cm cube immersed in permanganate solution for 5 minutes, removed, blotted and cut in half to inspect the penetration. Jelly holds the water still, so currents cannot carry colour through it. The teacher prepares the jelly and a dilute solution and cuts the cube (hot agar, blade and stock-chemical controls). The source gives no concentration or rim depth, so the plates are illustrations, not data, and the narration says so; no cube-size comparison or surface-area calculation (FA2).
- **What to measure for diffusion (scene 3).** The penetration depth of the colour on the cut face, measured with a ruler. No diffusion numbers are spoken or drawn; the coded ruler and bracket carry no readings, and the learner records what they actually see. The interval (5 minutes) is the method time, not a promised outcome.
- **Particle explanation (scene 4).** A coded zoom into the jelly: dissolved purple particles move randomly through the water held in the gel; there are more outside the cube than deep inside, so more enter than return; net movement down the gradient; the coloured rim is evidence the particles entered even though the water could not swirl. The "no net movement at an even concentration" idea is v09's (not repeated here; it stays in the notes). A recall of v09 applied to the jelly, not re-taught.
- **Osmosis demonstration: Visking tubing (scenes 5 and 6).** The site's method: tubing filled with concentrated sugar solution, tied, weighed, placed in distilled water, reweighed after 30 minutes. The video names the sugar as sucrose (table sugar) and adds the details a student needs to write the method: soaking the tubing, knotting both ends, rinsed and blotted the same way before every weighing.
- **Science correction carried: sugar and Visking tubing (scene 5; site finding 1 below).** The site's short-answer model says "water molecules are small enough to cross but sugar molecules are not". Standard dialysis tubing lets glucose through (the classic starch and glucose Visking demonstration depends on it) and lets sucrose through slowly, and no universal 30-minute retention rate is established for unspecified tubing, concentration, area and temperature. Saying "sugar can't cross" or "barely gets through" would claim more than the evidence supports, so the narration says sucrose molecules are much bigger and "cross far more slowly in this set-up", and the result is described as "during this observation, the difference drives net water inward". Author note: pilot the actual tubing and interval before filming or teaching it, and keep any faster-water explanation tied to that set-up.
- **The prediction beat (scene 6).** Before the bag is reweighed, students predict heavier or lighter. 3 s countdown with a "Predict" chip (root decision 7; a quick check, not a practice question).
- **Liquid level as the second measure (scene 7; route stop "or liquid level").** A filled bag tied around a narrow glass tube, clamped upright: the level rises as water enters. Reason given for the narrow tube (a small volume makes a big rise). Measured at timed intervals.
- **Fair test (scene 8).** The site's control (a bag of distilled water, same water, same time) checks for background change from the tubing or procedure; it does not by itself prove that clinging water is equal, so blotting every bag the same way is taught as its own step. The video separates **a control** (an extra set-up without the factor being tested) from **controlled variables** (things kept the same), because students starting Year 11 often merge them; attempt 2's crossed mistake depends on it. Repeats: record leaks, repeat the failed trial, and average the valid repeats (a mean does not correct a known failed seal, and no inconvenient result is silently discarded). The words validity and accuracy are not taught (exam skills series); repeats reduce random scatter, not systematic blotting or calibration error.
- **Percentage change in mass (scene 9).** Bags start at different masses, so the fair comparison is percentage change. One worked case with illustrative numbers (15.0 g to 17.4 g, +16%; control under 1%), laid out as the v06 calculation stack. This stays inside the route stop ("state what to measure ... change in mass"), is the standard way school osmosis results are compared, and its sign rule (positive means mass gain, negative means mass loss; mostly net water movement here because little solute is exchanged) is what attempt 1 tests.
- **Potato cylinders (attempt 1 only).** The site's second osmosis example ("potato cylinders in concentrated sugar solution lose mass") is the unseen transfer case, with the necessary condition supplied on the card ("the solution inside potato cells is more dilute than the sucrose solution"). The words hypotonic, hypertonic, isotonic, turgid, flaccid and plasmolysed are not used anywhere (v11's).
- **Risk table (scene 10).** Three spoken rows, each hazard, harm and control for this practical: potassium permanganate (oxidiser, harmful if swallowed, stains: the teacher prepares the dilute solution and cuts the jelly; eye protection, forceps, keep the solution away from sugar, follow the disposal instructions); glass beakers and the narrow tube (cuts: clamp gently, report breakages); spilt sugar solution (slips: wipe up at once). Teacher-note rows, not shown: the teacher handles the stock chemical and any crystal and the hot agar and cutting blade (heat and blade controls); compatible gloves; storing permanganate away from combustibles and reducing agents; collecting permanganate waste (aquatic toxicity) under the school's procedure. Shown table: only the row being spoken; earlier rows collapse to one-word grey chips (v05's rule). Public NSW guidance does not establish that students may handle the chemical, and the current staff guidance is access-restricted, so the teacher-prepares rule stands (root decision 7).
- **Memory aid (author-written).** "**Diffusion you can see; osmosis you can weigh.**" Each half names the measurement and carries its reason: a coloured solute spreading is visible; water entering or leaving a closed bag changes its mass. Second line on the notes screen: "Add a control. Repeat. Explain with particles." Shown in scene 12 after both demonstrations are explained. The site's L08 memory hook ("A door is not an engine") is about facilitated diffusion, so it belongs to v09. Assumptions under "Memory aid check".
- **Site alignment (L08, kept in substance).** The two demonstrations and their evidence (colour spread; Visking tubing gaining mass as water moves in); "Measuring mass change before and after is the quantitative evidence for osmosis"; the control "tubing in water vs water in water" and repeats; short answer 2 (predict the mass change after 30 minutes and explain it, carried with the correction above); short answer 3 (one control and one repeat measure, and what each contributes; turned into attempt 2 with a new scenario). Not used here: the definition cards and the explain and compare walk-throughs (v09's); facilitated diffusion (v09's); the salty-chips application (root to place); tonicity (v11's).
- **Site errors and gaps found (this read, 2 Nov).** For `../site-corrections-bio-y11.md` (root forwards to Codex; not repeats of logged items).
  1. L08 short answer 2 model answer and its mark guide: "water molecules are small enough to cross but sugar molecules are not, so the sugar stays inside". Standard Visking (dialysis) tubing lets glucose pass and lets sucrose pass slowly; the school demonstration works because water crosses much faster than sucrose. Proposed: "Water molecules cross the tubing much faster than the larger sucrose molecules, so over 30 minutes there is a net movement of water into the tubing and only a little sugar leaks out." **Confirmed by the science check (Revision 2; the tubing and interval still need a pilot).**
  2. L08 card 3, diffusion demo: "spreads colour outward over time with no stirring". Revision 2: an open-water permanganate beaker is not an isolated diffusion test, because a dissolving dense crystal creates its own bulk movement and the diffusion distance in school time is only millimetres (J. Chem. Educ. 2025, 10.1021/acs.jchemed.5c00262). Proposed site change: use a cube of agar jelly (Nuffield, printed p. 210) or state that currents may contribute. Not a confirmed factual error in the site text, a limitation of the demonstration.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) the problem: changes with no visible cause, and the three questions; (2) the agar-cube set-up and why jelly isolates diffusion; (3) what you see and what to measure; (4) the particle explanation (more enter than return); (5) Visking tubing, why water crosses faster than sucrose in this set-up, and the method with reasons; (6) prediction, the result and its explanation; (7) the liquid level measure and why the tube is narrow; (8) a control versus controlled variables, and repeats; (9) percentage change and its sign; (10) risk; attempts; decision rule and handoff; key notes.
- **Learner-visible vocabulary (each with a plain meaning at first use):** potassium permanganate (scene 2, on screen "dilute purple solution, prepared by the teacher"); agar jelly (scene 2, on screen "mostly water held in a gel"); Visking tubing and dialysis tubing (scene 5, "a thin, clear film with tiny pores"); partially permeable (scene 5, recalled, on screen "lets some particles through but not others"); sucrose (scene 5, "ordinary table sugar"); distilled water (scene 5, on screen "pure water"); control (scene 8, "an extra set-up without the factor you're testing"); controlled variables (scene 8, "kept the same"); percentage change in mass (scene 9, worked). Recalled from v09: diffusion, osmosis, concentration gradient, net movement.
- **Attempt systems (unseen, every condition visible, HSC wording, original).**
  - (A1, calculate and explain, 4 marks) A potato cylinder in concentrated sucrose solution for 60 minutes, blotted and weighed before and after: 5.00 g to 4.40 g; the card states that the solution inside potato cells is more dilute than the sucrose solution. (a) Calculate the percentage change in mass. (b) Explain the change using osmosis. Model: (a) (4.40 − 5.00) ÷ 5.00 × 100 = −12% (a 12% decrease); (b) water concentration is higher inside the potato cells than in the sucrose solution, so there is a net movement of water out of the cells, across their partially permeable membranes, into the solution, and the cylinder loses mass. Crossed mistake: dividing by the final mass (−0.60 ÷ 4.40 × 100 ≈ −13.6%). Unseen: a new tissue, a decrease rather than an increase, the condition supplied on the card.
  - (A2, propose and explain, 4 marks) One Visking bag of concentrated sucrose solution, weighed, left in distilled water for 30 minutes, weighed straight out of the water without drying; the student concludes osmosis caused the gain. Propose two changes that make the result stronger evidence for osmosis and explain how each helps. Model (any two): a control bag of distilled water in the same water for the same time (shows the gain needs a concentration difference rather than background change from the tubing or procedure); blot each bag dry the same way before every weighing (clinging water isn't counted as gain); several bags and a mean (one leaky or badly dried bag can't decide the result). Crossed mistake: "Control: keep the temperature the same" (a controlled variable, not a control). Unseen: a new flawed method, not the site's question.
- **Mistake scene:** none separate. Misconceptions addressed: "colour spreading always means diffusion" (2: stirring or currents also spread it, which is why the jelly is used); "particles stop moving when the colour is even" (v09, kept in the notes); "sugar can't cross Visking tubing at all" (5: it crosses far more slowly than water in this set-up); "water moves only one way in osmosis" (6: both ways, more inward); "a control means keeping things the same" (8, A2); "compare raw mass changes between bags" (9); "divide by the final mass" (A1).
- **Legacy logic ported:** none. **Reuse** (no prototype components): the explainer kit (`Plate`, `Callout`, `Mark`, `Countdown`, `HandText`, `DrawPath`, `scribbleEllipse`, `Enter`, `Cam`, `NumberTicker`); v05's step rail, method card, risk table (row at a time), attempt cards and answer table; v06's calculation stack (task, equation card, grouped givens with units, one working line at a time); B6a's `WrongChip`, `GreyTag`, `Cross`. New coded items (lesson-local): the **particle lens** (a circular `Cam` lens on the cube's outer edge with violet particles in random walk through a coded jelly mesh, densest outside; two counters, entering versus returning); the **water and sugar lens** on the tubing wall (blue water dots crossing both ways through gaps, more inward; larger amber sucrose dots nearly all staying inside); the **coded ruler and penetration bracket** on the cut face (no readings drawn); the **balance readout** (coded digits in the blank display of the new balance plate, ticking to each mass); the **liquid column** (a coded blue column inside the painted narrow tube, rising, with a level mark and a coded scale); the **swirl arrows** (terracotta curved arrows for currents in open water, blocked by the jelly mesh); the **percentage stack** with its sign chips.
