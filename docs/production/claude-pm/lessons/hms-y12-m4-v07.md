### 2. reversibility: not everything is lost at once
> **Reversibility** is overload in reverse. Take away the stress, and the body sheds what it's no longer asked to keep, because keeping it costs energy. But things are lost at different rates. Fastest are blood plasma volume and stroke volume, measurably down within one to two weeks, so aerobic performance drops before the muscles have changed at all. Aerobic capacity falls more steadily, losing much of recent gains within four to eight weeks. Strength goes more slowly, and is often largely held for several weeks. Skill and technique are kept longest, for months or years. That last gap matters. A footballer coming back after a long break can still read the game and still do the skills, so they get into positions their fitness can't support. A returning athlete is unfit before they're unskilled, and that's where many comeback injuries start.

### 3. maintenance: reduce volume, protect intensity
> The other half of reversibility is maintenance. Holding an adaptation takes far less work than building it, often around a third of the usual training frequency, as long as intensity stays up. That's a **maintenance load**. So when volume has to fall, protect intensity. Now the rower. Their legs, heart and lungs can still be trained on the bike. That's **cross-training**, training a quality through a different activity. With a few hard bike sessions a week, most of their leg strength and aerobic fitness can be held. And since aerobic fitness is lost first, that's the priority to keep. So the six weeks aren't lost at all. The rower goes back fitter than if they'd rested, though they'll still ease back into rowing.

### 4. variety: three reasons, one direction
> **Variety** is often written off as keeping training fun. Adherence does matter, because a program people quit produces nothing. But there are two physiological reasons too. An unchanging stress stops driving adaptation, which is v-six's plateau from another angle. And repeating one movement loads the same tissues every session, which is how **overuse injuries** like shin pain and stress fractures develop. Here's the catch. Variety pulls against specificity. The answer is to vary the means, and hold the direction. A swimmer varies stroke, distance, equipment and intensity, but keeps training in the water. In aerobic training, variety often means changing surface or cross-training to spread impact. In strength training, it means changing exercises or tempo to keep a lift progressing.

### 5. the warm-up, with its mechanisms
> A **warm-up** is a graded rise in temperature, circulation, range and readiness. Stage one is five to ten minutes of light, whole-body activity. It raises muscle temperature, heart rate and blood flow. Warmer muscle contracts and relaxes faster and is more pliable, and blood gives up oxygen to muscle more readily when it's warm. The heart also isn't asked for a sudden maximum from rest. Stage two is dynamic stretching through the ranges the session will use, as in v-five. Stage three is sport-specific skills at rising intensity, rehearsing movements and decisions. Stage four is mental preparation. For strength training, there's one more piece: warm-up sets of the actual lift with lighter loads, which rehearse the pattern as the weight climbs. A good warm-up reduces injury risk, especially for muscle strains, but it doesn't remove it.

### 6. the cool-down, and judging the three
> A **cool-down** runs the same sequence in reverse. Five to ten minutes of light activity keeps the muscle pump working, so blood doesn't pool in the legs and cause dizziness, and it helps clear by-products. Then static or PNF stretching while the tissue is warm, and fluid and food to start recovery. Now evaluate, because these three aren't equally supported. The warm-up has the strongest case, for both performance and injury. Variety is important, but only while specificity is kept. And the cool-down is the weakest. Its popular claim, that it stops next-day soreness, isn't well supported. Its value is the gradual return to rest and a chance to stretch. And all of them share one limit: the athlete's capacity to recover.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing. No official figures.
- **Rates of loss (2).** Plasma volume and stroke volume fall measurably within about one to two weeks of stopping; a substantial part of recent aerobic gains is lost within about four to eight weeks; strength is largely retained for several weeks; muscle size is regained faster than first built; skill is retained longest. Shapes illustrative.
- **Maintenance (3).** Adaptations can be largely maintained with about one third of usual frequency if intensity is preserved (the site's figure; commonly cited from detraining and maintenance studies).
- **Warm-up mechanisms (5).** Higher muscle temperature speeds contraction and relaxation and increases compliance; oxygen dissociates from haemoglobin more readily at higher temperature; gradual cardiovascular rise; warm-ups reduce (not eliminate) injury risk, particularly muscle strains. Illustrative temperatures about 35 to 38 °C and heart rates 72 to 150.
- **Cool-down (6).** Light activity maintains the muscle pump and venous return and can speed lactate clearance; evidence that cool-downs reduce delayed-onset muscle soreness is weak (WE b1).
- **Handoff (9).** Resting heart rates 48 and 72 are illustrative (v08 works them).
