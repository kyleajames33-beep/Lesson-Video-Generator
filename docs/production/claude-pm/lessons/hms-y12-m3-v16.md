### 3. health apps
> Start with **health apps**, software that does a health job. There are huge numbers, and very few have been evaluated. Sort them by what they do. Tracking and coaching apps have the best evidence, when they set goals, give feedback and add some accountability. An app that only shows numbers changes little. Condition apps, like medication reminders or asthma plans, work best when a clinician is connected. Symptom checkers are the riskiest, because errors in either direction matter. And digital therapy programs have good evidence for anxiety and depression, and extend a stretched workforce. Software that diagnoses or monitors a condition is regulated as a medical device, but wellbeing apps mostly aren't, and on your phone they look the same. Three more limits. Most are never tested, people stop using them within weeks, and privacy is often poor.

### 4b. where AI fails, and its safe role
> But its failures are specific. It learns from **training data**, so it inherits that data's gaps. A system trained mostly on one group performs worse on others, including groups who are under-counted in health data. That's **algorithmic bias**, and it can repeat unequal past care while looking objective. It often doesn't transfer to a new hospital or scanner. **Automation bias** means people accept its answer even when other evidence disagrees. And when it's wrong, who's accountable is unclear. So the safe role is **decision support**. It narrows the field, and a clinician decides. Accuracy on a test isn't the same as better outcomes in a real service.

### 5. assistive technology
> Then **assistive technology**, devices that keep or improve what a person can do. Wheelchairs and walking aids. Hearing aids and screen readers. Communication devices. Rails, ramps and home changes. It gets the least attention, and it's often the most direct. It doesn't cure anything. It changes what someone can do with the body they have, which is exactly how we defined healthy ageing. Untreated hearing loss, for example, is linked to isolation and to cognitive decline, so a hearing aid isn't just a convenience. And the barriers aren't technical. There's cost, and eligibility rules that differ between disability, aged care and health. There are long waits, devices handed over without training that end up unused, and in remote areas, nobody nearby to fit or repair them.

### 6. the pattern, and how to investigate
> Across all three, the same pattern. The technology is rarely the limit. Funding, workforce, internet, training and eligibility are. Accuracy is needed, but not enough. A tool can be accurate and still worsen outcomes, if nobody can act on what it finds. Each reaches the best-resourced first. And each shifts work toward the patient. So a new technology improves health only when the system around it changes to use it. To investigate one properly, name your evidence, like trials and the national committees that test whether a new test or medicine is worth funding, and name a limit. Trial groups are often younger, healthier and better connected than the people who'll actually use it.
