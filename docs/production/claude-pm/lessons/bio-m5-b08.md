### 2. two jobs: reshuffle or make new
> There are four sources of genetic variation. Three of them you've met: **crossing over** and **independent assortment** in meiosis, and **random fertilisation**. Those three reshuffle alleles that already exist, like dealing new hands from the same deck of cards. The fourth, **mutation**, is different. It changes the DNA itself, so it's the only one that can make a brand-new allele, a new card in the deck.

### 3. tracing a parent's alleles
> Let's trace where a seed's alleles come from. Take one parent plant and two of its genes, on two different chromosomes. It got one set of chromosomes from each of its own parents: capital **Y** and capital **R** from one, small **y** and small **r** from the other. When it makes gametes, each pair lines up either way, on its own. So a gamete can get Y with R, or small y with small r, like the grandparents. Or it can get two **new** mixes: Y with small r, and small y with R. If both ways are equally likely, and nothing mutates, each kind is a quarter of the gametes.

### 4. genes on the same chromosome
> But what about two genes on the **same** chromosome? Assortment moves whole chromosomes, so on its own it would keep their alleles together, always capital A with capital B, and small a with small b. Crossing over is what splits them up. If the swap you saw in meiosis happens **between** the two genes, it makes chromatids with capital A and small b, and small a with capital B. A swap in that stretch happens in only some cells, so these new combinations are usually less common.

### 5. random fertilisation multiplies
> Now fertilisation. In a pea flower, each ovule is reached by its own pollen tube, from its own pollen grain, and which grain gets there is chance. So each seed in the pod comes from a different pair of gametes. With four kinds of pollen and four kinds of egg, that's **sixteen** possible pairings for just these two genes. For people, each parent can make more than 8 million chromosome combinations, so one couple has about **70 trillion** possible pairings, before crossing over adds even more.

### 7. mutation: the only source of new alleles

### 8. genetic or environmental?
> Not every difference is genetic, though. Remember the hydrangea cuttings, clones with the same genotype, that flowered blue or pink? Differences between clones come from the **environment**. Differences between seeds from a cross are mostly **genetic**, from the four sources. To tell them apart, grow them in the same conditions, or compare clones. And only genetic differences are inherited, because the environment doesn't change the alleles in the gametes.

### 9. why it matters, and which source does what
> Why does any of this matter? Variation means that when conditions change, some individuals are more likely to survive and reproduce, so the population is more likely to keep going. In any one generation, crossing over, assortment and fertilisation make huge numbers of new combinations. Mutation is rare for any one gene, but over long periods it supplies every new allele that the reshuffling then mixes.

### 11b. feedback, item one

### 12. decision rule and handoff
> So when a question asks where variation comes from, sort it first. New combinations of existing alleles come from crossing over, assortment and fertilisation. ### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Four sources of genetic variation: crossing over and independent assortment (meiosis), random fertilisation, mutation.
- The first three make new combinations of existing alleles. Only mutation makes new alleles, and it is inherited only if it is in a cell that makes eggs or sperm.
- Two genes on different chromosomes (YyRr, no mutation): four gamete types, YR, Yr, yR, yr, each 1/4. Genes on the same chromosome are reshuffled only by crossing over, so those combinations are usually less common.
- Fertilisation multiplies: 4 x 4 = 16 pairings for two such genes; about 70 trillion whole-chromosome pairings for one human couple (2^23 x 2^23), before crossing over.
- Genetic variation (different genotypes) is inherited; environmental variation (same genotype, different conditions) is not.

Memory: Cross, Independently, Randomly; only Mutation makes new.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (17 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Griffiths et al., Introduction to Genetic Analysis), the Morgan 1910 paper and the sources named.
- **2 to the power 23** = 8,388,608 ("more than 8 million", as B5b). **2 to the power 46** = 70,368,744,177,664: "about 70 trillion" pairings of whole-chromosome sets for one couple, ignoring crossing over and assuming every combination is possible and distinct. Correct.
- **Two genes on different chromosomes.** A YyRr parent makes YR, Yr, yR, yr, each 1/4, by independent assortment, assuming no mutation and equal chances (Mendel's second law; the site's 5-mark model). 4 x 4 = 16 equally likely gamete pairings at fertilisation. Correct.
- **Pea genes.** Seed colour (I, the *SGR* gene, yellow and green cotyledons) and seed shape (R, the *SBEI* gene, round and wrinkled) are on different chromosomes and assort independently (Mendel's dihybrid 315:108:101:32; modern mapping places them in different linkage groups). Seed colour and shape are traits of the embryo, so seeds in one pod can differ: correct (this is why Mendel could score them in the pods). The video uses Y and R letters as labels and says nothing about dominance; the four seed kinds are shown, not mapped to genotypes.
- **Pea fertilisation.** Each ovule receives its own pollen tube and sperm; each seed is a separate fertilisation. Correct (B2b).
- **Crossing over between two genes on one chromosome** produces recombinant chromatids only when a crossover falls between them; it occurs in only a fraction of meioses, so recombinant gametes are less than half of all gametes for linked genes (recombination frequency below 50 per cent). "Usually less common": correct (genes very far apart approach 50 per cent and behave almost as unlinked, which is why the word "usually" is kept; author note).
- **Fruit fly 11a.** With no crossover: D-E and d-e chromosomes, each with F or f: DEF, DEf, deF, def. With a crossover between D and E: De and dE chromatids, each with F or f: DeF, Def, dEF, dEf. Male *Drosophila* have no meiotic crossing over (achiasmate male meiosis), so the stem says "a cell in a fruit fly's ovary is making eggs" (Revision 2; no reason is spoken).
- **Morgan 1910.** T. H. Morgan found a single white-eyed male among red-eyed *Drosophila* in 1910 (Science 32:120); the white allele was inherited by later generations. "In a lab full of red-eyed fruit flies, one male hatched with white eyes": correct. "A new allele had appeared": it arose by mutation (now known to be an insertion in the *white* gene; author note). The X-linked pattern of inheritance is not shown (B10).
- **Mutation inheritance.** Only mutations in the germ line (cells that give rise to eggs or sperm) are passed to offspring in animals. A gut cell mutation is somatic and not inherited. Correct. Plants differ (no early set-aside germ line), which is why flies are used (author note).
- **Mutation rate.** About 1 x 10 to the minus 8 per base per generation in humans; a few dozen new mutations per person, most outside genes. Learner copy says only "rare for any one gene": correct.
- **Strawberry runners.** Runners (stolons) produce genetically identical daughter plants (B1). Cultivated strawberries are octoploid and highly heterozygous, so seedlings vary widely: correct and supports the item.
- **Variation and survival.** Variation increases the chance that some individuals survive a changed environment; it guarantees nothing for any individual (bank insight in B1). Scene 9 says "more likely". Correct. NESA 2024 Q33 feedback (link crossing over to diversity and population survival) applied.
- **Heritability correction.** The site's h squared 0.79 claim is not used (see Decisions).
