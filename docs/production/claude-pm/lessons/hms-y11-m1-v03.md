### 2. epidemiology
> A doctor sees one patient at a time, and can say what's wrong with that person. But a health system has to answer questions about groups, and those can only be answered by counting. That's **epidemiology**: the study of the patterns and causes of health and disease across populations. It asks four questions, in order. What's happening? To whom? Is it changing? And why, and what works? In Australia, the main source is Australia's Health, from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare.

### 3. mortality, rates and infant mortality
> The first measure is **mortality**, which counts deaths. It's usually given as a rate per 100,000 people, because a count can't compare a small town with a city. Mortality is the most reliable measure we have, because every death must be registered by law. Its weakness is that it says nothing about anyone still alive. **Infant mortality** counts deaths before age one, per 1,000 live births. It reacts quickly to housing, nutrition and access to care, so it's one of the most sensitive signs of how well a whole population is living.

### 4. morbidity
> **Morbidity** is the illness, disease and injury carried by people who are alive. In a country like Australia, where many conditions are managed for decades rather than being quickly fatal, that's where most of the burden sits. But morbidity is harder to measure, because an illness only gets counted when someone goes to a service, is diagnosed, and is recorded. If you never walk in the door, you never appear in the data.

### 5. incidence, prevalence and the bathtub
> The last two measures are the ones most often confused. **Incidence** counts new cases in a period, like a year. **Prevalence** counts all the cases present at one time, new and old together. Think of a bath. Incidence is the tap, the flow of new cases coming in. Prevalence is the water level, how many cases are in the bath right now. And the drain is people leaving, because they recover or they die.

### 6. what the bathtub predicts
> Everything follows from the picture. Influenza has a fast tap and a fast drain, so incidence is high, but prevalence stays low, because people recover within days. Type one diabetes has a slow tap and almost no drain, because it's lifelong, so prevalence climbs year after year. And here's the surprise. A new treatment that stops people dying, without curing them, narrows the drain. Prevalence goes up, even though nothing got worse. In a table it looks like failure. It's actually success.

### 7. reading any table: five questions
> When you meet a table or graph you've never seen, ask five questions, in order. What's the unit, a count, a percentage, or a rate, and per how many? Who's in it, which ages, which years? An area full of retirees will show far more cancer than a university suburb, just because its people are older. Standardising adjusts for age, so you're comparing health, not birthdays.

### 9. what the numbers hide
> These numbers are powerful, but they hide things. An average hides the spread, so a national figure can improve while one group gets worse. Morbidity data depends on being counted, so people who can't reach a service can look healthier than they are, which is exactly backwards. These measures count disease, not wellbeing, so they miss the mental and social dimensions. And the numbers arrive late, so a report describes a situation that's already moved on.

### 11d. feedback, item two
> Define them in parallel: incidence is new cases over a period, prevalence is all cases at one time. Then name the difference. Prevalence also depends on how long people stay cases. ## Memory aid check

- **The bathtub.** Assumptions: a closed population (no migration of cases in or out), outflow is recovery plus death, the period is fixed. It also explains the site's "prevalence rose because mortality fell". Correct.
- **"Prevalence can rise because mortality fell."** True when incidence does not fall faster than deaths fall; in condition X it holds (outflow falls by 90 while inflow falls by 40). Stated as "can", not "does". Correct.
