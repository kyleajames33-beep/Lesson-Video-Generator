Core material is v29's secondary-source choice; a grey extension chip "a third run: swap the nail for other rods" appears in 10 (the copper rod result is v28's). - **Apparatus (site activity 1, made measurable).** The delivered low-voltage supply (set to a fixed 6 V by its dial; a grey tag "fixed"); a coded switch; a rheostat (new plate; gloss "rheostat: a variable resistor, so we can set the current"; tag "a series circuit: v20"); v15's multimeter set to amps, in series (v15's rule); v28's coil on a clear tube, 50 turns over 5.0 cm, with v28's iron nail as the core; the delivered retort stand and boss holding the coil vertical, nail point down; paper clips from one box in Biology's petri dish on the bench below. Measure: the dish rises until the clips touch the nail's point, waits 2 seconds, and lowers 5 cm; the clips still hanging are counted (coded count chip). No hand appears.
- **Building from the diagram (`ci86146789`).** As v15: the HSC-symbol circuit draws first (supply, switch, rheostat, ammeter, coil drawn as an inductor-style coil symbol labelled "coil on iron nail"), then each painted part slides into the matching place and coded leads draw around the loop.
- **Variables and controls, with reasons (`ci9ce164ec`).** Run 1: IV current (rheostat, read on the ammeter); DV clips lifted; controls each with a reason: the same nail (the core sets the boost, v29); the same 50 turns over the same 5.0 cm (turns per metre set the coil's field, v27); the same box of clips (a heavier clip changes the count without any change in the magnet; site Q3); the same lift: 2 seconds' contact, lowered 5 cm; start each trial with no leftover magnetism (added after the inconsistency, 6); the coil cool, by switching off between trials (a hot coil's resistance rises, so the current drifts; site Q3's model answer). Run 2: IV turns on the same 5.0 cm (20, 40, 60, 80, wound in layers); current held at 1.5 A.
- **Illustrative data (labelled; generating model stated, author note c).** Run 1 (50 turns): 0.5 A: 2, 3, 2 (mean 2.3, range 1); 1.0 A: 5, 4, 5 (4.7, 1); 1.5 A: 7, 7, 12 → checked → retaken 8 (7.3, 1); 2.0 A: 9, 10, 9 (9.3, 1); 2.5 A: 11, 10, 11 (10.7, 1). Run 2 (1.5 A): 20 turns: 3, 3, 3 (3.0, 0); 40: 6, 7, 6 (6.3, 1); 60: 9, 9, 10 (9.3, 1); 80: 11, 12, 12 (11.7, 1). Said aloud in scene 4: "These numbers are illustrative, made up to show the method, not real results." Every table, graph and readout carries the grey tag "illustrative data".
- **Prediction 1 (3 s, scene 5): leftover magnetism.** After trial 2 at 1.5 A, with the switch off, the nail still holds two clips (shown). "If we don't clear them, will the next count be too high, too low, or fine?" Chips. Answered in 6: too high; trial 3 reads 12 against 7 and 7. Before replacing it the cause is checked (the two clips were still hanging when the current came on, and the nail kept a little magnetism), explained (an ordinary nail is low-carbon steel, not pure soft iron, so it keeps a little magnetism, v29's coercivity recalled in a phrase) and the method changed: before every trial, switch off and check that no clips hang; clear any that do, and reverse the current briefly at a small value only if they still cling (author note d keeps this optional, not spoken). Retaken: 8. The narration says "before replacing it, we checked why" (as v15), so the lesson does not teach dropping awkward numbers (`ci7afa7b23`, `ci8856aeb0`, `ci706ca3dc`).
- **Repeats (`ci6c11c587`).** Three trials at each setting, with the mean and range; the range (at most 1 clip) shown as grey bars. A clip count is a whole number, so the resolution is one clip: this is the main limitation (11).
- **Run 1's graph (`ci7f1f6fbd`).** Clips lifted (mean) up, current across (strength is the dependent variable, so it goes up; tag "strength up, current across"). Five mean points; a smooth best-fit curve through the origin; qualitative: the count rises with current; quantitative: from 0.5 A to 1.0 A the count roughly doubles (2.3 to 4.7), but from 2.0 A to 2.5 A it rises by only 1.4 clips. Two possible causes are named and not settled: the core approaching saturation (v28) and the clip count itself (clips hanging from clips); "this data can't tell them apart" leads to the force method in 11. The equation's B ∝ I is for the coil's own field (v28), so a straight line is not expected all the way.
- **Run 2's control (the key reasoning stage).** More turns means a longer wire, so a larger resistance; at the same supply voltage the current would fall (v14 recalled in a phrase). So after each rewind the rheostat is re-set until the ammeter reads 1.50 A. This shows why the ammeter is there at all. Prediction 2 (3 s, scene 8): "Double the turns from 20 to 40 on the same 5 cm. What happens to the count?" Chips "about the same?", "about double?", "about four times?". Answered by the data (3.0 to 6.3: about double), with "prediction" tags until the readings arrive (route stop: predictions are not observations).
- **Cross-check between runs (`ci6c11c587` applied).** Run 1's 50 turns at 1.5 A gave 7.3 clips, between run 2's 40 turns (6.3) and 60 turns (9.3). Two runs agreeing at a shared setting is evidence the method is consistent. One sentence.
- **Conclusion (`cib271cbdb`'s "examine the factors").** Both a larger current and more turns per metre made the electromagnet stronger, as B = μ₀NI/L predicts for the coil's field, which the iron core multiplies. At first the count rose roughly in proportion; at the highest current it rose less per step, consistent with the core approaching saturation (or a limit of the clip method). The core is fixed in both runs.
- **Limitations and improvements (`ci7e38e278`).** (1) Clip counts: whole numbers, and clips hang from clips: measure the force instead: an iron block on a top-pan balance under the magnet; the magnet pulls it upwards, so the reading drops; the drop times g is the force (the delivered balance and wooden-block plates, with a coded iron block; author note e). (2) Leftover magnetism: check before every trial (built into the method), or use a soft iron rod. (3) Heating at higher currents: short trials, switch off between them. (4) Only three trials: more trials, in a random order of currents.
- **Risk assessment (`cif90164e3`, `ci6e7b9681` partial).** Two spoken rows, hazard, harm and control: (1) the coil and nail heat up at high currents (burns; melted insulation can let the turns short): switch off between trials, keep to 2.5 A or less, keep trials short, let it cool before touching; (2) a short circuit: the coil has very little resistance, so without the rheostat the current can be very large (overheating leads, a blown fuse): set the rheostat to its largest resistance before switching on, and check the circuit against the diagram. Only the spoken row is shown; the earlier row collapses to a grey chip. A grey note: "low-voltage supply only".

  (b) Currents: 2.5 A in 0.5 mm wire warms it noticeably within tens of seconds; short trials and switching off keep it safe. With 6 V across a coil of well under 1 Ω, the rheostat sets the current; at its largest resistance (10 Ω) the current is about 0.6 A, so 0.5 A needs the supply turned down or a larger rheostat (the design keeps the supply fixed and says nothing about this; teacher note: choose a rheostat or supply setting that reaches 0.5 A).
  (c) Data model: run 1 counts follow about 4.7 clips per amp at low current, curving down at high current (2.3, 4.7, 7.3, 9.3, 10.7); run 2 about 0.15 clips per turn (3.0, 6.3, 9.3, 11.7) with slight curvature at the top; trial scatter ±1 clip. At 50 turns and 1.5 A run 1's 7.3 sits within run 2's trend (interpolated 7.8 at 50 turns; within one clip). H = nI = 1000 × 2.5 = 2500 A m⁻¹ at the top of run 1; an open nail core partly saturating there is plausible. All tagged "illustrative".
  (d) Leftover magnetism: a common nail is low-carbon steel with a coercivity of a few hundred A m⁻¹, so after 1.5 A it can hold a clip or two. Clearing: remove the clips, tap the nail, or briefly pass a small reverse current. The video says only "check that no clips hang before each trial; clear any that do"; using a soft iron rod is the improvement.
  (e) Balance method: an iron block on a top-pan balance under the magnet; the magnet's upward pull reduces the balance reading; force = (drop in mass reading in kg) × 9.8 N kg⁻¹. A2's 0.10 N is a drop of about 10 g. Not calculated in the video (one sentence and a picture).
  (f) Short circuit row: the coil's resistance is far below 1 Ω, so 6 V straight across it would drive a current of tens of amps (limited by the supply's protection). Setting the rheostat to its largest resistance first keeps the current small at switch-on.
  (g) Pronunciations: "rheostat" (REE-oh-stat); "electromagnet".

## Safety facts check (self-check, 3 Nov; Codex to confirm)

- **Hot coil.** Thin enamelled wire carrying 2 to 3 A warms within tens of seconds and can burn; overheated enamel can soften and let adjacent turns touch, shorting part of the coil and raising the current further. - **Short circuit.** The coil's resistance is well under 1 Ω; directly across a 6 V supply the current would be limited only by the supply (author note f). Control: rheostat at its largest resistance before switching on; check the circuit against the diagram. Learner copy as scene 12.
- **Low voltage.** A low-voltage DC supply only; no mains connection at the bench (grey note).
