### 2. recall: each beat gets bigger
> Here's the recall. Cardiac output, the blood the heart pumps each minute, is heart rate times stroke volume, the blood pumped per beat. Endurance training enlarges the left ventricle's chamber, and its wall strengthens to match. So it fills more, and stroke volume rises. At rest the body needs about the same output either way, so fewer beats are needed. Seventy-two times seventy and forty-eight times a hundred and five both give about five thousand millilitres a minute. Same output, split differently. Now the new part: what does that buy when the body works hard?

### 3. exercise, maximum, oxygen and the lungs
> Now exercise. At the same light workload, both need the same cardiac output, but the trained heart meets it with fewer, bigger beats, so its heart rate is lower and it sits further below its ceiling. That's why the same session feels easier. At maximum, the picture changes. Maximum heart rate is set mostly by age, and training doesn't raise it. But stroke volume is much higher, so maximum cardiac output is much higher, perhaps thirty litres a minute against about twenty-one. That's a big reason **maximal oxygen uptake** rises: the most oxygen the body can take in and use each minute. It depends on two things: how much oxygenated blood the heart delivers, and how much the muscles extract, helped by more capillaries and mitochondria. What about the lungs? Lung size and how much air you can breathe out after a full breath barely change. But in healthy people at sea level, the lungs aren't the limit. Delivery and extraction are.

### 4. haemoglobin: more in total, thinner in concentration
> **Haemoglobin** is the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells, and it hides a trap. Endurance training raises total blood volume, and the liquid part, **plasma**, rises first and by more than the red cells do. So a trained athlete carries more haemoglobin in total, but their concentration can look normal or even a little low. Say an untrained person has a hundred and fifty grams per litre in five litres of blood. That's seven hundred and fifty grams. A trained athlete has a hundred and forty-five grams per litre, but in five point six litres. That's eight hundred and twelve grams. Lower concentration, more in total. It's dilution, not a deficiency. Thinner blood flows more easily, and there's more fluid for sweating to keep cool.

### 5. muscle: hypertrophy and fibre types
> In muscle, **hypertrophy** means fibres getting bigger, not more numerous, and it raises the force a muscle can produce. It comes from overloading resistance training. But a beginner's first four to eight weeks of strength gains come mostly from the nervous system, recruiting fibres better, before size changes much. You met the two fibre types in Year 11, slow twitch and fast twitch, and that their mix is mostly inherited. What training changes is inside each type, not which type it is. Aerobic training gives slow fibres more mitochondria, capillaries and aerobic enzymes. Heavy and explosive training makes fast fibres grow and fire better. That's specificity again: fibres adapt to how they're used.

### 6. the chain, both ways, and reading the evidence
> The dot point asks you to examine a relationship. That's a chain with three links: a principle is applied, it produces an adaptation, and the adaptation improves performance. Aerobic volume, overloaded between the two thresholds, enlarges the left ventricle and raises stroke volume, so race pace sits at a lower share of maximum aerobic capacity and can be held longer. But here's the subtle part. An adaptation only improves performance if it improves what limits that athlete. And the chain runs backwards too. Stop training, and reversibility takes the adaptation away first. Coaches read these as evidence too, but illness, poor sleep and fatigue also raise resting heart rate. One number moving the wrong way is a question, not an answer.
