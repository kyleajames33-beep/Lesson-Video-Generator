### 2. each beat gets bigger
> In Year 11 you met the link: cardiac output, the blood the heart pumps each minute, is heart rate times stroke volume, the blood pumped per beat. Endurance training thickens and strengthens the left ventricle's wall and enlarges its chamber. So it fills more and ejects more forcefully: stroke volume rises. At rest, the body needs about the same cardiac output either way. So if each beat carries more, fewer beats are needed. Say the untrained person pumps seventy millilitres a beat. Seventy-two times seventy is five thousand and forty millilitres a minute. The trained cyclist pumps a hundred and five. Forty-eight times a hundred and five is five thousand and forty again. Same output, split differently. So training doesn't make the heart beat faster. It makes each beat bigger.

### 3. exercise, maximum, oxygen and the lungs
> Now exercise. At the same light workload, both need the same cardiac output, but the trained heart meets it with fewer, bigger beats, so its heart rate is lower and it sits further below its ceiling. At maximum, the picture changes. Maximum heart rate is set mostly by age, and training doesn't raise it. But stroke volume is much higher, so maximum cardiac output is much higher. That's a big reason **maximal oxygen uptake** rises: the most oxygen the body can take in and use each minute. It depends on two things: how much oxygenated blood the heart delivers, and how much the muscles extract, helped by more capillaries and mitochondria. What about the lungs? Lung size and how much air you can breathe out after a full breath barely change. But in healthy people at sea level, the lungs aren't the limit. Delivery and extraction are.

### 4. haemoglobin: more in total, thinner in concentration
> **Haemoglobin** is the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells, and it hides a trap. Endurance training raises total blood volume, and the liquid part, **plasma**, rises first and by more than the red cells do. So a trained athlete carries more haemoglobin in total, but their concentration can look normal or even a little low. Say an untrained person has a hundred and fifty grams per litre in five litres of blood. That's seven hundred and fifty grams. A trained athlete has a hundred and forty-five grams per litre, but in five point six litres. That's eight hundred and twelve grams. Lower concentration, more in total. It's dilution, not a deficiency. Thinner blood flows more easily, and there's more fluid for sweating to keep cool.

### 5. muscle: hypertrophy and fibre types
> In muscle, **hypertrophy** means fibres getting bigger, not more numerous, and it raises the force a muscle can produce. It comes from overloading resistance training. But a beginner's first four to eight weeks of strength gains come mostly from the nervous system, recruiting fibres better, before size changes much. Muscles also contain two main fibre types. **Slow twitch** fibres contract more slowly, resist fatigue, and are packed with mitochondria and capillaries. **Fast twitch** fibres contract quickly and forcefully, but tire fast. The proportion of each is largely set by your genes. Training changes what's inside each type, not which type it is. Aerobic training gives slow fibres more mitochondria, capillaries and aerobic enzymes. Heavy and explosive training makes fast fibres grow and fire better. That's specificity again: fibres adapt to how they're used.

### 6. the chain, both ways, and reading the evidence
> The dot point asks you to examine a relationship. That's a chain with three links: a principle is applied, it produces an adaptation, and the adaptation improves performance. Aerobic volume, overloaded between the two thresholds, enlarges the left ventricle and raises stroke volume, so race pace sits at a lower share of maximum and can be held longer. But here's the subtle part. An adaptation only improves performance if it improves what limits that athlete. And the chain runs backwards too. Stop training, and reversibility takes the adaptation away first. Coaches read these as evidence too, but illness, poor sleep and fatigue also raise resting heart rate. One number moving the wrong way is a question, not an answer.

- **Oxygen uptake and lungs (3).** Maximal oxygen uptake depends on delivery (maximum cardiac output) and extraction (capillaries, mitochondria, enzymes); vital capacity and lung volumes change little with training; breathing efficiency improves; lungs are not usually limiting in healthy people at sea level.
