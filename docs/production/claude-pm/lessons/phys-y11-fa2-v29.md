A trough three centimetres deep travels the other. At the instant they exactly overlap, how far is the spring from its rest position, right where they **meet**?

### 2. two crests meet
> Let's watch two crests first, each four centimetres high. As they overlap, the spring rises higher than either one alone. At every point, the spring's displacement is the first pulse's displacement plus the second's. When the peaks line up, four plus four gives eight centimetres. That's the principle of superposition: where waves overlap, the total displacement is the sum of their separate **displacements**.

Visual: camera continue; the hook's pulses fade back to their starting ends and are replaced by two crests, "+4 cm" each (pulse 1 navy, pulse 2 slate). ### 3. why they add
> Why simply add? Each pulse is a push travelling along the spring. Where they overlap, each bit of spring gets both pushes at once, so it moves by what the first would have done, plus what the second would have done. Displacement has a direction, so a crest counts as plus, and a trough as **minus**.

Visual: camera push to one coil at full overlap (a marked coil, purple tape, as v02). On "a push travelling" a small hollow gold energy arrow rides along with each pulse. On "both pushes" two short teal arrows on the marked coil, one from each ghost, stack end to end: "+4" then "+4" to "+8". ### 6. why the pulses come back
> They carry on, unchanged. The spring looks flat, but it isn't still. On one side, the coils are moving up, and on the other, they're moving down. The pulses' energy is all in that motion, so the moving coils rebuild both pulses, and each one travels on as if the other was never **there**.

### 8. in phase and out of phase
> The same rule works for whole trains of waves. Take two identical waves along the same line. When they're in phase, crests line up with crests, so the displacements add, and the result is a wave twice as tall. That's constructive interference. Slide one wave along by half a wavelength, and now crests line up with troughs. The displacements cancel, and the result is flat. That's destructive interference. Shift it by anything else, and you get something in **between**.

Visual: board change: `PhaseSlider`, three stacked axes: "wave 1" (navy, amplitude 2.0 cm), "wave 2" (slate, 2.0 cm), "sum" (ink). On "in phase" the two waves align (gloss "in phase: crests line up with crests"); dotted verticals tie crest to crest; the sum's amplitude bracket reads "4.0 cm". On "constructive interference" a label on the sum axis (gloss "interference: what happens where waves overlap and add"). On "Slide one wave" wave 2 slides along; the sum shrinks smoothly (no values); on "half a wavelength" a bracket "½λ" and wave 2 stops with its crests under wave 1's troughs (gloss "out of phase by half a wavelength: crests line up with troughs"); on "flat" the sum lies on its axis line: "0". On "destructive interference" a label. On "anything else" wave 2 slides on through other shifts, the sum's amplitude rising and falling between 0 and 4.0 cm (no values); on "between" it settles back at ½λ. All three waves keep moving to the right on one clock.

### 9. worked example: partly cancelling
> Waves don't always cancel completely. Task: find the total displacement at two points, and name the interference. At point A, wave one is at plus four centimetres, and wave two is at minus one. > Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Principle of superposition: where waves overlap, the total displacement is the sum of their separate displacements: y_total = y₁ + y₂ (crest +, trough −).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "Two pulses travel towards each other along a stretched rope. At its peak, pulse P has a displacement of +5 cm and pulse Q has a displacement of −2 cm. (a) Calculate the displacement of the rope at the instant the two peaks coincide. (b) Name the type of interference that occurs at that instant. (c) Describe what happens to the two pulses immediately afterwards."
Model answer: (a) By the principle of superposition, y = y_P + y_Q = (+5) + (−2) = +3 cm. (b) Destructive interference (partial): the displacements have opposite signs, so the resultant is smaller than pulse P's displacement. (c) The pulses pass through each other and continue unchanged, each with its original shape, amplitude, speed and direction. Classic mistake (crossed): "the pulses merge into one +3 cm pulse".

**Attempt 2 (scene 16; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested).** "Two waves with the same frequency and wavelength travel in the same direction along the same string. One wave has an amplitude of 3.0 cm and the other has an amplitude of 2.0 cm. (a) Determine the largest and the smallest possible amplitudes of the resultant wave. (b) Explain how the two waves must line up to produce each result."
Model answer: (a) Largest: 3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0 cm. Smallest: 3.0 − 2.0 = 1.0 cm. (b) The largest amplitude occurs when the waves are in phase: crests line up with crests and troughs with troughs, so the displacements add at every point (constructive interference). The smallest occurs when the waves are half a wavelength out of phase: crests line up with troughs, so the displacements partly cancel at every point (destructive interference). The cancellation is not complete because the amplitudes are unequal. Classic mistake (crossed): "destructive interference always gives zero".
