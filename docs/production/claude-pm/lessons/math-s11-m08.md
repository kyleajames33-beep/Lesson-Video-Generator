### 2. one period: everything weekly
> First, use your net pay, what actually lands in your account after tax. Budgeting with pay before tax makes everything look better than it **is**. Then put every amount in the same period. You're paid weekly, so make it weekly. Rent is already weekly, two hundred and fifty. Your phone plan is fifty-two dollars a month. A month isn't four weeks, so don't divide by four. Times it by twelve for the year, then divide by fifty-two weeks. That's twelve dollars a **week**. The same move works for anything. The hatchback costs fifty-four hundred and eight a year, so that's a hundred and four a **week**. Your third of the house's bills is about two fifty-four a quarter. Round a cost up when you budget, so call it two sixty, times four, divided by fifty-two, twenty dollars a **week**. Amount, times how many times a year, divided by fifty-**two**.

### 3. fixed, essential, or a choice?
> Now sort the spending. Fixed costs are the same every time, like rent at two fifty, your phone at twelve, and your share of the internet, six dollars. Some costs are needs, but the amount changes. Groceries, a hundred and ten. The car, a hundred and four. That's mostly fixed costs, but the fuel changes with how far you drive, so it goes with the needs that change. Bills, twenty. Call those variable essentials. Then there's discretionary spending, the wants you choose, and can cut. Eating out sixty, entertainment forty, clothes twenty-**five**. The syllabus talks about fixed and discretionary spending. Needs that change sit in between, and it's worth keeping them separate, because they're the hardest to cut **quickly**.

### 4. surplus or deficit
> Add it all up. Two sixty-eight, plus two thirty-four, plus a hundred and twenty-five, is six hundred and twenty-seven dollars a **week**. Take that from the pay. Eight sixty minus six twenty-seven leaves two hundred and thirty-three dollars a **week**. That's a surplus, what's left after spending. As a share of your pay, it's about twenty-seven per cent, called your savings **rate**. If spending exactly matched pay, the budget would be balanced, with nothing left over. If spending were bigger than pay, the answer would be negative, a deficit. And a deficit doesn't stay small. Just eighteen dollars a week short is over nine hundred dollars a year of **debt**.

### 5. the budget as a spreadsheet
> Now make it a model, like the car. Each row gets an item, an amount, and how many times a year it's paid. Then column D holds the weekly amount, with one formula, equals B four times C four, divided by fifty-two. Fill it down, and every row converts **itself**. D thirteen is the sum of the costs, and D fourteen is the pay minus D thirteen, the surplus, two hundred and thirty-**three**. Now try a what-if. Cut eating out from sixty to thirty. The surplus jumps to two hundred and sixty-three dollars, thirty dollars more, straight **away**. That's the point of a model. You can test a change before you make it, and because every total is a formula, the sheet never goes out of date.
