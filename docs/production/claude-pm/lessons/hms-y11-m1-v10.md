### 2. self-efficacy
> The first skill is **self-efficacy**: your belief that you can do the specific thing a goal needs. It's about capability, not worth, so don't confuse it with self-esteem. And it's task-specific. You might be sure you can keep a lights-out time on school nights, and unsure you can ask a doctor a question. It predicts three things: whether you attempt a change at all, how hard you try, and most importantly, whether you start again after a slip. Two people can lapse the same way, and the one who believes the goal is reachable starts again.

### 3. four sources, and the runner who quit
> Self-efficacy is built from four sources, and they're not equal. **Mastery experience**, having succeeded at something similar, is by far the strongest. Seeing someone like you succeed helps, but the likeness matters, so watching an elite athlete does less for a beginner. Being told you can do it, by someone credible, is weaker, and it collapses if experience says otherwise. And how you read your body counts: a racing heart can mean readiness, not inability. Take a student who started running three times, quit three times, and now says, I'm just not a runner. What's missing is mastery. So design the first week to be achievable.

### 4. health literacy
> The second skill is **health literacy**, and it's much wider than reading a leaflet. It's four abilities: find, understand, evaluate and use health information and services. Find means knowing a service exists, and that you're allowed to use it. Understand means making sense of a dose or a risk. Evaluate is the critical step: is the source reliable, and who benefits if you believe it? A sleep supplement promising fast results, with a discount code, fails that straight away. Use means acting on it, including asking again when something wasn't clear. And it's shared. A clinician who explains clearly raises the health literacy of every patient they see.

### 5. help-seeking, and what stops it
> The third skill is **help-seeking**, reaching out for support, formal or informal. Almost every issue we've met gets worse with delay and better with early contact, yet many young people delay seeking help, or never seek it. Five common barriers stand in the way: stigma, not recognising the problem, not knowing where to go, fear of what happens next, like judgement or parents being told, and preferring to handle it alone. Three things reliably help: one trusted adult, knowing exactly where to go, and having done it before with a good result, which is mastery again.

### 7. problem-solving, made not defaulted
> The fourth skill is **problem-solving**. Most health decisions young people make aren't really decided, they're defaulted, in the moment, under pressure. Problem-solving gives you a structure in advance. Define the real problem: I'm always exhausted could be sleep, workload or mood, and each has a different fix. Generate several options. Weigh what each costs, risks and protects, including friendships. Choose and act. Then review, the step everyone skips. The most useful version is a pre-decided response, like a sentence ready for your manager: I can't work past nine on school nights. Rehearsed words are there under pressure, and good intentions often aren't.

### 8. what the skills can and cannot do
> So what can these skills do? Within the same circumstances, they change outcomes. Two young people in identical conditions, one who knows where to go, believes it'll help, and has the words ready, won't end up with the same health. But skills can't create a service, pay for one, provide a trusted adult, or make a street safe. So a program that teaches only skills is taken up most easily by those who already have the conditions, and it can lift the average and widen the gap. The fix is pairing: teach help-seeking and fund the service, teach critical health literacy and regulate the marketing. Look at your kept change: was the difference a skill, or a condition?

- **Self-efficacy and its four sources (2, 3).** Bandura's social cognitive theory: mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal (social) persuasion, physiological and emotional states, with mastery the most influential (Bandura, 1977, 1997). Self-efficacy predicts initiation, effort and persistence after setbacks. Correct as the site states.
- **Health literacy (4).** Find, understand, evaluate, use matches the common access, understand, appraise, apply model (Sørensen and others, 2012) and the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care's description of individual and environmental health literacy ("shared": the health literacy environment). Critical health literacy is Nutbeam's highest level (2000). Correct.
- **Help-seeking (5, 6).** Barriers (stigma, poor recognition, not knowing where to go, confidentiality and judgement concerns, self-reliance) and facilitators (trusted adult, knowing where to go, positive past experience) follow Gulliver, Griffiths and Christensen (2010, systematic review of young people's mental health help-seeking). Early contact improving outcomes for most youth issues: well supported. **Site correction 9** removes the "largest single effect" ranking.
