**Boundary:** this video starts by locating the carbonyl group in an aldehyde and a ketone and naming both families, and stops at explaining how tests distinguish an aldehyde from a ketone and using them to identify the product of an alcohol oxidation. It teaches: the carbonyl group (recalled from c08a) is polar because oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly; in an aldehyde the C=O is at the end of the chain and the carbonyl carbon carries an H (–CHO); in a ketone the C=O is inside the chain, with a carbon on each side and no H on the carbonyl carbon; naming aldehydes (longest chain including the carbonyl carbon, drop the final "e", add "al"; the carbonyl carbon is C1, so no locant; branches numbered from it: 2-methylpropanal); naming ketones ("one"; propanone and butanone need no locant; from C5 a locant from the end nearer the C=O: pentan-2-one, pentan-3-one; branches as in c01); aldehydes and ketones with the same number of carbons (three or more) are functional group isomers (propanal and propanone, C₃H₆O); intermolecular forces: dipole–dipole from the C=O plus dispersion, and no hydrogen bonding between their own molecules (no O–H), so for similar sizes their boiling points sit between an alkane and an alcohol; the aldehyde H is on carbon, not oxygen, so it cannot donate a hydrogen bond; water can hydrogen bond to the carbonyl oxygen's lone pairs, so small aldehydes and ketones dissolve in water, less as the chain grows; tests: the aldehyde's carbonyl H lets it be oxidised by mild oxidising agents while the reagent is reduced; Tollens' reagent (silver ions in ammonia solution, warmed) gives a silver mirror with an aldehyde and no change with a ketone; Fehling's or Benedict's solution (blue, copper(II)) gives a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide with an aldehyde and stays blue with a ketone; acidified dichromate turns green with an aldehyde and stays orange with a ketone; using a test on the distilled product of an alcohol oxidation to identify a primary or secondary alcohol, and why dichromate alone cannot (primary and secondary alcohols turn it green too); Tollens' hazard: made fresh, used at once and disposed of promptly, because on standing it can form an explosive solid. **Not here:** alcohol reactions and conditions (chem-m7-c08a, recalled); carboxylic acids, their dimers, boiling points and acidity (chem-m7-c09); the practical procedure, reagent preparation and risk table (chem-m7-p04, with `C7-IQ2-05`); half-equations for the tests (author note d); aromatic aldehydes and ketones (vanillin, raspberry ketone; author note g); reduction of carbonyl compounds; aldehyde and ketone uses beyond one hook line; cyclic ketones; the site's olfactory-receptor discussion.

(g) Vanillin (an aromatic aldehyde with phenol OH and ether groups) and raspberry ketone (an aromatic ketone with a phenol OH) are not used (site correction 8). ## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the C=O is polar: oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly (2). Why an aldehyde carries an H on its carbonyl carbon: the C=O is at the chain end (2).
