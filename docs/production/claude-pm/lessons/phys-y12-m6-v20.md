### 2. currents inside solid metal
> The plate has no wires, but it's full of closed paths. Picture any loop drawn inside the copper. Where the copper is moving into the field, the flux through loops there is **increasing**. That induces an emf, and because copper conducts, a current swirls round inside the metal. A current induced in a loop inside a solid piece of metal is called an eddy current, like a swirl in a stream. Where the copper is moving out of the field, the flux through loops there is decreasing, so the eddy current swirls the other **way**.

### 3. the field pushes back
> Each eddy current flows partly inside the field. That part of the current feels a force, the motor effect. Work out the direction for both swirls, and both forces point backwards, against the **motion**. That's Lenz's law: the eddy currents oppose the change causing them, the plate's motion through the field. Coming in, it pushes back. Going out, it pulls **back**. Either way, the plate is **braked**, and nothing touches it.

### 4. where the energy goes
> So where does the plate's kinetic energy go? The braking force works against the motion, taking kinetic energy away from the plate. That energy drives the eddy currents round the copper. Copper has some resistance, so the currents heat it, at a rate of I squared R. Kinetic energy becomes electrical energy, which becomes **heat**. No energy is lost. It's only **transformed**.

Visual: energy bars at the right: "kinetic" (amber) shrinking as the swing decays; on "drives the eddy currents" an "electrical" bar (orange) filling and passing on; on "I squared R" a chip "P = I²R"; on "heat" a "heat in the copper" bar (red) growing to the height the kinetic bar lost, and a faint red tint on the plate. On "transformed" a tick: "kinetic → electrical → heat: total unchanged".

### 5. a magnet in a copper tube
> The same idea works in a tube. Drop a strong magnet down a copper tube, and it drifts down slowly. Below the magnet, the flux through each ring of copper is increasing, so an eddy current there **repels** it. Above the magnet, the flux is decreasing, so the eddy current there **attracts** it. Both forces point **up**. It speeds up until that upward force equals its weight, then falls at a steady terminal speed. In a plastic tube, nothing conducts, so it simply falls, thirty centimetres in about a **quarter of a second**. In copper, its lost gravitational energy becomes heat, not kinetic **energy**.

### 6. cut the paths
> Now cut slots into the plate, like the teeth of a comb. The slots break the big loops into small ones. A small loop encloses less changing flux, so it has a smaller emf, and its narrow path has more resistance. So the currents are much smaller, the braking force drops, and the slotted plate swings much **longer**. A long slot down a copper tube does the same, and the magnet falls much **faster**. Small eddy currents still flow, though. It's the same idea as laminated transformer cores.

### 7. no motion, no brake
> Here's an important limit. Braking needs motion. The force comes from a changing flux, so the slower the conductor moves, the weaker the **force**. As a train slows down, its magnetic brake fades, and it can't hold the train still. A friction brake has to finish the stop, and hold **it**. For the same reason, a magnet can't float at rest over a still copper sheet. With nothing moving, the flux doesn't change, and there's no force at **all**. Magnetic braking resists motion. It can't make things **levitate**.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Eddy currents: currents induced in loops inside a solid conductor when the flux through it changes (relative motion of a conductor and a field).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A roller-coaster car is slowed by a magnetic brake: copper fins attached to the car pass between rows of strong permanent magnets fixed beside the track. (a) Explain, with reference to Lenz's law, how the brake slows the car. (b) Apply the law of conservation of energy to the braking process."
Model answer: (a) As the fins move between the magnets, the magnetic flux through closed paths in the copper changes, so emfs are induced (Faraday's law) and eddy currents flow in the fins. By Lenz's law the eddy currents flow so as to oppose the change causing them, the relative motion; the currents lie in the magnets' field and experience forces (motor effect) that act against the car's motion, slowing it without contact (2 marks). (b) The car's kinetic energy is not destroyed: the braking force does work against the motion, transferring the kinetic energy to electrical energy in the eddy currents, which is dissipated as heat (internal energy) in the fins through their resistance (I²R); the decrease in kinetic energy equals the heat produced, ignoring air resistance (2 marks). Classic mistake (crossed): "the magnets attract the copper fins".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks).** "A strong magnet is dropped down a vertical copper tube. An identical magnet is dropped down an identical copper tube that has a narrow slot cut along its full length. Compare the motion of the two magnets and explain the difference."
Model answer: In the solid tube the falling magnet changes the flux through the rings of copper around it, inducing large eddy currents circling the tube (below the magnet they repel it, above they attract it); by Lenz's law both oppose the fall, so the magnet soon reaches a low terminal speed. The slot interrupts the circular current paths, so only small local eddy currents flow; the opposing force is much smaller and the magnet falls much faster, close to free fall, though not exactly. Classic mistake (crossed): "the slot stops all eddy currents".
