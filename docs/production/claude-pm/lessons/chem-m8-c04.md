- **Which light (the lesson's first surprise):** "you measure with the colour it absorbs, not the colour you see". The cause: a copper solution looks blue because it absorbs orange and red light and lets blue through. This is the step students most often get backwards, and it explains both the colourimeter's filter choice and λmax.
- **The complex (link to c03a).** The copper ammonia complex confirmed copper in c03a; here it makes copper easier to measure. Plain hydrated copper(II) absorbs weakly (ε about 12 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ near 800 nm), the ammine complex several times more strongly (ε about 50 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ near 600 nm; author note b). "Add ammonia in excess, to the standards and the sample alike" is a necessary condition (every copper ion must be in the same absorbing form), kept in learner copy. It also sets up p04, which is a Cu²⁺ calibration.
- **Beer-Lambert as a calculation.** The site's example is reused exactly: A = 0.480, ε = 120 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, l = 1.00 cm, c = 0.00400 mol/L. The site's "Must know" (students omit l) is the crossed chip on screen, not a spoken sentence. The narration then says why a calibration line is usually trusted instead: a book ε may not match an instrument's light (a filter's band, a slightly different wavelength). The pack notes ε here is exercise data, not a universal constant.
- **AAS in cause order.** Lead ions are colourless and too dilute for a colour test; spray the sample into a flame; water evaporates and heat breaks the compounds into free atoms (atomising); a hollow cathode lamp of lead emits lead's wavelengths; lead atoms absorb them as electrons jump up; the detector measures what went missing. Why specific: other atoms need different energies. The flame test link (c03a): same energy levels, emission there, absorption here. The site's misconception ("AAS detects ions in solution") is said positively (scene 8) and crossed in feedback 2.
- **AAS sensitivity at the lead guideline (author note d).** Flame AAS reaches roughly hundredths of a milligram per litre for lead; the drinking water guideline (0.005 mg/L) needs graphite furnace AAS or a concentrating step. Learner copy says "sensitive enough for trace metals"; feedback 2 and key note 3 carry a small grey tag "lowest levels: graphite furnace AAS". Leaving the tag out would imply flame AAS reaches 0.005 mg/L, so it stays on screen without a spoken caveat.

- **Author notes (not learner copy):**
  (a) Complementary colour: hydrated copper(II) absorbs most strongly in the red and near infrared (λmax about 800 nm) and transmits blue-green, so it looks pale blue; school colourimeters use a red or orange filter for copper(II) sulfate. The narration says "absorbs orange and red light".

"Several times more strongly" is said; no ε for copper is on screen except as grey axis values on the illustrative spectrum ("shape illustrative"). λmax "near six hundred nanometres" is said; the screen shows "about 600 nm".
  (c) Beer-Lambert: A = log₁₀(I₀/I) (site; not taught, as in p02); proportionality holds for dilute solutions and narrow bands of light; a filter's band, stray light and chemical changes bend the line at high absorbance, which is why the line is used only inside the standards. The site's ε = 120 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ is exercise data.
  (d) AAS: flame (air-acetylene, about 2,300 °C); nebuliser sprays the sample; lead lines 217.0 nm and 283.3 nm; copper 324.7 nm. Flame AAS detection limits for lead are of the order of 0.01 mg/L; graphite furnace AAS reaches about 0.001 mg/L or below; so the hook's guideline level (0.005 mg/L) needs a furnace (or a concentrating step). The site's lead calibration (gradient 0.162 per mg/L) is steeper than typical flame AAS sensitivities for lead; kept as the site's exercise data. Copper attempt data (gradient 0.0902 per mg/L) constructed for this video, of the order of flame AAS copper sensitivity. Matrix effects: other dissolved substances change nebulisation, atomisation or background absorption; matrix-matched standards, spikes and background correction are the site's L08 responses; the video says "other substances in the sample can change how many atoms form, so standards should resemble the sample".
  (e) Specificity: a hollow cathode lamp emits the narrow lines of its element; only that element's atoms absorb at those lines (spectral overlaps exist but are rare and are corrected in practice; not said). "Atoms in the flame, not ions in the solution" is the site's own correction.
  (f) Flame test link: in a flame test, excited atoms emit at wavelengths set by their energy levels (c03a); in AAS, ground-state atoms absorb at the same wavelengths. Most atoms in an AAS flame stay in the ground state, which is why absorption is more sensitive than emission for most metals (not said).

### 2. which light?
> You met the colourimeter in Module 5. Light passes through a cuvette, the solution absorbs some of it, and the meter reports **absorbance**. So you measure with the colour it **absorbs**, not the colour you see. In a colourimeter, that's a red or orange filter.

### 5. Beer-Lambert as a calculation
> The law behind this is Beer-Lambert: A equals epsilon l c. Say a solution reads an absorbance of point four eight zero, epsilon is a hundred and twenty litres per mole per centimetre, and the cuvette is one centimetre wide. A book value of epsilon may not suit your instrument's light, though, so chemists usually trust a calibration line made on their own **instrument**.

### 6. AAS: atomise, then absorb
> Lead ions are colourless, and far too dilute for any colour test. So for metals at trace levels, chemists use atomic absorption spectroscopy, or AAS. The sample is sprayed into a flame. The water evaporates, and the heat breaks the lead compounds apart into free lead **atoms**. That's called atomising. A hollow cathode lamp, with its cathode made of lead, shines lead's own wavelengths through the flame. Lead atoms absorb those wavelengths as their electrons jump to higher energy **levels**, and the detector measures how much light went **missing**.

### 7. why AAS is specific
> Other atoms in the flame need different energies, so they let lead's light **pass**. You've seen these energy jumps before. In a flame test, heated atoms give out their own colours. In AAS, atoms in the flame take in those same wavelengths from the lamp. So AAS measures atoms in the flame, not ions in the **solution**. That makes it specific, and sensitive enough for trace metals. The costs: one lamp means one element at a time, and other substances in the sample can change how many atoms form, so standards should resemble the **sample**.

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. A coloured species absorbs the colours it doesn't show; measure with the light it absorbs (copper(II): red or orange filter). Colourimetry: a filter's band of visible light. UV-visible: a grating and slit pick one wavelength (visible or UV); measure at λmax, the peak of the absorption spectrum.
