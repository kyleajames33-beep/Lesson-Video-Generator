### 1. hook (an approved treatment and bigger claims)
> In late 2023, a treatment made with the gene-editing tool **CRISPR** was approved for the first time, for sickle cell disease. Since then, the claims have grown: CRISPR will wipe out malaria, cure every genetic disease, end crop failure. Some of these could happen, and some are hype. So which future uses of biotechnology are **plausible**, and how can you tell?

### 2. how CRISPR finds and cuts
> CRISPR changes a chosen stretch of DNA using two parts. A short **guide RNA** has bases that pair with the target stretch, by normal base pairing. It leads an enzyme called **Cas9** to that spot, and Cas9 cuts both DNA strands. The cell repairs the cut, but often adds or loses a few bases, which can switch that stretch off. But the guide can sometimes pair with a similar stretch elsewhere, and cut there too.

### 3. the approved use: sickle cell
> Now the approved use. In sickle cell disease, one changed base gives haemoglobin that makes red cells **sickle**. But before birth, you made a different kind, **foetal haemoglobin**, which doesn't sickle. After birth, a protein switches the foetal gene off. So scientists take a patient's blood-forming stem cells and, in the lab, use CRISPR to cut the DNA that lets these cells make that switch. With less of it, the cells make foetal haemoglobin again. The sickle allele is still there, but the foetal haemoglobin keeps most red cells from sickling.

### 5. how far has the evidence got?
> So sort each claim by how far the evidence has got. Is it only an idea? Has it worked in the lab? In animals? In human trials? Or is it approved and in use? The sickle cell treatment is on the top rung. Biotechnology is heading in several directions: editing cells inside the body, editing crops to resist disease, and screening DNA to spot disease risk early. But editing works best where one gene causes the problem. Common diseases, like type 2 diabetes, involve many genes and the environment, so there's often no single stretch to fix.

### 6. a proposed use: a gene drive
> Malaria, spread by certain mosquitoes, kills hundreds of thousands of people a year. Normally, a parent with one copy of an allele passes it to about **half** its offspring, because each gamete gets one chromosome of the pair. A **gene drive** cheats that rule. The drive allele carries the code for its own CRISPR editor. In the cells that make gametes, the editor cuts the matching spot on the other chromosome, and the cell repairs the cut by copying the drive across. So nearly **all** the offspring inherit it.

On "copying the drive across" the amber band copies onto the second homologue (both now amber; gloss fades). On "nearly all" the `OffspringRow` refills: all eight glyphs amber (tag "nearly all offspring"). Hold one second.

### 7. crashing a population
> Now aim the drive at a gene that female mosquitoes need to lay eggs. Females with one copy can still breed and pass it on, but females with two copies can't. As the drive spreads, fewer females can breed, and the population can **crash**. In small lab cages, a drive like this reached every mosquito, and the populations collapsed within seven to eleven generations. But no gene drive has been released into the wild yet.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (21 Oct) against the sources named and standard texts (Campbell Biology; OpenStax Biology 2e, 17.1 and 17.2). The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing; items marked "recheck near recording" can change.
- **CRISPR-Cas9 mechanism.** A guide RNA of about 20 nucleotides base-pairs with a target DNA sequence (next to a PAM, not mentioned); Cas9 makes a double-strand break; non-homologous end joining often leaves small insertions or deletions that can disrupt the sequence (Jinek et al. 2012; Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2020, Charpentier and Doudna). Off-target cleavage at sites with partial complementarity is documented. Narration correct as stated.
- **First CRISPR approval.** Exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy) was authorised by the UK MHRA on 16 November 2023 and approved by the US FDA on 8 December 2023 for sickle cell disease (patients 12 and over). "In late 2023, a treatment made with the gene-editing tool CRISPR was approved for the first time, for sickle cell disease": correct.
- **Exa-cel mechanism.** CRISPR-Cas9 edits the erythroid-specific enhancer of *BCL11A* in autologous CD34+ haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells ex vivo, reducing BCL11A expression in red-cell precursors and raising foetal haemoglobin (HbF); the sickle allele is not corrected (FDA product information; Frangoul et al. 2021 and 2024, NEJM). "Cut the DNA that lets red-cell precursors make that switch ... The sickle allele is still there": correct. "Foetal haemoglobin keeps most red cells from sickling": HbF inhibits HbS polymerisation; high pancellular HbF prevents sickling in most cells. Correct at this level.
- **Trial figure.** Frangoul et al. 2024 (NEJM 390:1649): of 30 patients with sufficient follow-up, 29 (96.7 per cent) were free of vaso-occlusive crises for at least 12 consecutive months. Confirmed by search 21 Oct. "Twenty-nine of the thirty patients followed for long enough had no severe pain crises for at least a year": correct ("severe pain crises" is the plain term for severe vaso-occlusive crises).
- **Conditioning and hospital time.** Patients receive myeloablative busulfan conditioning before infusion and typically stay in hospital for weeks for engraftment (FDA label; NEJM). Correct.
- **Price.** US wholesale acquisition cost announced at US$2.2 million (Vertex, December 2023). "Over two million dollars": correct; recheck near recording.
- **Where patients live.** Most births with sickle cell disease occur in sub-Saharan Africa (about three quarters; WHO, Piel et al. 2013). Revision 2 says "most babies with the disease are born in sub-Saharan Africa" (a births claim, which the source supports, rather than a claim about where most living patients are). Access: the Claude science check found Casgevy is not commercially available in any sub-Saharan African country (SciDev.Net, February 2026); hence "where almost no patients can get it yet" (Revision 1's "very few hospitals can offer it" implied some can). Recheck near recording.
- **Many genes.** Most common diseases are polygenic and influenced by environment (site L10 Explain model). Correct.
- **Directions.** In-body (in vivo) editing in human trials (for example liver-targeted editing for transthyretin amyloidosis and PCSK9); gene-edited crops (for example disease-resistant varieties in development and some approved overseas); expanded DNA screening. Named generally; correct.
- **Malaria deaths.** WHO World Malaria Report 2024: about 597,000 deaths in 2023; World Malaria Report 2025: about 610,000 deaths in 2024. "Hundreds of thousands of people a year": correct. Spread by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes ("certain mosquitoes").
- **Gene drive mechanism.** A homing gene drive encodes Cas9 and a guide RNA targeting the homologous wild-type allele; cleavage in the germline followed by homology-directed repair copies the drive, so heterozygotes transmit it to well over half (often over 90 per cent) of offspring. "Nearly all the offspring inherit it": correct at this level.
- **Suppression drive and cages.** Kyrou et al. 2018 (Nature Biotechnology 36:1062): a drive targeting *doublesex* in *Anopheles gambiae*; heterozygous females fertile, homozygous females sterile; the drive reached 100 per cent prevalence and caused complete population collapse within 7 to 11 generations in small cages with no resistance detected; Hammond et al. 2021 large indoor cages also suppressed populations. "Females with one copy can still breed ... two copies can't ... In small lab cages ... collapsed within seven to eleven generations" (Revision 2): correct. Kyrou et al. attribute the absence of resistance to the target's functional constraint, which Revision 2's "the cage drive aimed at a stretch that rarely changes" states; Hammond et al. 2021 (Nature Communications) suppressed large, age-structured cage populations within about a year.
- **No wild release.** No gene drive organism has been released in the wild (Target Malaria's 2019 Burkina Faso release used non-drive sterile males; a non-drive male-bias strain was released there on 11 August 2025, after which the national authorities suspended the project on 18 August and the research ministry ended its activities in the country on 22 August 2025; Target Malaria's April 2026 statement announces no drive release). Author note only: the Burkina Faso decision is a real "who decides" example for b13 or b20, not used here. Recheck near recording; fallback "no gene drive has been released into the wild, as far as published reports show".
- **Resistance.** Cut-resistant alleles (changed target sites) can arise and halt homing drives; some designs target conserved sites to reduce this (site L19 correction: not inevitable). "Resistance could stop the drive spreading": correct with "could".
- **Ecology.** *Anopheles gambiae* is one of several hundred *Anopheles* species and thousands of mosquito species; ecological effects of suppressing one are considered likely small but uncertain. "Might affect animals that feed on them, though many other mosquito species would remain": correct as hedged.
- **PCSK9 (attempt 1).** PCSK9 protein promotes degradation of hepatic LDL receptors, so loss of PCSK9 increases LDL receptors and lowers blood LDL; base-editing treatments delivered in lipid nanoparticles (VERVE-101, VERVE-102) have been in early human trials. The card states the effect in plain terms ("reduces the liver's ability to remove LDL") and calls the trials "small, early-stage" (VERVE-102, now Lilly's, reported interim phase 1b Heart-2 data on 35 participants in May 2026, with phase 2 planned); recheck trial status near recording. Scene 5's many-genes example is type 2 diabetes (Revision 2), not heart disease, so it does not contradict attempt 1's one-gene edit lowering a heart-disease risk factor.
