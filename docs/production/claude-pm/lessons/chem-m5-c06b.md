## Decisions

- **Main question:** *If a question gives you K and the start, how do you find the equilibrium concentrations, and which shortcut gets you there without the quadratic formula?*
- **Site source used:** `site-extracts/chem-m5-l10.md` and the site lesson 10 (the forward ICE case with K given, +2x for HI and the square-root shortcut for H2 + I2). The site's small-x material is in Q7 and the consolidation lesson L11; this video teaches the tiny-K drop with its check in the order the site's L11 uses (assume, solve, check), without the site's quadratic.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) type 3 gives no equilibrium value, so the change is unknown: call it x (1, 3); (2) the coefficients give the change row from x (3); (3) equilibrium = initial + change, in terms of x, substituted into K (4); (4) when the top and the bottom are both squares, the whole fraction is a square, so the square root removes the powers; keep the positive root because concentrations are positive (4); (5) rearrange and solve, then check by substituting back, and reject the other root because it would take more than the start (5, 6); (6) only the change row follows the coefficients (7); (7) the root worked because the starts were equal, so both parts were squares, and K need not be a square number (8); (8) when K is tiny, hardly any reactant changes, so x is tiny next to the start, and the start minus x is about the start; drop x there, keep x where it is the answer (9a, 9b); (9) solve the simpler equation (9c); (10) check the shortcut: x under about 5 per cent of the start, and substitute back (9d); (11) HSC questions are written so you should not need the quadratic formula: look for a perfect square or a tiny K (10); (12) attempt on a case where both shortcuts work (11), feedback with both routes and the likely slips (12a to 12c).
- **Entry knowledge:** C1 to C5, C6a and Year 11. The learner knows the three types and the ICE table (C6a), the H2 + I2 settled tube 0.10, 0.10, 0.70 with K = 49 (C6a type 1), K expressions with powers (C5), and from Year 11 maths, rearranging a linear equation and taking a square root. The quadratic formula is not needed and not assumed.
- **Start and stop:** starts with "K and a start are given; nothing at equilibrium is measured"; stops after the learner can set up x from the coefficients, solve a perfect-square case with the positive root, solve a tiny-K case by dropping x with a stated condition and a 5 per cent check, and say that HSC questions are written so the quadratic formula should not be needed (look for a perfect square or a tiny K). No huge-K or product-start case (G03), no Q (C8), no temperature dependence (C9).
- **Handoff:** chem-m5-c07, why factories do not wait for equilibrium (route playlist order 9). If the playlist runs another video next, change the line in scene 13.
- **Learner-visible definitions:** x (the unknown amount, in mol/L, that the reaction uses up of one species); perfect square (something squared, so a square root undoes it); "tiny K" (shown as a power of ten, for example 10^-5, much smaller than the start); "drop the x" (replace "start minus x" by "start" because x is tiny next to it); rule of thumb (a school check, not a law).
- **Units policy:** as C6a: mol/L in the headings and on the givens card; K without a unit, tag "K: no unit written" at each substitution.
- **Examples and why:** (1) The recorded H2 + I2 worked example (start 0.45, 0.45, 0; K = 49; x = 0.35; settled 0.10, 0.10, 0.70) is reused unchanged, now as the perfect-square case. It lands on the exact tube C6a used for type 1, which is the "other way round" link. (2) Tiny K: I2(g) ⇌ 2I(g), start 0.25 mol/L I2 and no atoms, K = 1.6 x 10^-5, labelled "illustrative value". It is a real reaction with the iodine colour the learner knows, it has one reactant so the drop happens in one place, its coefficient of 2 practises (2x)^2 = 4x^2, and its bottom (0.25 - x) is not a square, so the square root cannot be used and the shortcut is needed. The numbers give x = 0.0010 exactly with the shortcut. (3) Attempt: N2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2NO(g) in a very hot engine, K = 4.0 x 10^-4 (illustrative), start 0.50 and 0.50 mol/L, no NO. Unseen reaction, a 1 : 1 : 2 ratio, and both shortcuts work (equal starts and a tiny K), so the feedback can compare the two and show they agree to about 1 per cent.
- **Mistake scene:** none separate. The wrong instincts (writing +x for a coefficient of 2, (2x)^2 as 2x^2, keeping the negative root, dropping x where it is the answer, dropping x without checking, forgetting that the product is 2x, putting one concentration on the bottom when there are two) are corrected in scenes 3, 4, 6, 9b, 9d and 12c.
- **Memory aids** (bank, a draft): "Only the C row follows the coefficients" (recorded, scene 7). New: "Perfect square: square root. Tiny K: drop the x, then check it." (scenes 10, 13, key notes). Not used: "Q chases K" (C8).
- **Reuse (no prototype components):** the C6 board and components (`src/explainer/lessons/chem-m5-c06/`: `SceneKGiven`, `SceneUnknownX`, `SceneSubstitute`, `SceneSolve`, `SceneCheck`, `SceneAid` for the reused takes, with the C6 voiced-review fixes already applied), the ICE table, the givens card, the hot tube, the algebra line (square-root draw-on, a number sliding into a bracket, a term sliding across the equals sign), the C5 expression builder, the countdown ring and the notes board. New: the check ruler (a long bar for the start with a sliver for x, a percentage label and a dashed 5 per cent line; G03 reuses it), a coded hot iodine tube with I2 molecules and a few single I atoms, a coded engine-cylinder vignette for the attempt, and the two-shortcut card.
- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) **Quadratic formula.** No official NESA statement says an HSC question never needs the quadratic formula (Codex check, 15 Oct); the 2019 Chemistry feedback for Question 31 advises "avoiding solving for quadratic equations, making x approximation if appropriate". The learner copy therefore says "HSC questions are written so you shouldn't need it" and "you can use it if you like" (owner decision A, 15 Oct), not that it is never needed or not required. If a student's shortcut check fails, the exact route is the quadratic or a calculator's equation solver; the video says only that "with a K this small and a normal start, it passes easily", which is true for every case in the video. (b) **Where tiny K is most common.** The drop-the-x shortcut is used most in Module 6 weak acid and weak base questions (Ka, Kb); it appears in Module 5 equilibrium questions too, and C6b teaches it here so Module 6 can call it back. (c) **The condition.** The shortcut is safe when the start divided by K is large (a common school guide is more than about 400 to 500 for a 5 per cent error; here 0.25 / 1.6 x 10^-5 is about 16 000, and in the attempt 0.50 / 4.0 x 10^-4 is 1250 with two reactants). The learner copy says "K very small and the start not tiny", then the 5 per cent check after solving, which is the test that matters. (d) **The 5 per cent rule** is a school rule of thumb (said so on screen); with K to two significant figures, a 1 per cent error in x is inside the uncertainty of K. (e) **Iodine K.** Textbook Kc for I2(g) ⇌ 2I(g) is 3.80 x 10^-5 at 1000 K (general-chemistry problem sets; one gives 3.76 x 10^-5); The 3.1 x 10^-5 at 800 K quoted in Brown's Central Science is Kp (in bar), not another Kc: NIST data give Kp = 3.05 x 10^-5 and Kc = Kp / (RT) = 3.05 x 10^-5 / (0.08314 x 800) = 4.6 x 10^-7 at 800 K (the gas moles rise by 1). It is not used to compare with the Kc values (Codex check finding 1). The dissociation absorbs heat (the I-I bond enthalpy is about 151 kJ/mol), so K is smaller at a lower temperature; a van 't Hoff estimate from 3.8 x 10^-5 at 1000 K puts 1.6 x 10^-5 at roughly 950 K, so "very hot" is fair. Kept as an illustrative value because it gives x = 0.0010 exactly and the "÷ 0.25 is x 4" step; the narration says "take it as" and the chip says "illustrative value". A real-value alternative with clean numbers exists (K = 3.8 x 10^-5 at 1000 K with a 0.38 mol/L start gives x = 0.0019 exactly, [I] = 0.0038 mol/L, 0.5 per cent) but its spoken division (4x^2 / 0.38) is harder; see `../checks/c6b-claude-science-check.md`. (f) **NO in an engine.** Textbook Kc for N2 + O2 ⇌ 2NO is 4.1 x 10^-4 at 2000 K (common problem-set value), and a check from standard data (delta H +180.6 kJ/mol, delta S +24.8 J/(K mol)) gives about 3.8 x 10^-4 at 2000 K, so 4.0 x 10^-4 is close to the real K near 2000 K, a fair temperature for the hottest gas in an engine cylinder. The starts are the illustrative part. C5 quoted about 10^-30 at room temperature; the attempt card says "much hotter than in the expressions video, so a different K" (temperature and K is C9). Real engine air has about four times as much nitrogen as oxygen; the equal start is a teaching simplification and is not claimed as real engine air. Codex to confirm the K range. (g) **Reused takes.** Scene 4 is the recorded "substitute" take, which merged the old 6a and 6a2 scenes; it is one scene here. Scene 5 keeps the recorded "Find x. Pause here and try it." wording (root decision 14 Oct). (h) Pronunciation: "I two", "two I" ("iodine atoms" is said first so "two I" is not heard as "two eye" alone), "N two", "O two", "N O" (letters), "ten to the minus five", "sixteen x squared", "per cent", "nought point".

### 3. unknown-x (reuse take: unknown-x)
> We don't know how much reacts, so call the amount of hydrogen used up **x**, in moles per litre. The equation, H two plus I two making **two H I**, gives the rest. For each hydrogen used, one iodine is used and two hydrogen iodide are made. So the change row is **minus x, minus x and plus two x**.

### 4. substitute (reuse take: substitute; substitute and take the square root)
> The equilibrium row is initial plus change: **0.45 minus x** for hydrogen and for iodine, and **two x** for hydrogen iodide. Substitute into K. Hydrogen iodide squared is **two x all squared**, over hydrogen times iodine, and both of those are 0.45 minus x, so the bottom is **0.45 minus x, all squared**. That equals 49. The top is squared and the bottom is squared, so the whole fraction is squared: **two x over 0.45 minus x, all squared, equals 49**. Something squared equals a square number, a perfect square, and 49 is **seven squared**. So take the square root of both sides: two x over 0.45 minus x equals **seven**. Hydrogen iodide and hydrogen are both positive, so this ratio is positive and we keep plus seven.

Visual: as built for C6 (`SceneSubstitute`, with the voiced-review overprint fixes): the Equilibrium row fills "0.45 - x", "0.45 - x", "2x"; "(2x)^2 / (0.45 - x)^2 = 49" with the chip "(2x)^2 = 2x times 2x, not 2x^2"; the rewrite "(2x / (0.45 - x))^2 = 7^2"; on "a perfect square" the label "perfect square: something squared" points at the bracketed fraction on the left, and "49 = 7^2" writes small under the 49 on "seven squared" (so the screen ties the term to the fraction, which scene 8 then states in words); the root signs draw over both sides; "2x / (0.45 - x) = 7" with a tick on "plus seven" and "[HI] / [H2] > 0". ### 8. square-condition (why the square root worked)
> Why did the square root work so neatly? Hydrogen and iodine **started equal**, so the bottom of the fraction was 0.45 minus x, times itself, a **square**, just like the top. K does not have to be a square number. If K were **50**, the root would be about 7.07, and the method would still work. What it needs is a top and a bottom that are **both squares**, so the whole fraction is something squared. That is what a perfect square means from now on: the fraction, not the number K. Many questions are not built like that, and the most common other case is a **tiny K**.

Visual: the K equation "(2x)^2 / ((0.45 - x)(0.45 - x)) = 49" returns large. ### 9a. tiny-k-setup (hot iodine: x and the equilibrium row)

Each I two makes two atoms, so the change row is **minus x and plus two x**, and the equilibrium row is **0.25 minus x** and two x. So K is two x all squared, over 0.25 minus x.

### 9b. tiny-k-drop (the condition and the drop)
> The bottom is not a square, so the square root will not help. But look at the size of K. A tiny K means hardly any I two splits, so **x is tiny** next to 0.25. Taking a tiny amount from 0.25 still leaves **about 0.25**, so we **drop the x** from the bottom. Keep the x on **top**, because that is the answer we want. This shortcut needs two things: **K very small**, and a **start that is not tiny**.

Visual: the check ruler (new) draws under the equation: a long purple bar labelled "start: 0.25" and, at its end, a hair-thin sliver labelled "x?" on "x is tiny". On "about 0.25" the bar with the sliver taken off is laid over the full bar: they look the same length. On "top" a pen ring holds the "(2x)^2" with the tag "keep: this is the answer". ### 9c. tiny-k-solve (solve the easier equation)
> Now the equation is easy. Two x all squared is four x squared, so four x squared over 0.25 equals 1.6 times ten to the minus five. **Multiply both sides by the bottom**, 0.25: four x squared is **4.0 times ten to the minus six**. Then **divide both sides by four**: x squared is 1.0 times ten to the minus six, so x is **0.0010** moles per litre. The iodine atoms are two x, **0.0020** moles per litre, and the I two is still about 0.25.

Visual: the algebra line transforms one stage at a time, each step on its own line under the last: "4x^2 / 0.25 = 1.6 x 10^-5"; on "Multiply both sides by the bottom" a pen arrow carries the 0.25 across to the right side with the aside "multiply both sides by the bottom", giving "4x^2 = 1.6 x 10^-5 x 0.25 = 4.0 x 10^-6"; on "divide both sides by four" the aside "divide by 4" and "x^2 = 1.0 x 10^-6"; then "x = 0.0010 mol/L" ticks. The atoms "2x = 0.0020 mol/L" and "[I2] ≈ 0.25 mol/L" write on as spoken.

### 9d. tiny-k-check (check the shortcut)
> Now check that dropping x was fair. x is 0.0010, and 0.0010 out of 0.25 is **0.4 per cent**.
