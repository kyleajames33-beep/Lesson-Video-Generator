Main question: *Dolly the sheep and a flask of bacteria copying one gene are both called cloning. So what does each kind of cloning copy, how is the copy made, and how well does each do the job it's meant for?*
Entry knowledge: see above. Students start Year 12 and have watched the Module 5 reproduction, cell division and DNA videos and Module 6 b01 to b15. They know that a clone has its parent's alleles, that mitosis copies chromosomes exactly, that bacteria carry plasmids, that mitochondria carry a little DNA, that phenotype depends on genes and environment, and that cloning sits in the "copies DNA" kind of biotechnology. They have not met nuclear transfer, whom a clone matches, why cloning often fails, vectors and hosts, PCR, or how to assess effectiveness against a purpose.
Stops after: defining a clone as a genetically identical copy and separating whole-organism cloning (a new individual with the same nuclear DNA as an existing one) from gene cloning (many copies of one selected piece of DNA); tracing somatic cell nuclear transfer with Dolly (a body cell from a white-faced ewe's udder; an egg from a black-faced ewe with its nucleus removed; an electric pulse fuses them and starts division; the embryo carried by a black-faced surrogate); explaining that the clone's nuclear DNA matches the nucleus donor while its mitochondrial DNA comes from the egg, and that development and environment can still make it differ; assessing whole-organism cloning by reliability (277 fused cells, 29 embryos, one lamb, because the egg often resets the body-cell nucleus's gene switches only partly), identity and health (pregnancy losses and abnormal births are common; Dolly's illnesses do not show early ageing; later clones from her cells aged healthily), with a verdict by purpose (too unreliable for routine breeding; worth it to copy one rare or valuable animal, as with the black-footed ferret); tracing gene cloning (a gene in a plasmid vector, taken up by a bacterial host, copied at every division into billions of copies; PCR copies a gene without cells) and assessing it (very effective for a narrow, checkable aim: one bacterium is enough; colonies must be checked for the gene; rare copying errors); distinguishing the two by what exists at the end; applying this to an unseen assess item and an unseen distinguish item.
Handoff: how scientists cut a gene out, join it into a plasmid, get it into a host and make it work there (route entry bio-m6-b18).

### 7. gene cloning
> Gene cloning has a narrower aim: lots of copies of one gene. Scientists put the gene into a **plasmid**, a small ring of DNA that bacteria carry. Here the plasmid is a **vector**, a carrier that moves DNA into a cell. The bacterium that takes it up is the **host**. Each time the host divides, it copies the plasmid as well as its own DNA, so the new cells get the gene. Bacteria can divide every half hour or so, so by the next day one cell becomes billions. There's also a way to copy a gene with no cells, called **PCR**. Heat separates the DNA's two strands, then, as it cools, an enzyme copies each one, so every cycle doubles the gene.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- A clone is a genetically identical copy. Whole-organism cloning makes a new individual with the same nuclear DNA as an existing one; gene cloning makes many copies of one selected piece of DNA.
- Somatic cell nuclear transfer: an egg's nucleus is removed; a body cell from the donor is fused with the empty egg (for Dolly, by an electric pulse), and the egg is stimulated to divide; the embryo is placed in a surrogate. The clone's nuclear DNA matches the nucleus donor; its mitochondrial DNA comes mainly from the egg.
- Whole-organism cloning: low reliability (Dolly: 277 fused cells, 29 embryos, 1 lamb), because the egg often resets the body-cell nucleus's gene switches only partly; pregnancy losses and abnormal births are common; a clone can still differ in phenotype. It can be worth it to copy a rare or valuable animal, but many copies of one animal narrow a gene pool.
- Gene cloning: a gene is put into a plasmid (the vector) and taken up by a bacterium (the host), which copies it every time it divides; PCR copies a gene without cells (heat separates the strands, then an enzyme copies each one). Very effective for its narrow aim (one host is enough; easy to check), but colonies must be checked for the gene.
- To distinguish them, ask what exists at the end: a new organism, or many copies of one gene.

Memory: The clone matches the nucleus donor. Gene cloning copies a gene; organism cloning copies an individual.

Recheck near recording.

- **Gene cloning outline.** A gene inserted into a plasmid vector is taken up by bacteria (transformation) and replicated with each division; E. coli can divide about every 20 to 30 minutes in good conditions, so an overnight culture reaches billions of cells (OpenStax Microbiology 12.1; NIH genome.gov "Plasmid" and "Cloning"). Revision 2 wording "every half hour or so, so by the next day one cell becomes billions": at a 30 minute doubling time one cell needs about 16 hours to pass 4 billion (2^32), and growth slows as nutrients run out, so "by the next day" replaces "by morning" (science check finding 2; OpenStax Microbiology 9.1). Plasmids replicate separately from the chromosome and are occasionally lost at division, so the narration says "copies the plasmid as well as its own DNA, so the new cells get the gene" (finding 8).
- **PCR.** Repeated cycles of heating (strand separation, about 95 degrees Celsius) and cooling (primer binding, then extension) roughly double a target sequence each cycle (OpenStax Biology 2e, 17.1). Revision 2 wording: "Heat separates the DNA's two strands, then, as it cools, an enzyme copies each one, so every cycle doubles the gene." Heat only separates the strands; a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq) does the copying, extending primers after cooling (science check finding 1). The enzyme, primers and the 72 degree extension step are not named; "an enzyme" is the plain subject of the copying.
