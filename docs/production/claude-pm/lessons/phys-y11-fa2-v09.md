### 2. what we observe
> Here's what happens. The ringing gets quieter and quieter, until we can barely hear it at all. But we can still see the hammer striking the bell, just as hard as before. Let the air back in, and the ringing comes straight **back**. The bell hangs on soft threads, so the sound can't escape through its supports. The only way out is through the air in the jar. So the sound needs the **air**.

### 3. mechanical waves need a medium
> Sound is a mechanical wave. A mechanical wave travels by moving the particles of a material, which we call its medium. The cord, the spring, water and air are all media. The wave is passed on from particle to particle. So a mechanical wave needs a medium to travel, or as physicists say, to propagate. With no particles at all, which is a vacuum, there's nothing to pass it **on**.

Visual: four small panels in a row, each from earlier videos (coded): v03's cord, v04's spring, a coded water surface with a bobbing marker, v04's air row; each with the tag "medium: cord", "spring", "water", "air" (gloss "mechanical wave: travels by moving the particles of a material; medium: that material"). ### 4. how sound passes energy along
> So how does sound pass its energy along? Zoom in on the air in front of a loudspeaker. The cone moves forward and pushes on the layer of air touching it. A push through a distance is work, so the cone gives those particles energy. They crowd together, a compression, and push on the next layer, which pushes on the next. Then the cone moves back, and the air beside it spreads out, a rarefaction. Each particle only moves back and forth near home. But each one pushes on its neighbour, and hands energy on, so the energy travels away from the **cone**.

### 5. the energy arrives
> Put a microphone across the room. Its thin diaphragm sits in the air. Each compression that arrives pushes the diaphragm in, and each rarefaction lets it move back out. So the diaphragm vibrates at the same frequency as the cone. The air that touched the cone never gets there. It's the energy, handed on particle by particle, that **arrives**.

### 6. back to the jar
> Now back to the jar. As the air is pumped out, there are fewer and fewer particles. The vibrating bell has less air to push on, so it hands less energy to the air, and less reaches the glass. The sound fades. In a perfect vacuum, there'd be nothing to push at all, so no sound could leave the bell. The sound isn't trapped in the jar, and it isn't just quieter. It can't travel at all. That's why space is **silent**. We still see the hammer, because light doesn't need a medium. That's a different kind of wave, coming **later**.

### 7. prediction: stiff or soft?
> Sound needs a medium. But does the medium change how fast it travels? Here are two rows of particles joined by springs. In one row, the springs are stiff, like the bonds in a solid. In the other, they're soft, more like a gas. Push the first particle in each row at the same moment. Which push reaches the end **first**?

Visual: board change: the spring-chain model (two coded rows of identical dots joined by drawn springs; top "stiff springs", thick coils; bottom "soft springs", thin coils; tag "a model, not to scale"). ### 8. the medium sets the speed
> The stiff row wins. A stiff spring pushes back hard when it's squeezed even a little, so each particle gets its neighbour moving sooner. That's why sound travels at about three hundred and forty metres per second in air, about fifteen hundred in water, and about five thousand in **steel**. It isn't just that steel is denser. On its own, heavier particles would be harder to get moving, and that would slow the wave down. Stiffness is what makes solids **fast**.

Visual: the push runs along both rows (author note d): the slate crowding races along the stiff row and reaches the end while the soft row's is a third of the way (the "stiff row?" chip fills; gloss "stiff: pushes back hard when squeezed a little"). On "fast" the stiff row pulses with "stiffness".

### 9. worked example: one fork, two media
> So the medium sets the speed, and the source sets the frequency. Task: a tuning fork vibrates at five hundred and twelve hertz, and sound travels through the air at three hundred and forty metres per second. Find the wavelength of its sound. Lambda is v over f, three hundred and forty over five hundred and twelve, zero point six six **metres**. Now imagine the same fork vibrating in water, where sound travels at about fifteen hundred metres per second. The frequency is still five hundred and twelve hertz, set by the fork.
