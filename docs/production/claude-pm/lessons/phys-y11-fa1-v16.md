## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route, root wording).** "How do we find the forces on an object that is speeding up, slowing down or turning?" The answer, built in order: work out which way the velocity is changing; that is the acceleration's direction, and the net force points the same way (2, 3, 7); find its size from ma (2, 5 to 7); then the free-body diagram gives the unknown force (5 to 8). Speeding up: net force along the velocity (2). Slowing down: net force against the velocity (3). Vertical with support: the support force adjusts so the net force matches the acceleration (4, 5). Vertical with air resistance: the net force, and so the acceleration, shrinks as the drag grows, down to zero at terminal velocity (6). Turning: the net force points to the inside of the bend (7).

### 2. speeding up
> Let's rewind to the push. The crate has a mass of four point oh kilograms. A push of twenty newtons acts forward, and friction from the floor acts backward, with eight point oh newtons. Take forward as positive. The net force is twenty minus eight point oh, which is twelve newtons **forward**. So the acceleration is twelve divided by four point oh, three point oh metres per second squared, forward. The velocity and the acceleration point the same way, so the crate speeds **up**.

### 3. slowing down
> Now the push stops. The crate is still sliding forward, so friction still acts backward, eight point oh newtons. It's the only horizontal force left, so it's the net force. The acceleration is minus eight point oh over four point oh, minus two point oh metres per second squared. The crate loses two metres per second, every second, until it **stops**. So the answer is backward. The net force points against the motion, and that's exactly why the crate slows down. A moving object doesn't need a forward net force. The net force sets how the velocity changes, not which way it **points**.

### 6. a falling crate and air resistance

### 7. turning
> An object can accelerate without changing speed. A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Its direction changes, so its velocity changes, and over any short interval the change points toward the inside of the bend. So the acceleration points inward, and so must the net force. Here, it comes from friction between the road and the tyres, pushing sideways toward the inside. If the twelve hundred kilogram car's acceleration toward the inside is three point oh metres per second squared, the net force is twelve hundred times three point oh, three thousand six hundred newtons, **inward**. On ice, friction can't supply that much, so the car carries on straighter, just as the first law **predicts**.
