### 2. the data: two successes, unevenly spread
> First, the data, in four long-running trends. One, a growing share of Australians is older, and the very oldest group grows fastest, which matters because care needs climb steeply with age. Two, there are two causes, not one. People live longer, and fewer babies are born, because families can now choose how many children to have, below the level that replaces each generation. So the pyramid's base narrows as its top widens. That's two successes, not a failure. Three, years of life are rising, and years lived with illness are rising too. Four, it's uneven. Regional towns are ageing faster than cities, as young people leave and retirees arrive, so the oldest places often have the thinnest services. And be careful with the **dependency ratio**. It counts ages, not what people do. Many older people work, care and volunteer.

### 3. healthy ageing: ability, not diagnosis
> So what's healthy ageing? **Healthy ageing** means building and keeping the **functional ability** to do what you value: get around, meet your own needs, learn and decide, keep relationships, and contribute. Three things follow. Someone with several well-managed conditions can be ageing healthily, because this isn't a diagnosis count. The environment is part of it, because the same person can do more in a suburb with footpaths, seats and buses than in one without. And the goal is **compression of morbidity**: pushing the onset of disability later, so the stretch of poor health at the end is shorter, even as life gets longer. More years isn't the target. More healthy years is.

### 4. opportunities first, then challenges

### 5. strategies by where they act
> So which strategies help? Group them by where they act on that bar. Prevention across life acts earliest: activity, nutrition, not smoking, and blood pressure care from midlife. It's slowest to show, and biggest in effect. Maintaining function acts at the turning point: strength and balance training, falls prevention, vision and hearing checks, and medicine reviews. Falls prevention stands out, because one fall can end independence. Age-friendly places and social connection turn the same body into more ability. And care design acts on the final stretch: support to age at home, coordinated care, and dementia and carer support, with technology extending the workforce, never replacing it. Here's the catch. Most spending sits on the final stretch. Most of the gain sits earlier. It costs now, and pays in twenty years.

### 6. what agencies must consider
> Finally, what must government and non-government agencies consider? The population is growing as well as ageing, so demand rises twice over. Plan for where demand will be, not just how much. Build the workforce early, because training takes years. Decide who pays, because relying on out-of-pocket costs carries the income gradient into old age. Fund prevention, even though the benefit arrives after the next election. Join up health, aged care, disability and housing, because older people fall between them. Remember that older people aren't all the same. Women, for example, often live longer on smaller savings. And design services with them. Non-government agencies deliver much of the care, but on short contracts that don't match a twenty-year problem.
