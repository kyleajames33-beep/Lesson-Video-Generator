### 6. two flows keep the gradient steep
> It would slow down, and then stop. Oxygen would keep entering the still blood until the oxygen partial pressure, meaning the oxygen pressure on each side, was about equal. Breathing replaces the air in the sacs, bringing in oxygen and carrying carbon dioxide out. Neither flow ever stops for long.

> So where does energy come in? The lung spends no energy moving the gases across that wall. Every molecule diffuses down its own gradient. So the lungs never pump oxygen into the blood.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (2 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Transit and loading time (scene 1).** At rest a red cell spends about 0.75 s in a pulmonary capillary; blood oxygen reaches near equilibrium with alveolar air in about 0.25 s (standard respiratory physiology, for example West's Respiratory Physiology). "less than a second ... about a third of that time": correct at rest. During exercise transit falls towards 0.25 s (not spoken).
- **Thousand-balloon calculation (scene 3).** Dividing one sphere of volume V into n equal spheres of the same total volume multiplies the total surface by n^(1/3); n = 1,000 gives 10 ×, each small sphere having one tenth the radius. Correct. The bag plate's balloons are fewer than 1,000; the overlay balls carry the count (grey tag).
- **Number and area (scene 3).** Adult lungs contain about 300 to 500 million alveoli (Ochs et al. 2004: mean about 480 million). Alveolar surface about 50 to 100 m², often quoted as about 70 m². Revision 2 says "about seventy square metres" (root decision 23's fallback; site error 3 and Open decision 2). Grey tag "estimates vary".
- **Barrier (scene 4).** The blood-air barrier (type I alveolar cell, fused basement membranes, capillary endothelial cell) averages about 0.6 µm, thinnest parts about 0.2 µm. "two thin cells, less than a thousandth of a millimetre": correct (the shared basement membrane is not mentioned; author note). Plate tag "drawn about 5,000 times larger" puts 0.6 µm at about 3 mm on a 1080p frame scale: Codex to adjust the tag to the plate's actual drawn barrier thickness.
- **Single file (scene 4).** Pulmonary capillaries are about 5 to 8 µm across; red cells (about 7.5 µm) deform and pass in single file against the wall. Correct.
- **Water film (scene 4).** Alveoli are lined by a thin liquid layer (with surfactant; author note); gases dissolve in it before crossing. Correct.
- **Alveolus size (scene 10).** Adult alveoli about 0.2 to 0.3 mm across. "about a quarter of a millimetre": correct. Balloon rubber about 0.1 mm (grey "estimate"; latex party balloons about 0.05 to 0.2 mm before inflation, thinner when inflated). Ratio statement on screen only.
- **Gradient and equilibrium (scene 6).** Without flow, the blood's oxygen would approach the alveolar air's level and net diffusion would stop. Correct. Revision 2 states it as partial pressure ("until the oxygen partial pressure, meaning the oxygen pressure on each side, was about equal"), since total oxygen content includes haemoglobin-bound oxygen and does not drive diffusion.
- **Energy (scene 7).** No active transport of O₂ or CO₂ across the alveolar wall; the heart and breathing muscles use ATP to drive blood flow and ventilation. Correct.
- **Emphysema (11c, 11d).** Destruction of alveolar walls reduces surface area; the site states walls thicken with damage (fibrosis and remodelling can thicken remaining walls; emphysema's dominant change is surface loss, author note). Breathlessness on exertion; increased ventilation cannot fully compensate. Correct at this level; the stem states the thickening as a given, so the answer does not depend on it being universal.
- **Time-sensitive facts spoken.** None.
- **No em dashes** in this file (searched by script).
