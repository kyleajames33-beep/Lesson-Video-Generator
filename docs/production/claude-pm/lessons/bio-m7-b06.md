## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (22 Oct) against the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Metchnikoff's thorn.** In December 1882, at Messina, Élie (Ilya) Metchnikoff introduced rose thorns into transparent starfish larvae and found the next morning that mobile cells had surrounded them; he went on to name these cells phagocytes (his own later account, and Tauber 2003, *Nature Reviews Molecular Cell Biology* 4: 897 to 901; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1908, shared with Ehrlich). "By the next morning, moving cells had crowded around the thorn": matches his account. "A starfish larva makes no antibodies": echinoderms have no antibodies (no immunoglobulin-based adaptive immunity). Correct. Narration calls him a zoologist (he trained and worked as one). Pronounce "Élie Metchnikoff" as "ay-LEE METCH-nik-off".
- **Barriers.** The stratum corneum is dead, keratin-filled cells that are continually shed (desquamation), removing surface microbes. Mucus traps particles; cilia beat to move it towards the pharynx, where it is swallowed (mucociliary clearance). Gastric acid (pH about 1.5 to 3.5) kills many ingested microbes. Lysozyme in tears, saliva and other secretions hydrolyses peptidoglycan, most effectively in Gram-positive bacteria, so "breaks down the walls of many bacteria" is hedged correctly. Correct.
- **Innate immune system.** Present from birth; recognises molecular patterns shared by many pathogens using inherited receptors; acts within minutes to hours; classically no memory. "Respond in much the same way to many different pathogens": correct. "Doesn't get better with practice": correct for the HSC's classical distinction. Author note: "trained immunity" (epigenetic changes that make some innate cells respond more strongly later) is an active research area (the site notes it too); it is not in learner copy because it does not create pathogen-specific memory.
- **Mast cells and histamine.** Mast cells are tissue-resident, concentrated near small blood vessels and under skin and mucosal surfaces; on tissue damage, complement fragments (C3a, C5a) or allergen binding they release granules containing histamine. Histamine widens arterioles (more blood flow) and increases the permeability of post-capillary venules (plasma leaks out). Narration says "small blood vessels" and "those vessels widen ... their walls leaky" (the plate shows one small vessel standing for both). Correct at this level.
- **Four signs.** Redness and local warmth from increased blood flow; swelling (oedema) from fluid leaking into tissue; pain from bradykinin stimulating pain receptors, prostaglandins sensitising them, and pressure from swelling. "Chemicals made at the site, plus the pressure of the swelling, set off pain nerves": correct. Purpose: delivery of leukocytes and plasma proteins (complement, clotting proteins, antibodies if present) to the site. Correct.
- **Neutrophil recruitment.** Chemokines (such as CXCL8), complement C5a and bacterial products form a concentration gradient; neutrophils roll on and then firmly attach to activated endothelium (selectins, then integrins), cross the wall mostly between endothelial cells (diapedesis), and migrate up the gradient (chemotaxis). Neutrophils are usually the first leukocytes recruited in large numbers, arriving within hours and peaking around 6 to 24 hours. "Squeeze out between the cells of its wall": mostly true (some cross through a cell); acceptable. Correct.
- **Phagocytosis.** Pattern recognition receptors (and receptors for antibody and complement coatings) bind the microbe; pseudopodia surround it; the membrane closes to form a phagosome; lysosomes fuse to form a phagolysosome; hydrolytic enzymes and reactive oxygen species destroy it. Macrophages are larger (about 15 to 25 µm) tissue phagocytes; neutrophils about 12 to 15 µm. Correct.
- **Pus.** Mainly dead and dying neutrophils with bacteria, debris and tissue fluid. "A sign the response has been fighting there" (Revision 1 wording, site error 7). Neutrophils live hours to a day or two once in tissue. Correct.
- **Fever.** Pyrogenic cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) reach the hypothalamus, where prostaglandin E2 raises the temperature set point; a moderate rise can enhance some immune functions and slow some pathogens. A small local infection usually causes no fever, so the narration conditions it: "If an infection spreads". Narration says only "raise the body's temperature"; the screen tag names the brain's set point (the hypothalamus is not named). Correct.
- **Dendritic cells.** Tissue-resident dendritic cells (in the epidermis, Langerhans cells; in the dermis, dermal dendritic cells) take up antigen, migrate along afferent lymph vessels to the draining lymph node and present antigen fragments on MHC molecules to T cells that haven't met their antigen, starting the adaptive response; a primary adaptive response takes days (about 4 to 7 days for T cell expansion; antibodies typically detectable after about a week). "Show pieces of the bacteria to T cells": correct. Long branching processes: correct.
- **"Often, the innate response clears a small infection on its own."** Hedged (site error 15). Correct as hedged.
- **Attempt 1.** Based on the dye leak (Miles) test: Evans blue binds albumin; intradermal histamine causes plasma protein extravasation, seen as a blue patch, within minutes. Original wording; correct.
- **Attempt 2.** Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I (defective β2 integrin, CD18): neutrophils cannot firmly adhere to endothelium, so blood neutrophil counts are high, bacterial skin and soft tissue infections recur, and pus formation is absent or reduced. Original wording, condition not named; correct.
