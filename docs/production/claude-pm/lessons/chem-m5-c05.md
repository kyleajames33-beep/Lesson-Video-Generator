### 3b. the pattern for any equation (and a check)
> We have shown why for two reactions. Real reactions are often several smaller steps, but measurements on many reactions give the same pattern, so we use it as the rule for any balanced equation, for the equation exactly as given. Products go on top, reactants underneath, and each concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient. Our settled tube has two, two and fourteen, and fourteen squared over two times two is a hundred and ninety-six over four, forty-nine. A tube that started with more iodine than hydrogen settles at two, eight and twenty-eight, and twenty-eight squared over two times eight is seven hundred and eighty-four over sixteen, forty-nine again. Leave the power off and you get three and a half, then one point seven five, so the ratio no longer stays put.

### 4. writing one from scratch (the cobalt tube)

### 5. why a solid or the solvent is left out
> Why leave out the solvent, or a solid? Because their concentration does not change while the reaction runs. Remember the rate equations: a number times the concentrations. If one concentration never changes, it is just another fixed number, so it folds into the fixed numbers we already have, and K already includes it. We met this with carbon in the squeeze video, which squeezing could not make any more crowded. So for carbon plus carbon dioxide making two carbon monoxide, K is the carbon monoxide squared over the carbon dioxide, and the carbon never appears. If water is a gas, as steam, its concentration changes, so it counts.

Visual: a beaker of solution with a few reacting dots and many water dots; on "does not change" a counter beside the beaker (water about 55 mol/L, no more than "very large and steady") stays level while a second counter for a dissolved species ticks up and down; a pen ring on "solvent". ### 6. what the size of K tells you
> What does the number tell us? K is products over reactants, so the bigger it is, the more the settled mixture leans towards products. As a rough guide, above about a thousand means almost all products, and below about a thousandth means almost all reactants. In between, you find measurable amounts of both. Hydrogen and iodine at four hundred and fifty degrees have a K of forty-nine: in between, so our tube holds a lot of hydrogen iodide, with some hydrogen and iodine left. Nitrogen and oxygen making nitric oxide have a K near ten to the minus thirty at room temperature, so almost no nitric oxide forms. Copper ions with plenty of ammonia make a deep blue ion with a K near ten to the thirteen, so almost all the copper ends up in that ion. K tells you where the mixture settles, not how fast it gets there.

### 11. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- At a given temperature, the ratio of products to reactants written from the balanced equation, with each concentration raised to the power of its coefficient, stays constant for a settled mixture, even when a disturbance changes every amount. Its value is the equilibrium constant, K, also written Keq.
- The equilibrium expression for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD is K = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b), written for the equation as given. Products go on top, reactants underneath. Square brackets go around the whole ion, including its own brackets and charge.
- Pure solids, a pure liquid and water as the solvent do not appear, because their concentration does not change, so it is already part of K. Gases and aqueous species do appear, and so does water as a gas, H2O(g).
- Rough guides (for typical laboratory concentrations): above about 10^3, the settled mixture is almost all products; below about 10^-3, almost all reactants; in between, measurable amounts of both. K says where the mixture settles, not how fast.
