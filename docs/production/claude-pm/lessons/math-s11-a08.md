## Decisions

- **Anchor: how much cardboard? (a07's final frame).** Surface area is the area of everything you'd have to cover, wrap or paint: the outside skin. The box unfolds flat into its net, so a 3D question becomes a05a's flat areas.
- **Surface area against volume, said once.** "Surface area is the outside skin, in square units. Volume, next time, is the space inside, in cubic units."
- **Prisms: pairs, or perimeter times length.** The box's three pairs of rectangles; the chocolate box (a triangular prism) has two triangle ends plus a strip of rectangles that unrolls to one long rectangle: the end's perimeter times the length. Its third edge needs a06's Pythagoras.
- **The cylinder's label (simulation).** The tin's label peels off and flattens into a rectangle whose width is the circumference: curved area = 2πr × h. Closed: add two circles; open: one.
- **The sphere as four circles.** The golf ball's surface equals exactly four circles of its own radius (Archimedes' result, shown, not proved). A hemisphere has half the curved surface plus a new flat circle: 3πr².
- **Composite and partial solids by a face list (recurring model).** Each face is listed and ticked "exposed" or crossed "hidden" before any number (the site's "list before you add"). The polytunnel covers its curved roof and two ends, but not its floor.

### 1. hook: how much cardboard?
> A cereal box is thirty centimetres tall, twenty wide and eight deep. How much cardboard does it take to make one? That's a question about the outside skin, every face you'd have to cover. Its total is called the surface area, the total area of all the outside faces. It's measured in square units, because it's flat cardboard. Next time we'll measure the space inside, the volume, in cubic units. Same box, a different question. So how do we find the surface area of prisms, cylinders, spheres and solids made from **them**?

### 2. a box: unfold the net
> The easiest way to see every face is to unfold the box flat. That flat shape is called a net, the solid unfolded with every face showing once. A box has six faces, in three matching pairs. The front and back are thirty by twenty, six hundred square centimetres each. The two sides are thirty by eight, two hundred and forty each. And the top and bottom are twenty by eight, a hundred and sixty each. So the surface area is two lots of six hundred plus two forty plus one sixty, two thousand square **centimetres**, ignoring the flaps that glue it together. Listing the pairs first means you can't forget a **face**.

### 3. a triangular prism: perimeter times length
> A box is one kind of prism, a solid with the same shape all the way through. A chocolate bar box with triangle ends is another. Its ends are right-angled triangles with shorter sides of nine and twelve centimetres, and the box is twenty centimetres long. The two ends are half of nine times twelve, fifty-four each, so a hundred and eight altogether. The sides are three rectangles, one for each edge of the triangle. But we only know two of the edges. The third is the hypotenuse, the square root of nine squared plus twelve squared, fifteen **centimetres**. Now here's a shortcut. Unroll the three sides and they make one long rectangle. Its width is the triangle's perimeter, nine plus twelve plus fifteen, thirty-six, and its length is twenty. That's seven hundred and twenty. Altogether, eight hundred and twenty-eight square **centimetres**. Five faces, and none **missing**.

### 4. a cylinder: unroll the label
> A food tin is a cylinder, a prism with a circle at each end. This one has a radius of four centimetres and a height of eleven. The two ends are circles, pi r squared each. The curved side is the tricky part, so peel off the label. Flat, it's a rectangle. Its height is the tin's height, eleven. Its width is the distance once round the tin, the circumference, two pi r. So the curved area is two pi r times h, eighty-eight pi, two hundred and seventy-six point four six square **centimetres**. Add the two ends, thirty-two pi, and the whole tin is a hundred and twenty pi, three hundred and seventy-six point nine nine square **centimetres**. If a cylinder is open at the top, like a cup or a tank with no lid, only count one **end**.

### 5. a sphere: four circles
> A sphere is a perfectly round ball, like a golf ball. There's no net for a sphere, but there's a lovely fact. The surface of a sphere is exactly four circles with the same radius. A golf ball is about four point two seven centimetres across, so its radius is two point one three five. Four pi r squared is about fifty-seven point two eight square **centimetres**. Now cut a sphere in half, and you get a hemisphere, half a sphere. Its curved part is half of four pi r squared, two pi r squared. But cutting it made a brand new flat face, a circle, pi r squared. So a solid hemisphere has three pi r squared altogether. Forgetting that flat face is the classic **slip**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 3): the golf ball `ma11-golf-ball-v1.png` (about 420 px) at the left; grey chip "sphere: a perfectly round ball". On "hemisphere" the ball splits into two halves; grey chip "hemisphere: half a sphere"; on "two pi r squared" the dome lights "2πr²"; on "brand new flat face" the flat circle lights "+ πr²", hand tag "plus the flat face"; row "hemisphere = 2πr² + πr² = 3πr²". On "slip" a grey ghost "2πr² only" flashes and fades.

### 6. composite and partial solids: list the faces
> Real objects are often parts of solids joined together. This garden polytunnel is half a cylinder lying on its side, three metres wide, so a radius of one point five, and six metres long. The plastic covers the curved roof and the two half-circle ends, but not the floor, which is the ground. So, list first. Roof, exposed. Two ends, exposed. Floor, hidden. The roof is half of two pi r times the length, nine pi. The two half-circle ends make one whole circle, pi times one point five squared, two point two five pi. That's eleven point two five pi, about thirty-five point three four square metres of **plastic**. Adding the floor as well would buy eighteen square metres you don't **need**.
