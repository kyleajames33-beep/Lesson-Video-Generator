## Decisions

- **Site sources used:** lesson 13 Activity 3: n = 2 homologous pairs, "two long chromosomes and two short, with one of each colour representing the maternal and paternal copy"; beads or paper strips; crossing over by "swapping a segment between one homologous pair" and recording the new combination; lining the pairs up "in every possible orientation" and listing gamete types, confirming 2^n = 4; random "fertilisation" of two modelled gametes and recording the zygote; stating which step contributed most variation. Lesson 13 Predict walkthrough: four gamete types for two unlinked heterozygous loci, each about a quarter, "expected rather than guaranteed ... a small sample can deviate substantially by chance alone" (the basis of scene 10 and the attempt). Lesson 08: one copying, two divisions, 2n to n, fertilisation restores 2n; 23 pairs in humans give over eight million whole-chromosome combinations (scene 12).
- **Site additions (recorded here only):** (1) **Mutation step.** The syllabus dotpoint names mutations, but site Activity 3 has no mutation step. This design adds one: before one fertilisation, one allele sticker in one gamete is replaced by a new allele in a new colour (amber, B8's colour rule: amber only for a new allele). (2) **Gene choice.** The site's chromosomes carry no named genes; this design puts two genes on the long pair (so a crossover between them gives new combinations) and one on the short pair (so assortment can be seen), following B8's model. The organism is "a model plant" with invented genes (flower colour, height, seed shape), so no real linkage claim is made. (3) **Repeated fertilisations and a tally.** The site records one zygote; this design repeats the random draw to give a class tally for one gene, so the student can compare observed with expected and see why repeats matter. (4) **Partner group as the second parent**, so two separate meioses supply the gametes.
- **Site's "which step contributed the most variation":** not asked as a closed question, because the answer depends on the numbers of genes and chromosomes modelled. Scene 11 says what each step adds; scene 12 notes that with only two pairs the model hugely understates assortment.
- **Main teaching move:** build one parent cell with colour-tracked chromosomes and labelled alleles, copy, cross over at matching points, randomise each pair with a coin, divide twice, record the gametes, fertilise by random draw with a partner group's gametes, add one deliberate mutation, tally offspring for one gene, compare with the prediction, then assess the model (strengths, limits, validity and reliability).
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) the model can predict which offspring genotypes are possible, and test those predictions against chance (1); (2) colours track where each chromosome came from (2); (3) matching sticker positions because homologues carry the same genes at the same places (3); (4) copying before meiosis gives two sister chromatids, still one chromosome (4); (5) breaking at matching points means a crossover exchanges the same genes, so nothing is lost or doubled, and it makes new combinations (5); (6) a coin per pair makes each pair's direction random and independent (6); (7) without crossing over there are 2 x 2 = 4 gamete types; crossing over adds rarer mixed types (7); (8) a random draw models random fertilisation and restores two of each chromosome (8); (9) only mutation makes a new allele, and only in a gamete-making cell is it passed on (9); (10) observed counts differ from expected by chance; pooling brings them closer (10); (11) assessment: what the model represents, what it leaves out, and how to keep it valid and make it reliable (11 to 13).
- **Entry knowledge:** B5a (chromosome, homologous pair, diploid, haploid, sister chromatids, count centromeres), B5b (one copy, two divisions; prophase I pairing and crossing over; metaphase I either way round, independently; anaphase I partners separate; meiosis II chromatids separate; 2^n; fertilisation restores the number), B8 (four sources; reshuffle against new allele; gamete types for two genes; crossover combinations rarer; mutation inherited only in the egg or sperm line). Allele and genotype from B5b and B8. Punnett squares are **not** assumed (B9 comes next); the expected 1 : 2 : 1 in scene 10 is given from the gamete reasoning in one clause ("each parent gives either allele half the time") with a grey tag "Punnett squares: next video".
- **Learner-visible vocabulary (plain meaning at first use):** model (a simplified representation used to explain or predict); expected (what the model predicts on average); observed (what you actually got). Biology terms recalled, not introduced.
- **Model data (on screen):** Parent cell: long plum chromosome P (purple) and T (tall); long teal p (white) and t (short); short plum R (round); short teal r (wrinkled). Gamete types without crossing over: P T R, P T r, p t R, p t r. Extra with a crossover between the two long-chromosome genes: P t R, P t r, p T R, p T r. Second parent (partner group): the same genotype. Class tally for the seed-shape gene from 16 random fertilisations (example data): RR 3, Rr 9, rr 4; expected 4, 8, 4.
- **Attempt system (unseen, every condition visible):** a group models Rr x Rr but runs only four fertilisations; none gives rr; they conclude rr offspring cannot occur. Demand: evaluate. Answer: not supported; rr expected about one in four; four trials can easily give none by chance (about one time in three, (3/4)^4 = 0.32); repeat many times and pool. Crossed mistake: "the model is wrong" (it is the sample size, not the model).
- **Mistake scene:** none separate. Misconceptions blocked: a copied chromosome counts as two (4); crossing over can happen at any unmatched points or creates new alleles (5, 11); one meiosis shows every gamete type (7); the prediction must come out exactly (10, attempt); a model is useless because it is simplified (11 to 13).
- **Reuse:** B5b's lesson-local `AlleleBead` (white with ink outline and letter), `PairUp`, `CrossOver`, `TwoWays` (the coin flip, coded in B5b, reused here as the coin), `CombosGrid`; B8's amber-only-for-new-allele rule and `GameteList`; B5a's `ModelCell` counting chips; the real-structure comparison plates `b5x-chromosome-duplicated-pink-v1.png`, `b5x-chromosome-duplicated-teal-v1.png` and `b5x-cell-dividing-nospindle-v1.png` (scenes 4 and 11, small, "the real thing"). The explainer kit (`Plate`, `Callout`, `Mark`, `Countdown`, `HandText`, `DrawPath`, `scribbleEllipse`, `Enter`), B6a's `WrongChip`, `GreyTag`, `Cross`, `Cam`. Bead chains are built in code from the painted single pop-bead plates (Codex items, shared with bio-m5-p01). New coded items: the method step rail, the colour key chip, the gamete record table, the tally table and a small expected-against-observed column chart (counts, labelled axes), the assessment card, the attempt card.
- **No human figures:** chromosomes are bead chains on a bench; cells are loops of string; gamete cards are face-down index cards that shuffle themselves; the coin flips on its own. No hands.
- **Safety:** none spoken (beads and string). Not a hazard for Year 12.
- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Plum and teal follow B5b's colours ("from mother" and "from father" of the parent). (b) Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids at matching points (synapsis); the model swaps the end segments distal to the break. Number and position of crossovers vary; at least one per homologous pair is usual in humans (obligate crossover). Spoken only as "its position varies, and there can be several". (c) Pop-bead kits usually include a magnetic or plastic centromere piece that joins two chains; plain beads with a paper clip also work. (d) The 1 : 2 : 1 expectation for Rr x Rr assumes equal gamete frequencies, random fertilisation and equal survival. (e) 3, 9, 4 against 4, 8, 4: chi-squared = 0.25 + 0.125 + 0 = 0.375, well within chance (not spoken). (f) (3/4)^4 = 81/256 = 0.316, "about one time in three". (g) 2^23 = 8,388,608 ("over eight million", site L08). (h) Mutation: in the model one sticker is changed deliberately; real mutations arise from copying errors or mutagens, are rare per gene per generation, and most are neutral or harmful; only those in the germ line are inherited (B8). Spoken as "rare and random, and many have no effect". (i) The model's coin assumes the two chromosome pairs assort independently, which is true for genes on different chromosomes (the short-pair gene against the long-pair genes); the two long-pair genes are linked, which is why only crossing over separates them (B8). (j) Die rule (Revision 2): a crossover between the two long-pair genes in one run in six gives two recombinant chromatids of four in those runs, so recombinant gametes are about 1/6 x 1/2 = 1/12 of all gametes (each of the four mixed types about 1 in 48), against about 11/12 for the four unmixed types (each about 23 percent). A crossover in every run would make recombinants 50 percent, contradicting "less common" (bp02 reviews). (k) Pronunciation: "homologous" huh-MOL-uh-gus, "zygote" ZY-goat, "chromatid" KROH-muh-tid.

Main question: *How do you model meiosis, fertilisation and mutation to predict the genotypes offspring can have, and how far can you trust the predictions?*
Entry knowledge: B5a, B5b and B8 as above.
Stops after: building a parent cell with colour-tracked homologues and alleles at matching positions; copying each chromosome into sister chromatids and counting by centromeres; modelling crossing over at matching points and recording the new combinations; randomising each pair with a coin and dividing twice into four gametes; listing the four gamete types without crossing over (2 x 2) and the extra crossover types; modelling random fertilisation by random draw; adding a deliberate mutation and tracing the new allele into an offspring; tallying offspring for one gene and comparing observed with expected; assessing the model (strengths, limitations, validity, reliability); evaluating a conclusion drawn from too few trials. No Punnett square method (B9), no dihybrid ratios, no linkage maps.
Handoff: Punnett squares and pedigrees (route entry bio-m5-b09).

### 1. hook (one pair of parents, many offspring)
> Two parent plants can produce a hundred seeds, and no two are quite the same. Can you predict which genotypes their offspring could have? You can, with a few pop-beads on a desk. In this practical you'll model **meiosis** with crossing over, then **fertilisation** and a **mutation**, and use the model to predict the variety in the offspring, and to see where those predictions can go wrong.

Visual: the bench strip draws across. A loose heap of painted pop-beads (plum and teal single-bead plates, Codex items 1 and 2, scattered by code) sits at the left; at the right a row of eight coded seed outlines appears, each with a different short letter label (screen only). ### 2. aim and kit
> The aim: model meiosis, fertilisation and mutation to predict the variation in offspring genotypes. You need pop-beads in two colours, centromere pieces, small stickers, a coin, a **die**, string loops for cells, blank cards and a results sheet. **Plum** beads stand for chromosomes the parent got from its mother, and **teal** for those from its father. The colours let you track where every piece came from.

### 3. step 1: build the parent cell
> Step one: build a parent cell with two pairs of chromosomes. Make a long plum chain and a long teal chain the same length, then a short pair the same way. Stick alleles at **matching positions**: purple and tall on the long plum chromosome, white and short on the long teal one, round on the short plum one, and wrinkled on the short teal one. Matching positions matter, because homologous chromosomes carry **the same genes in the same places**.

Visual: step rail lights "1. Build the parent cell". A string loop opens on the bench. Bead chains assemble bead by bead (painted single-bead plates chained in code): long plum, long teal, short plum, short teal. On "matching positions" sticker dots (white, ink outline) land on each chain with letters on screen only: P and T on long plum (P near the top, T near the bottom), p and t on long teal at the same heights, R on short plum, r on short teal. Tag under the loop: "2n = 4". Hold about 1 second.

### 4. step 2: copy each chromosome
> Step two: the DNA is copied before meiosis starts. So build an identical twin of each chain, same colour, same stickers, and join the pair with a **centromere** piece. Each chromosome is now two sister chromatids. Count them: there are still **four** chromosomes, because you count centromeres, not chromatids.

Visual: step rail "2. Copy". Each chain duplicates beside itself (same colours, same stickers) and a painted centromere piece (item 4) clips the two together, forming an X-like pair of bead chains. On "sister chromatids" the real plates `b5x-chromosome-duplicated-pink-v1.png` and `-teal-v1.png` fade in small at the right as "the real thing". On "four" B5a's counting chip counts centromeres: 1, 2, 3, 4 (each centromere piece rings in turn); a crossed chip "8 chromosomes" (grey tag "count centromeres"). Hold about 1 second.

### 5. step 3: crossing over
> Step three, in **prophase one**: crossing over. Pair each long chromosome with its partner, and roll a **die**: cross over only on a six. Unclip one plum chromatid and one teal chromatid at the same point, between the two genes, and **swap the end pieces**. Break at exactly **matching points**, because a real crossover exchanges the same stretch of genes, so nothing is lost or doubled. Now one chromatid carries **purple with short**, and another carries **white with tall**. Record those new combinations.

Visual: step rail "3. Cross over (prophase I)". The long pair slides together side by side (B5b's `PairUp`). On "die" the painted die (`m5x-die-v1.png`) tumbles on the bench and settles; code draws six pips on its blank top face (tag "six: cross over"; a small rule chip "1 to 5: no swap" sits beside it). On "Unclip" one inner plum chromatid and one inner teal chromatid separate at the bead between P and T (a small gap opens at the same bead count on both, tag "same point"). On "swap the end pieces" the two end segments exchange places (B5b's `CrossOver` motion), keeping their own colours (B5b rule: swapped pieces keep their parent's colour). On "matching points" a level line confirms the gap is at the same position on both; a small inset shows an unmatched swap with one chromatid gaining an extra bead and the other losing one, grey cross, tag "unmatched: genes lost or doubled". On "purple with short" the plum-top, teal-bottom chromatid rings (P with t); on "white with tall" the teal-top, plum-bottom chromatid rings (p with T). The gamete record table opens in the right zone with the line "new combinations: P t, p T". Hold about 1 second.

### 6. step 4: line up and divide twice
> Step four, in **metaphase one**: line the pairs up across the middle of the cell. Flip the **coin** for each pair to decide which side the plum chromosome goes. The coin makes each pair's direction **random**, and independent of the other pair. Pull each pair apart into two new loops. Then, for meiosis two, pull the **sister chromatids** apart in each loop. You end up with **four gametes**, each with one long and one short chromosome.

Visual: step rail "4. Line up (metaphase I), divide twice (meiosis I, II)". A coded dashed equator draws across the loop; both pairs slide onto it. On "coin" B5b's `TwoWays` coin flips over the long pair (heads: plum left) and again over the short pair (tails: plum right), each result pinning a small tag. On "random" the second flip's tag reads "decided separately". On "apart into two new loops" the homologous partners separate to opposite sides and two new string loops close around them (tag "partners separate; centromeres stay joined", grey tag "B5b"). On "sister chromatids" each loop splits again, centromere pieces unclipping, into four small loops. On "four gametes" each small loop gets a "n = 2" tag and its alleles print beside it. Hold about 1 second.

### 7. step 5: record the gametes, and run it again
> Step five: record each gamete's alleles on a card, then rebuild and run it again with different coin results. Without crossing over, you'll only ever find **four types**: purple and tall, or white and short, each with round or with wrinkled. That's two choices for each of two pairs: two times two. Crossing over adds the **mixed types**. They're less common because, like your die, a crossover between those two genes happens in only some cells.

Visual: step rail "5. Record and repeat". Each gamete's letters write onto a blank card plate (item 6) which slides into the record table. The bench resets with a quick rewind; the die rolls a three (code draws three pips; tag "no swap this run") and the coins flip differently; new, unmixed gametes drop in. The table sorts into two blocks: "no crossover: P T R, P T r, p t R, p t r" with "2 x 2 = 4 = 2^n" (grey tag "site: confirm 2^n"), and "with a crossover: P t R, P t r, p T R, p T r" with a smaller font and the tag "less common: only on a six". On "like your die" the die rolls once more beside the table. Grey tag "B8". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 8. step 6: fertilisation by random draw
> Step six: fertilisation. A partner group builds a second parent the same way and keeps every gamete card. Each group shuffles its cards face down and draws **one at random**. Put the two gametes together in one loop. That's the **zygote**, back to two of each chromosome. Record its genotype, return the cards, and repeat. The random draw models random fertilisation: any gamete can meet any gamete.

Visual: step rail "6. Fertilise". Two card stacks sit at the left and right of the bench, labelled "parent 1 gametes" and "parent 2 gametes". On "shuffles" each stack shuffles itself (cards riffle in code). On "one at random" one card flips from each stack (P T r and p t R). On "together in one loop" their bead chromosomes join in one string loop; tag "zygote: 2n = 4". On "Record its genotype" the line "Pp Tt Rr" writes on a tally sheet; on "return the cards" both cards slide back into their stacks and reshuffle. Hold about 1 second.

### 9. step 7: add a mutation
> Step seven: mutation. Before one fertilisation, swap one sticker in one gamete for a **new allele** in a new colour. That models a DNA change in a cell that makes gametes. The offspring from that gamete carries an allele that **neither parent had**. Crossing over, assortment and fertilisation only reshuffle alleles. Only mutation makes a **new one**.

Visual: step rail "7. Mutate". One gamete card is drawn; its R sticker peels off and an amber sticker lands in the same place, labelled "R*" (tag "new allele", B8 rule: amber only for a new allele). On "DNA change" a small grey tag: "in a cell that makes eggs or sperm (B8)". On "neither parent had" the zygote forms with the amber sticker and both parents' cards show no amber (tags "parent 1: no R*", "parent 2: no R*"). On "reshuffle" the earlier white stickers pulse; on "new one" the amber sticker rings. Hold about 1 second.

### 10. results: expected against observed
> Here's an example class result for just the seed-shape gene, from sixteen fertilisations. Each parent gives round or wrinkled equally often, so four pairings are **equally likely**: round with round, round with wrinkled, wrinkled with round, and wrinkled with wrinkled. Two of the four give one of each. So out of sixteen, the model **expects** four, eight and four. The class **got** three, nine and four. Close, but not exact, because each draw is random.

### 11. assess: what the model represents well
> Now assess the model. It represents several things well. Matching sticker positions show that homologues carry the same genes. The swap shows crossing over making **new combinations** without new alleles. The coin makes each pair's direction random, and joining two gametes shows fertilisation **restoring** the chromosome number. And it **predicts** which gamete types are possible.

Visual: the assessment card writes in the right zone with three headings: "Represents well", "Leaves out or distorts", "Keep it valid and reliable". Rows in the first column, one per cue, each pen-linked to the bench: "same genes at matching places"; "crossing over: new combinations, no new alleles"; "random direction for each pair (coin)"; "2n to n to 2n" (counter chips "4, 2, 4"); "predicts possible gametes". The real cell plate `b5x-cell-dividing-nospindle-v1.png` sits small beside the string loop (tag "real cell"). Hold about 1 second.

### 12. assess: limitations
> Its limits matter too. Two pairs give only four whole-chromosome combinations, so the model hides most of the variety: humans' twenty-three pairs give **over eight million**. You chose where the crossover went, but in real cells its position varies, and there can be several. You chose the mutation, but real mutations are **rare and random**, and many have no effect. And beads with stickers show alleles, but not the DNA itself, or the spindle that moves the chromosomes.

Visual: the second column fills, one row per cue: "2 pairs: 4 combinations, so most variety is hidden; humans 23 pairs: 2^23, over 8 million" (a number ticker runs from 4 to 8,388,608); "crossover chosen: real position varies, often more than one" (the chain shows two faint possible break points); "mutation chosen: real ones rare, random, often no effect"; "no DNA, no spindle" (a faint spindle outline over the loop with a grey "not modelled" tag). Hold about 2 seconds (reading hold).

### 13. valid and reliable
> To keep it **valid**, so it really tests what meiosis does, follow the real rules every time: break at matching points, roll and flip fresh each run, and draw at random. To make it **reliable**, so repeats agree, repeat many times and pool the class results, because a few trials can land far from the prediction just by chance.

Visual: the third column fills: "valid (tests what meiosis really does): matching breaks, fresh die and coin each run, random draw" (each pen-linked to the bench step); "reliable (repeats agree): many repeats, pool the class" (the tally table from scene 10 returns small; a pooled class row writes on beneath it with larger totals: "class pooled: 41, 79, 40 / expected 40, 80, 40", example data). A crossed chip: "one run is enough". Hold about 1 second.

### 14. attempt (countdown, 10 seconds)
> Your turn. A group models two parents that each have one round and one wrinkled allele, but runs only **four** fertilisations. None of the four offspring has two wrinkled alleles. They conclude that offspring with two wrinkled alleles can't occur from these parents. Evaluate their conclusion. Pause here to answer.

Visual: the board clears: no tally, no assessment card. The attempt card writes on: two parent chips "R r" and "R r"; a small results row of four offspring cards "RR, Rr, Rr, Rr"; the conclusion in a quote box "rr offspring can't occur from these parents". Key line: "R = round allele, r = wrinkled allele" (screen only). Givens line: "4 fertilisations". Demand: "Evaluate their conclusion." One answer box. Nothing answer-bearing: no expected values, no fractions. **The countdown ring starts after "Pause here to answer." and runs for 10 seconds.** The card stays on screen during and after the countdown.

### 15. feedback (brief)
> The conclusion isn't supported. Each parent can give a wrinkled allele, so two wrinkled alleles are expected about **one time in four**. Each offspring has a three in four chance of missing out, so all four miss out about **one time in three**. They should **repeat** many more times and pool the results. If you said the model is wrong, it isn't: the sample is just too small.

Visual: the card shrinks to a strip. On "one time in four" gamete chips "r 1/2" from each parent multiply to "rr 1/4". On "three in four" a chip "not rr: 3/4" appears under each of the four offspring cards; on "one time in three" the working line writes on: "chance of no rr in 4 = 3/4 x 3/4 x 3/4 x 3/4 = (3/4)^4, about 0.32". On "repeat" the pooled row from scene 13 flashes in small with its rr count. Answer line: "not supported: rr expected 1/4; 4 trials is too few; repeat and pool". On "the model is wrong" the chip "the model is wrong" writes on and takes a grey scribble cross, tag "too few trials, not a bad model". Hold about 1 second.

### 16. decision rule and handoff
> So, to model offspring variation: track each parent's chromosomes by colour, copy before dividing, cross over on a six at matching points, randomise every pair and every fertilisation, add a mutation on purpose, and repeat enough times to trust the pattern. **Next**: Punnett squares and pedigrees, the quick way to predict crosses like these on paper.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Model: two colours track maternal and paternal chromosomes; alleles at matching positions on homologues. Copy each chromosome (two sister chromatids, one centromere): count centromeres.
2. Crossing over: swap matching segments between non-sister chromatids; new combinations of existing alleles. Roll a die: cross over only on a six (prophase I), so mixed gametes stay less common. Independent assortment: a coin for each pair (metaphase I). Two divisions give four haploid gametes. Without crossing over: 2^n types (n = 2 gives 4).
3. Fertilisation: random draw of one gamete from each parent; zygote restores 2n. Mutation: replace one allele in a gamete with a new one; only mutation makes new alleles.
4. Example Rr x Rr: four equally likely pairings (RR, Rr, rR, rr), so expected 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr; observed varies by chance. Valid (tests what meiosis really does): follow the real rules. Reliable (repeats agree): many repeats, pool class results.
5. Limits: only 2 pairs (humans 23: over 8 million combinations); crossover position and mutation chosen, not random; no DNA or spindle shown.

Memory aid (on the notes screen): *Colour, copy, cross, coin, combine, change.* Condition tag: "repeat and pool before you trust a pattern".

## Teaching checks
