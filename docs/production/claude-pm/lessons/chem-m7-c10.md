**Boundary:** this video starts by naming the acid and alcohol parts of an ester, and stops at explaining esterification, its equilibrium and how the product is identified. It teaches: a carboxylic acid and an alcohol react to form an ester and water (esterification); the acid loses the OH of its carboxyl group and the alcohol loses the H of its O–H, these form water, and the two parts link through the alcohol's oxygen; the ester group –COO– (C=O then O then carbon); a reaction in which two molecules join and release a small molecule such as water is a condensation reaction; naming: the alcohol part first as an alkyl group ("-yl"), the acid part second ending "-oate" (as in c09's salts), ethyl ethanoate; condensed formulas are usually written acid side first while the name puts the alcohol part first; order matters (ethyl butanoate and butyl ethanoate are different compounds with the same formula); working backwards from a condensed formula: find the C=O and cut at the single-bonded oxygen next to it; the side with the C=O came from the acid (methyl propanoate from propanoic acid and methanol); properties: no O–H, so no hydrogen bonding between ester molecules, only dipole–dipole and dispersion forces, so for similar molar mass an ester boils well below an alcohol and a carboxylic acid (ethyl ethanoate 77 °C, pentan-1-ol 138 °C, butanoic acid 164 °C, all about 88 g/mol); small esters are volatile, which is why they smell; esters are functional group isomers of carboxylic acids, and only the acid fizzes with sodium hydrogen carbonate (c09); conditions: a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst, heat under reflux (c06 recall: the condenser returns volatile ethanol and ester); hazards: concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive, the organic liquids are flammable, so heat electrically or with a water bath, not a flame; equilibrium: the reaction is reversible (water can split the ester back), so all four substances remain; from equal amounts of ethanoic acid and ethanol about two thirds becomes ester; Le Chatelier (Module 5): an excess of one reactant shifts the position right, using up some of it (with three times as much ethanol about nine tenths of the acid becomes ester); removing water also shifts it right, and concentrated sulfuric acid takes up a little water; the catalyst speeds both directions and does not change the yield; reflux keeps material in the flask and does not change the yield; isolating and checking the product: separating funnel with water (most of the ethanol and sulfuric acid dissolve in the water; the ester forms its own layer), wash with sodium carbonate solution (leftover acid becomes a salt that dissolves in the water layer, and CO₂ forms, so vent the funnel), separate, dry, distil, collecting near the ester's boiling point, which is only a rough check because ethanol boils at 78 °C; no fizz with carbonate shows the acid has gone; a two-step flow chart: ethanol oxidised to ethanoic acid (acidified dichromate, reflux), then ethanoic acid with more ethanol (concentrated sulfuric acid, reflux) to ethyl ethanoate, each arrow carrying reagents and conditions; the "main road" alkene, primary alcohol, acid, ester (only a primary alcohol oxidises to an acid). **Not here:** the practical procedure in full, quantities, the risk table and waste disposal (chem-m7-p05, with `C7-IQ2-05`); saponification, fats, oils and triglycerides (chem-m7-c12 territory; author note g); amides (chem-m7-c11); polyesters (chem-m7-c15); equilibrium constant calculations (author note d keeps the arithmetic); the site's avocado fat hook (site correction 3); reaction mechanism beyond which atoms form the water (author note b).

### 2. joining the halves
> Take ethanoic acid and ethanol. The acid loses the O H from its carboxyl group, and the alcohol loses the H from its O H. Together, those make **water**. When two molecules join and release a small molecule like water, it's called a **condensation** reaction.

### 8. pushing the yield up

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Esterification (a condensation reaction): carboxylic acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water, with conc. H₂SO₄ (catalyst), heated under reflux. The acid loses OH, the alcohol loses H. Ester group –COO–. Example: CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O (ethyl ethanoate).
2. Names: alcohol part first ("-yl"), acid part second ("-oate"). Working back: cut at the single-bonded O next to the C=O; the C=O side is the acid (CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ = methyl propanoate, from propanoic acid + methanol).
3. Esters have no O–H, so no hydrogen bonds between their molecules (dipole–dipole + dispersion): similar mass, ester < alcohol < carboxylic acid (77, 138, 164 °C at 88 g/mol). Small esters are volatile and often fruity-smelling. Esters are isomers of acids; only the acid fizzes with NaHCO₃.
4. Reversible: about 2/3 ester from equal amounts. More yield: excess reactant (it is partly used up), remove water. Catalyst and heat: faster, not more. Reflux: nothing escapes.
5. Get it out: separating funnel with water (most ethanol and H₂SO₄ dissolve in it), wash with Na₂CO₃(aq) (leftover acid becomes a salt in the water layer; vent the CO₂), separate, dry, distil. The boiling point is a rough check (ethanol boils at 78 °C).
6. Flow chart: ethanol → (K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺, reflux) ethanoic acid → (+ ethanol, conc. H₂SO₄, reflux) ethyl ethanoate. Main road: alkene → primary alcohol → acid → ester.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Alcohol gets "-yl" (first), acid gets "-oate" (second).* Second line: *Catalyst and heat: faster, not more. Reflux: nothing escapes.*

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Where the water comes from: the acid's OH and the alcohol's H (2). Why the alcohol part is named first and how to find the acid side: the C=O stays with the acid (3, 4). Why esters boil low and smell: no O–H, so no hydrogen bonds between them, so they evaporate easily (5). Why the catalyst and heat: the reaction is slow at room temperature (6). Why reflux: the vapours would escape; with reflux, nothing escapes (6, 11). Why the yield stops short: the reaction is reversible and reaches equilibrium (7). Why excess reactant or removing water helps: the system shifts to use up some of the added substance or replace some of the removed one (8). Why the catalyst doesn't help the yield: it speeds both directions (8). Why each separation step: most of the ethanol and sulfuric acid dissolve in the water, but the ester doesn't mix well; carbonate turns the leftover acid into a salt that dissolves in the water layer, making CO₂, so the funnel is vented; distillation collects what boils near the ester's boiling point, a rough check only, because ethanol boils at 78 °C (9). Why the main road needs a primary alcohol: only a primary alcohol oxidises to an acid (10). Why the name order trips students: formulas are written acid side first, names alcohol part first (3).
