### 2. the shape of the spending
> Start with the shape of the spending, as trends, not figures. **Health expenditure** is all spending on health, by governments, insurers and individuals. It's grown faster than the economy for decades, and that's true in almost every wealthy country, so the cause is general, not one bad policy. Governments pay most of it. Hospitals and primary care take the biggest shares, and prevention gets a few cents in the dollar. And individuals pay a real share themselves, as **out-of-pocket costs**, mostly for dental care, some allied health and psychology, and specialist gap fees. Put the last two together. The services with the biggest long-run return get the smallest share, and people pay most where cost most directly stops them getting care.

### 3. what treatment and prevention each buy
> So, treatment or prevention? The case for prevention isn't that treatment is wasteful. They buy different things. **Prevention** comes in three kinds: stopping a condition starting, finding it early by screening, and limiting harm once it exists. Structural prevention, like tobacco laws, road design or immunisation, changes risk for a whole population at once. Treatment acts on one person at a time, and it works. It's what you need when prevention didn't happen, and the person is right in front of you. And the timescales differ. Treatment shows a result now. Prevention shows it in ten or twenty years, as an illness that never appears.

### 4. why the balance doesn't shift
> So why doesn't the balance shift? Five reasons. Prevented illness is invisible. Nobody knows they're the person who didn't have the heart attack, so nobody campaigns for it. Costs land in this budget, and benefits three governments later. Urgency wins, because a waiting list is visible and someone gets blamed. Payment rewards activity, so the system is paid for treating, not for illness avoided. And prevention's levers, like housing, transport and planning, sit outside the health budget. Two honest limits. Prevention isn't always cheaper, though it can still be worth buying for the healthy years. And prevention that relies on people signing up reaches the advantaged first, which is why structural prevention is usually fairer.

### 5. sustainability: what drives the growth
> Now, **sustainability** means a pattern of spending can keep going without being cut, rationed or pushed onto individuals. So "health spending is unsustainable" is a claim, and you test it by asking what drives the growth. The biggest driver is usually new technology and medicines, which add treatments rather than replace them. Add earlier diagnosis, chronic conditions that cost for decades, and rising wages. Ageing is real, but smaller than most people assume. So some growth is a sign of success. The real question is how to get more health from each dollar, and who pays for the growth. Every response moves the burden. Improve value, which is best but slowest. Raise revenue. Shift costs to individuals. Or ration through waiting lists.

### 6. one decision, not three
> That's why the syllabus groups sustainability, access and equity. They're one decision, seen at different times. A government saves money by letting a subsidy fall behind the cost of care. Providers charge a gap. The service still exists, but for some people affordability now fails, and that's the moment it stops being about money. They delay care, and the delay piles up in the groups who already have the worst health. Conditions arrive later and worse, often in emergency departments, where care costs more. So the saving comes back as a cost somewhere else, and the health gap widens. Dental care is the clearest Australian example, largely outside universal coverage, with the most cost-related delay of any common service.

### 7. current and future populations
> Last, current and future populations. For people today, spending decides what's subsidised, what they pay, how long they wait, and who's protected from hardship. For people tomorrow, today's prevention sets their disease burden, and today's workforce and building decisions last for decades. Cost deferred isn't cost avoided. That's **opportunity cost**, the value of what you give up by choosing something else. Twenty more surgery places now, or a falls program for older people? The surgery patients are visible and waiting. The people who won't fall can't lobby, because they don't know who they are. So the balance isn't a mistake. It's a built-in bias. A defensible position: today's level of spending is justified, but its mix isn't.

### 9d. feedback, item two
> It saves on paper, but it shifts cost. As fees rise, a gap opens, affordability fails, and people on lower incomes stop attending first. ## Memory aid check

- **"Cost deferred is not cost avoided."** Assumption: the deferred condition will still need care later. Not every deferred condition worsens; some resolve. True on average for chronic and progressive conditions (dental disease, diabetes, mental illness), which are the cases the video uses.
- **"Only one side has a constituency."** Assumption: prevention's beneficiaries cannot be identified. Some prevention has visible advocates (cancer charities, public health bodies); the aid concerns the beneficiaries, not advocates. Spoken with its gloss "no one knows they benefit".
