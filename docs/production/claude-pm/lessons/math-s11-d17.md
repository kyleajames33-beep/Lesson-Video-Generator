## Decisions

- **Anchor: the investigation's two groups as boxes (d16's frame).** d14 compared them with means and σ; here the same comparison is made from box plots, which is how exam questions usually present two groups, and the picture adds the overlap and the shape.
- **One scale first (visual surprise).** Drawn on separate scales, the two box plots can look the same size; snapped onto one scale, the elsewhere box sits clearly to the right. Parallel box plots only work on a shared scale.
- **Centre: medians, then the overlap.** The elsewhere median is 8 h against 6.75 h, 1.25 h higher. The boxes meet at 7.5: the bedroom group's Q3 is the elsewhere group's Q1. So about three quarters of the bedroom group slept 7.5 h or less, while about three quarters of the elsewhere group slept 7.5 h or more (in fact 10 of 12 and 7 of 8). Plain meaning: overlap ("where two boxes cover the same values").
- **Spread: IQR and range can disagree (recalled from d15).** IQRs are close (1.5 h against 1.25 h): the middle halves are similarly spread. The ranges are not (5.5 h against 2.5 h): the bedroom group's long left whisker, including the 3 h night, stretches its range. So "the bedroom group is more spread out" is true of the range and only slightly true of the IQR; say which measure.
- **Shape: whiskers and median positions (d16's rule).** Bedroom: a long left whisker (3 h against 1 h): negatively skewed. Elsewhere: short, similar whiskers (0.5 and 0.75 h) and the median a little nearer Q1: roughly symmetric.
- **The written comparison.** Centre, spread and shape, each with numbers and a direction, then the context and the observational caution (d14's sentence pattern). Site lesson13 card 09's checklist (centre, spread, skewness, outliers) is used; outliers are named as "next".
- **Plain meanings at first use.** Parallel box plots ("two or more box plots drawn on the same scale, one above the other"; also called side-by-side box plots); overlap (above).

### 1. hook: two boxes, one scale
> Here are our two groups as box plots, one above the other. Phone in the bedroom, and phone elsewhere. We compared them before with the mean and standard deviation. Box plots show the same comparison as a picture, and they add something the numbers alone don't. How much the groups overlap, and what shape each one has. Box plots drawn like this are called parallel box plots. So how do parallel box plots compare two **groups**?

### 2. one scale, or nothing
> The first rule is the scale. Parallel box plots must share one number line. Watch what happens without it. Draw the bedroom group on a scale from two to ten, and the elsewhere group on a scale from six to ten. The boxes look about the same size, and sit in about the same place. Now put them on one scale. The elsewhere box jumps to the right, and the bedroom group's whisker stretches far out to the left. Same data, very different picture. If two box plots don't share a scale, you can't compare them. Exam questions draw them on one scale for you, so check the axis first, as **always**.

### 3. comparing centre
> Start with the centre, using the medians. The elsewhere group's median is eight hours, and the bedroom group's is six point seven five. So the elsewhere group typically slept one and a quarter hours **more**. That's a similar gap to the one the means showed, which makes it more convincing. Now look at the overlap, where the boxes cover the same values. The bedroom box ends at seven and a half, exactly where the elsewhere box begins. So about three quarters of the bedroom group slept seven and a half hours or less, while about three quarters of the elsewhere group slept seven and a half or more. The boxes barely overlap. When boxes overlap a lot, the groups are hard to tell apart. When they hardly overlap, the difference is clear. That's a big difference, and the picture makes it obvious at a **glance**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 2): the centre lens: both median lines extend into dashed violet verticals; a difference arrow grows between 6.75 and 8 with a readout "1.25 h" (hand tag "medians: centre"); on "more" (first bold) row "medians 8 h and 6.75 h: elsewhere 1.25 h higher" (framed green). ### 4. comparing spread
> Next, the spread. Here the two measures disagree, and that's worth noticing. The IQRs are close, one and a half hours for the bedroom group, and one and a quarter for elsewhere. So the middle halves are similarly spread. But the ranges are not close, five and a half hours against two and a half. The difference comes from the bedroom group's long left whisker, stretched out by that three hour night. So say which measure you mean. By range, the bedroom group is much more spread out. By IQR, only slightly. Careful, too. A box further to the right isn't more spread out. Position shows centre. Width shows **spread**.

Visual: the spread brackets: amber IQR brackets under each box ("1.5 h", "1.25 h"; hand tag "IQRs close") and amber range brackets above each plot ("5.5 h", "2.5 h"; hand tag "ranges differ"); row "IQR 1.5 h and 1.25 h (close); range 5.5 h and 2.5 h" (framed green). ### 5. comparing shape
> Then the shape. Look at the whiskers and where the median sits in the box. The bedroom group's left whisker is three hours long, and its right whisker just one. A long left whisker is a tail to the left, so the bedroom group is negatively skewed. The elsewhere group's whiskers are short and similar, half an hour and three quarters, and its median sits only a little off-centre. So it's roughly **symmetric**. One group has a tail of short nights. The other doesn't.

### 6. writing the comparison
> Now write it down, centre, spread and shape, each with numbers. The elsewhere group had a higher median, eight hours against six point seven five, and about three quarters of each group sat on opposite sides of seven and a half hours. The bedroom group was negatively skewed, and the elsewhere group roughly symmetric.
