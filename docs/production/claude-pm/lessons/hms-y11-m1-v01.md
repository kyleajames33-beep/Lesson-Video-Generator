### 2. contested, and the WHO definition
> Health is what's called a contested idea. That means sensible people disagree about where its edges are. The definition you'll meet most often comes from the World Health Organization. It says health is "a state of complete physical, mental and social wellbeing and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity." It's been part of the organisation's constitution since that came into force in 1948, and the wording hasn't changed since.

### 3. what "not merely" changed
> Two phrases do most of the work. "Not merely the absence of disease" makes health something you have, not just a gap where illness isn't. Older medical ideas worked the other way round, so anyone without a diagnosis counted as healthy by default. And "physical, mental and social" brings three dimensions inside health. So someone with no disease, but no sleep, no friends and nowhere secure to live, isn't healthy on this definition. That's why governments quote it. It lets them treat housing and loneliness as health problems.

### 5. two narrower meanings
> The WHO definition isn't the only meaning in use, and the others aren't just worse versions of it. Two are narrower. ### 6. one wider meaning

### 7. the trade
> Line the four meanings up from narrowest to widest, and a pattern appears. Every time you widen a definition, it becomes more relevant to real lives, but less precise. Every time you narrow it, it becomes more precise, but less relevant. The biomedical meaning can be settled with a lab test and tells you little about a life. The holistic meaning describes a life well, but it's very hard to turn into a number.

### 8. why people give different meanings
> So why do people choose different meanings? It isn't random. People tend to define health by what health has cost or given them. A sixteen-year-old often means energy and fitness, while an eighty-year-old means getting to the shops on their own. Living with a condition moves people from "nothing wrong" to "managing it well". Culture sets whether health belongs to one body or to a family and community. A labourer needs a different body from an accountant. And people with fewer resources often set the bar where they can actually reach it. None of these is wrong. Each meaning is **situated**: built by someone, somewhere, for a purpose.

### 9. comparing two meanings
> When a question says compare, don't describe one meaning and then the other, and don't just pick a winner. Put them side by side on the same three questions. What does each one count? What does it leave out? And whose work does it make possible? Try it on our athlete. Biomedical counts the diagnosis, so it calls them unwell. Functional counts what they can do, so it calls them healthy. Biomedical leaves out how their life is going, while functional has no fixed line. One serves the doctor, the other serves the person. Neither is wrong. They're built for different jobs.

   Model: The doctor is using the biomedical meaning, health as the absence of diagnosable disease or injury (1 mark).    Model (the site's full-mark structure, shortened): shared ground, both treat the absence of disease as relevant and the WHO names it in "not merely the absence of disease or infirmity", so the biomedical meaning is close to a subset of the WHO one (1 mark); counts, the biomedical meaning counts the physical state of the body alone, the WHO adds mental and social wellbeing, so isolation or insecure housing register under the WHO only (1 mark); leaves out, the biomedical meaning omits whether a life is going well, the WHO omits attainability because "complete" excludes most people and those managing a lifelong condition (1 mark); useful for, the biomedical meaning settles clinical decisions, the WHO justifies policy on social conditions (1 mark); judgement against purpose, neither is simply better (1 mark).
