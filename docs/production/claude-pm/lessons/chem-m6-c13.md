### 3. a reversible switch

1. An indicator is a weak acid, HIn, whose two forms have different colours: HIn(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + In⁻(aq). Added acid shifts it left; added base shifts it right. The change is reversible.
2. The colour changes over a transition range of roughly 2 pH units around the indicator's pKa. Methyl orange 3.1–4.4 (red to yellow); methyl red 4.4–6.2 (red to yellow); bromothymol blue 6.0–7.6 (yellow to blue); phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0 (colourless to pink).
3. The salt at equivalence takes after its stronger parent. Weak acid + strong base: basic (CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq)). Strong acid + weak base: acidic (NH₄⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₃(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)). Strong + strong: neutral (pH 7.00 at 25 °C).
4. Endpoint: the colour change. Equivalence point: the amounts match the equation. Choose an indicator whose colour change falls inside the steep pH jump around equivalence.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *The salt takes after its stronger parent.* Second line: *Pick the indicator that changes inside the jump.* Condition tag: "one parent strong, one weak; two strong parents: neutral".

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why an indicator changes colour: its two forms differ in colour, and the pH sets which dominates (2). Why the change reverses: added acid or base shifts the equilibrium, and the indicator is not consumed (3). Why the change spans a range: the ratio follows [H₃O⁺], and the eye needs about a tenfold excess (4). Why the equivalence pH matters: the endpoint should land at equivalence (5). Why ethanoate makes the flask basic: a weak acid's conjugate base takes protons from water, making hydroxide (6). Why NaCl is neutral and NH₄Cl acidic: chloride and sodium do not react with water, ammonium gives protons to it (7). Why the volume doubles the dilution: the base added has its own volume (8). Why the pH leaps near equivalence: one drop takes the flask from the last of the buffer to excess hydroxide (10). Why methyl orange misleads: it changes in the buffer region, before most of the acid has reacted (10). Why all three work for strong with strong: the jump is long at these concentrations (11).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** an indicator is a passive dye (2); the indicator is used up (3, feedback 1, crossed); the colour changes at one exact pH (4); every equivalence point is pH 7 (5 to 7, 9); the range must contain the equivalence pH (11, the rule said positively); a vivid colour change is a good choice (10); phenolphthalein for every titration (feedback 2, crossed).
