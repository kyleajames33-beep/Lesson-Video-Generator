### 1. hook: edging or mulch?
> Here's a backyard. The lawn is twelve metres by eight, and in one corner there's a triangular garden bed. You need two things from the hardware store. Edging, to run around the outside of the lawn, and mulch, to cover the garden bed. They sound similar, but they're completely different questions. Edging is a length, measured in metres. Mulch covers a space, measured in square **metres**. Buy the wrong amount of either, and it's another trip to the shop. So how do we find the perimeter and area of rectangles, triangles, parallelograms and trapeziums, and of shapes made from **them**?

### 2. perimeter and area of a rectangle
> First, the two words. Perimeter is the distance around the outside edge. Area is the space covered inside, counted in squares. For the lawn on its own, the perimeter is twelve plus eight plus twelve plus eight, or two lots of twelve and two lots of eight, forty **metres**. For the area, imagine one metre squares across the lawn. There are twelve in each row and eight rows, so twelve times eight, ninety-six square **metres**. Same lawn, two answers, and different units. The forty is metres of edging. The ninety-six is square metres of **grass**.

Visual: stage change: the lawn plan full size (1,000 × 670 px). Grey chips "perimeter: the distance around the outside edge" and "area: the space covered inside, in square units". On "twelve plus eight" the edge tracer runs around (simulation 3) with a counter "12, 20, 32, 40", hand tag "around the outside"; on "metres" (first) row "P = 2 × 12 + 2 × 8 = 40 m" (framed green). On "one metre squares" a 12 by 8 grid draws (teal), a row counting to 12, then rows to 8; hand tag "covers the inside"; on "metres" (second) row "A = 12 × 8 = 96 m²" (framed green), grey chip "a02: m² = square metres". On "grass" the "m" and "m²" units ring in two colours.

### 3. the parallelogram: slide it across
> Not everything is a rectangle. An angled parking bay is a parallelogram, a four-sided shape with two pairs of parallel sides. This one has a base of five point five metres, and its slanted sides are two point six metres long. But the height we need is the perpendicular height, measured at right angles to the base, and that's two point four metres. Here's why. Slide the triangle off one end across to the other end, and the parallelogram becomes a rectangle, five point five by two point **four**. So its area is base times perpendicular height, thirteen point two square **metres**. Using the slanted side gives fourteen point three, which is too **big**. Watch the bay lean further over. The slanted side gets longer, but the area doesn't change at **all**. The slanted side is still useful though. The painted line around the bay is two lots of five point five plus two point six, sixteen point two **metres**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): a coded parallelogram bay (900 × 380 px, teal fill) with "5.5 m" base and "2.6 m" slanted sides (blue); grey chip "parallelogram: two pairs of parallel sides". On "perpendicular height" a dashed amber line drops at right angles with a right-angle mark, labelled "2.4 m", hand tag "perpendicular: at right angles". ### 4. the triangle: half a parallelogram
> Now the triangle. Take any triangle and make an exact copy. Turn the copy round and fit it against the first, and together they make a parallelogram, with the same base and the same perpendicular height. So one triangle is half of it. Area equals a half, times base, times **height**. The garden bed is a right-angled triangle, with sides of three metres and four metres meeting at the corner, and a sloping edge of five metres. The two sides that meet at the right angle are a base and a height already. Half of three times four is six square metres of **mulch**. And if you wanted edging around the bed itself, add all three sides, three plus four plus five, twelve **metres**. A very common slip is forgetting the half. That doubles the mulch you **buy**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): a coded scalene triangle (teal, base 600 px) with "base" and a dashed perpendicular "height" (amber). On "exact copy" a second triangle appears, rotates 180° and slides against the first, forming a parallelogram outline; hand tag "half the parallelogram". On "height" row "area = ½ × base × height" (52 px), grey chip "area = ½ × base × height" (kept for Year 12 t09). On "garden bed" the bed plate (400 px) returns at the left with its coded triangle overlay: "3 m", "4 m" with a right-angle mark, "5 m" on the sloping edge (blue). On "mulch" row "A = ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 m²" (framed green), the bed fills teal. On "twelve metres" the edge tracer runs round the bed; row "P = 3 + 4 + 5 = 12 m" (framed green). On "buy" a grey ghost "3 × 4 = 12 m²?" flashes and fades, hand tag "don't forget the ½".

### 5. the trapezium: an average width
> A trapezium has just one pair of parallel sides. Picture a vegetable plot whose parallel sides are six metres and ten metres, four metres apart. Use the same trick. Copy it, turn the copy upside down, and fit them together. They make a parallelogram whose base is six plus ten, sixteen metres, and whose height is four. That's sixty-four square metres, and one trapezium is half of it, thirty-two square **metres**. So the area of a trapezium is a half, times a plus b, times h. There's another way to see it. Half of six plus ten is eight, the average of the two parallel sides. So a trapezium has the same area as a rectangle eight metres wide and four metres **long**. Average the two parallel sides, then times the height. That idea comes back later in the course.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): a coded trapezium plot (teal, top "6 m", bottom "10 m", dashed height "4 m" amber); grey chip "trapezium: one pair of parallel sides". On "Copy it" a copy rotates 180° and joins along a slanted side, making a parallelogram with base labelled "6 + 10 = 16 m"; row "16 × 4 = 64 m²"; on "metres" (first) row "½ × 64 = 32 m²" (framed green). On "a half, times a plus b" the formula strip, 52 px: "A = ½(a + b)h". On "average" a dashed teal line at mid-height across the trapezium, labelled "(6 + 10) ÷ 2 = 8 m", hand tag "average width"; on "long" a ghost 8 m by 4 m rectangle overlays the trapezium, the two slivers swapping places, row "8 × 4 = 32 m²" (framed green).

### 6. composite shapes: plan first
> Back to the backyard, with the bed in the corner. The grass is the lawn with the triangle cut out, a composite shape. Before touching any numbers, write the plan. Lawn equals rectangle minus triangle. Ninety-six minus six is ninety square metres of **turf**. Now the edging, which only goes along the outside of the grass. Trace it. Twelve along the top, eight down the side, nine along the bottom, because the bed takes three, then four up the side, because the bed takes four, and five along the bed's sloping edge. That's thirty-eight **metres**. The edges inside the shape, and the parts the bed took, don't count. Only the **outside**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why perimeter and area differ: one adds lengths, one counts squares (2). Why a parallelogram is bh: the slid triangle makes a rectangle (3). Why the slant side is wrong for area: the slider shows the area unchanged as it grows (3). Why a triangle has ½: two make a parallelogram (4). Why a trapezium is ½(a + b)h: two make a parallelogram with base a + b; it's the average width (5). Why only outside edges: edging goes along the boundary (6).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** slant side as height (3, A1 crossed); the missing ½ (4); internal edges (6); subtracting edges from a perimeter (A2 crossed).
