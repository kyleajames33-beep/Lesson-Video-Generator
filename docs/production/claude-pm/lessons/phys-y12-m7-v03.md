- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "How do different sources produce different spectral patterns?" The answer, built in order: what the source is made of and its state decides the pattern: dense and hot gives every wavelength (3); thin and hot gives only its own wavelengths (4, 5); cool and thin in front of a hot source removes those same wavelengths (6); reflection just returns the original pattern (7).

### 2. spreading light out
> A spectroscope spreads light out by wavelength. Inside, a grating or a prism sends each wavelength in a slightly different direction, and how a grating does that is a later video. Visible light is a tiny slice of the electromagnetic spectrum, from about four hundred nanometres for violet to about seven hundred for **red**. So a spectrum is a map. It shows how much light the source gives out at each **wavelength**.

### 3. hot and dense: every colour
> Start with the filament. It's a coil of tungsten wire, heated by the current to about twenty-nine hundred kelvin. In a solid, the atoms are packed tightly and interact strongly, so their charges are shaken in every possible way. Remember, accelerating charges radiate. Shake them in every way, and you get every **wavelength**, with no **gaps**. That's a continuous spectrum. Heat the filament more, and it gets brighter, and its light shifts towards the blue **end**. But its smooth spread depends mainly on temperature, not on the metal, which is why it can't identify an element.

### 4. hot and thin: bright lines
> Now the discharge tube. It holds hydrogen gas at low pressure, with a high voltage across it. Electrons crash into the atoms and give them energy. Here's the key idea. An electron in an atom can only have certain energies, called energy **levels**. When it drops from a higher level to a lower one, the atom gives out light of one particular **wavelength**. A bigger drop gives a shorter wavelength. Hydrogen's drops down to the second level give its four bright visible lines, at six hundred and fifty-six, four hundred and eighty-six, four hundred and thirty-four, and four hundred and ten **nanometres**. In a thin gas the atoms are far apart, so each behaves on its own, and the lines stay **sharp**.

### 5. a fingerprint for every element
> Every element has its own set of energy levels, so it has its own set of lines. That pattern is a fingerprint. Sodium gives two yellow lines, very close together, at five hundred and eighty-nine **nanometres**. Neon gives a crowd of red and orange **lines**. To identify a gas, match its lines to reference spectra, and every observed line has to match, not just one. Here's a surprise. In 1868, sunlight showed a yellow line that matched no element known on Earth. It was named helium, after the Greek word for the Sun, and it wasn't found on Earth for another twenty-seven **years**.

### 6. cool and thin in front: dark lines
> Now put cool sodium vapour in front of the hot filament. Cool atoms sit in their lowest energy level. They absorb exactly the wavelengths they would give out, because the same energy gaps are involved, this time with electrons jumping **up**. So the rainbow comes through with two dark lines, at exactly the same places as sodium's bright yellow **lines**. The absorbed light isn't destroyed. It's given out again, but in all directions, so very little carries on towards you, and the line looks **dark**. The Sun works the same way. Its hot, dense layers give a continuous spectrum, and cooler gas above them absorbs, leaving the dark lines first mapped by Joseph von Fraunhofer.

### 7. reflected sunlight
> What about sunlight bouncing off the Moon, a cloud or a sheet of white paper? You see the same dark lines, in the same places. Reflection doesn't make a new spectrum. The Moon isn't a glowing gas. It just sends sunlight back, so moonlight carries the Sun's **pattern**. That's also the safe way to look at sunlight. Never point a spectroscope at the Sun. ## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 10; original wording, 2 marks).** "The emission spectrum of an unknown gas in a discharge tube has bright lines at 447 nm, 502 nm, 588 nm, 668 nm and 707 nm. Using the reference spectra shown, identify the gas and justify your answer."
Model answer: Helium (1 mark). Every observed line matches a line in helium's reference spectrum; each element's energy levels give a unique set of lines, so every observed line must be accounted for. Neon also has a line near 588 nm, but the other four observed lines (447, 502, 668 and 707 nm) have no neon match, so it is not neon (1 mark). Helium's 471 and 492 nm reference lines are not seen; a missing (often fainter) reference line does not rule helium out, whereas an unmatched observed line does rule an element out. Classic mistake (crossed): identifying neon from the single 588 nm line.

**Attempt 2 (scene 12; original wording, 3 marks).** "Light from the full Moon is viewed through a spectroscope. The spectrum is continuous, crossed by many dark lines. (a) Explain how the dark lines are produced. (b) Explain why this spectrum is not an emission spectrum of the Moon."
Model answer: (a) The Sun's hot, dense layers emit a continuous spectrum; cooler gas in the Sun's outer layers absorbs light at wavelengths matching jumps between its atoms' energy levels and re-emits it in all directions, so less reaches the observer at those wavelengths, giving dark absorption lines (2 marks). (b) The Moon only reflects sunlight; it is not a hot thin gas, so it produces no emission lines of its own, and the reflected light keeps the Sun's absorption spectrum (1 mark). Classic mistake (crossed): attributing the lines to the Moon or its atmosphere.
