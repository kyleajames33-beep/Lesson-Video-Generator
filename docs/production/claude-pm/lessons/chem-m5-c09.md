- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) K = (forward number) / (reverse number) holds exactly for the C5 teaching rate model (elementary steps). N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2 is in fact close to elementary, but for multi-step real mechanisms the sign of the change in K with temperature still follows the sign of the heat of reaction (thermodynamics), though the "two numbers" picture is a teaching model; the learner copy says "in this model" once. In real N2O4 / NO2 the NO2 recombination barrier is near zero, so the real reverse number changes little and the real forward number rises by about the whole K ratio (about 80); the made-up pair in 4b is a general pattern labelled as made-up, so no false claim is made (science check finding 7). (b) "A catalyst changes both numbers by the same factor": true for a catalyst that lowers a single barrier for both directions (C4b). (c) The sign of the K change follows the sign of the heat of reaction at constant pressure or volume; the simple rule "endothermic: K rises" assumes the heat of reaction does not change sign across the temperature range and uses "heat" as ΔH. It is not contradicted because that video states no temperature, and no on-screen note is needed (review nice-to-have 7). The two eighties are equal because the cold settled Q equals the old K (Q = 0.0061), not by coincidence. (i) Qualitative only: no van 't Hoff, no ln, no calculation of K at a new temperature, no use of ΔG. "K is constant at a fixed temperature" is stated. (j) Naming clash with C8 is resolved by author note under Learner-visible vocabulary. (k) Spoken numerals are kept few: 0.0061, 0.49, 0.040, 18 and 79 percent, 160, 2.0, 0.0329, 0.0142, 200 and 2.5 only; the 164 and 408 rate numbers of revision 1 are gone. (l) Sources and other printed values (science check findings 1 and 2): Kc = 0.0061 and 0.49 follow from tabulated Kp (2NO2 to N2O4) = 6.7 at 298 K and 0.066 at 100 C, agree with NIST-JANAF (0.0060 and 0.485) and fit a measured Kc = 2.00 mol/L at 134 C. Other books print 0.0046 (25 C) and 0.211 (100 C); both are explained in the science check (0.0046 is about 24 percent low; 0.211 is about 2.3 times too low, and fits about 80 to 85 C) and neither is used. (m) Idealisation (finding 6) and the start concentration: at 0.10 mol/L a 25 C tube would hold N2O4 above its vapour pressure and condense. With 0.040 mol/L the cold tube holds N2O4 at about 0.0329 mol/L, a partial pressure of about 0.0329 x 24.47 = 0.80 atm (about 82 kPa), below the roughly 96 kPa vapour pressure at 20 C (higher at 25 C), so the tube stays gas. The vapour pressure at 25 C itself is unverified. (n) Quadratic, off screen: with N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2, start 0.040 mol/L and x the amount of N2O4 that reacts, K = (2x)^2 / (0.040 - x) = 4x^2 / (0.040 - x), so 4x^2 + Kx - 0.040K = 0. Worked in "Numbers to verify". (o) The reversed values: 1 / 0.0061 = 163.9 is rounded to two figures as "about 160", and 1 / 0.49 = 2.04 as "about 2.0"; the word "about" is always spoken. (p) Heat in the attempt: -9.4 kJ per mole of equation at 25 C; a learner may be told "about 10 kilojoules at room temperature" if the line is revised, but the spoken line stays "about 10".

Main question: *If you change the temperature of a mixture at equilibrium, what happens to K, and how can you tell which way it goes?*
Entry knowledge (C1 to C8 and Year 11): equal rates at equilibrium; the heat-absorbing reaction speeds up more when heated (C3); the N2O4/NO2 tube (colour); each rate is a fixed number times concentrations (C5); K is products over reactants with coefficients as powers; reversing the equation flips K (C6); Q compared with K gives the direction (C8); a catalyst speeds up both directions equally (C4b).
Stops after: saying that K depends only on temperature (for a given equation as written); explaining with the forward and reverse numbers why heating changes K; stating the rule (heat-absorbing forward reaction: K rises when heated; heat-releasing: K falls) and the reverse for cooling; reading the rule backwards from a K table; telling a shift caused by a change in concentration, pressure or a catalyst (K the same) from a temperature change (K different); describing a heated mixture as a new K followed by a movement of Q toward it; using the rule on an unseen reaction. No numerical K at a new temperature from ΔH, no van 't Hoff, no reading ΔH from tables beyond the one-sentence converse rule, no colourimetry, no Gibbs.
Handoff: chem-m5-p02, how measured colour data can give K (the route lists p02 next at playlist order 14, then c10). If the playlist runs c10 first, change the line and chip in scene 11.

### 1. hook (does anything change K?)
> In the last few videos we did all sorts of things to a mixture at equilibrium. We added a reactant, took out a product, squeezed the container. The mixture shifted every time, but **K never moved**. Here is a sealed tube of nitrogen dioxide, the brown gas, in balance with colourless N two O four. In **hot water** it goes darker. In **ice** it fades. K is built from the settled concentrations, so if the hot tube settles with very different concentrations, **can K really stay fixed**? The question is: does K change when the temperature does, and which way?

Visual: the C3 sealed tube (N2O4 and NO2 dots, brown tint) centre-left with the equation N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g) writing on. On "K never moved" a teal K marker (the C8 number line, small, top right) holds still with a pen tick, and three small ghost icons (a plus dot, a minus dot, a squeeze arrow) pass over it. On "hot water" the tube moves into a terracotta bath and the brown deepens; on "ice" it moves into a pale blue bath and the brown fades. On "can K really stay fixed" the teal marker pulses and a "?" and a small up-down arrow appear beside it. Heading "Does temperature change K?" writes on.

### 2. K is one fixed number divided by another
> First, why was K constant? Remember the rate model from the expressions video: each rate is a **fixed number times the concentrations**. **Call the two fixed numbers** the forward number and the reverse number. At equilibrium the two rates are equal, so K, the products over the reactants, **equals the forward number divided by the reverse number**. In this model, then, K can only change if one of those two numbers changes.

Visual: a recall on the C5 builder, not a derivation, over the tube. On "fixed number times" the rate line (a rate is a fixed number times concentrations) writes on. On "Call the two fixed numbers" two small dials appear and label themselves "forward number" (blue, for the forward reaction, N2O4 splitting) and "reverse number" (amber, for the reverse reaction, NO2 joining): the first use of these names. On "the two rates are equal" the two rate bars (blue, amber) flash at equal height with the teal bracket and fade, and the recall line "K = forward number / reverse number" writes under the dials. On the last sentence the two dials get a pen ring each and "K changes only if a dial changes" writes. The tag "in this model" stays small. Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 3. what changes a number (the cause of the temperature effect)
> So what could change a number? Adding more N two O four, or squeezing the container, changes the concentrations but not the numbers, and a **catalyst** raises both numbers by the same factor, so their ratio stays put. **Temperature** is different. When we heated the nitrogen dioxide tube, both reactions sped up, but the one that takes in heat sped up more. Going forward, N two O four splitting takes in heat, so heating raises the **forward number** far more than the reverse number.

Visual: the two dials stay. On "Adding more" a pen arrow drops extra N2O4 dots into the tube and the tube shrinks slightly on "squeezing"; the concentration readout changes and the dials do not move (a small "still the same" tick). On "catalyst" a small catalyst chip pushes both dials up equally, and a ratio chip "same ratio" lands. On "Temperature" a thermometer rises and the dials start to turn. On "takes in heat" the C3 heat-in-the-equation chip appears: "N2O4 + heat ⇌ 2NO2" with the heat term in terracotta on the left. On "forward number far more" the blue dial climbs a long way and the amber dial a short way (no numbers yet). The full four-way table waits for scene 6.

### 4a. the evidence: measured K and the tube
> Now the evidence. The measured values for this reaction give K as **0.0061** at twenty-five degrees Celsius, and about **0.49** at a hundred degrees. Now fill a tube with 0.040 moles per litre of N two O four. At twenty-five degrees only about **eighteen percent** of it splits, so the tube is **pale**. At a hundred degrees about **seventy-nine percent** has split, so it is a **deep brown**. A bigger K is what that deeper brown looks like.

### 4b. what the two numbers must have done (made-up example)
> So what must the two numbers have done? K is the forward number over the reverse number, and K grew to about eighty times as big, so the forward number must have grown about **eighty times as much** as the reverse number, in proportion. Here is one **made-up** pair that does it, just to show the pattern. Heating raises the forward number **two hundred times**, and the reverse number only **two and a half times**. Both rise, and two hundred over two and a half is eighty. Only the unequal rise matters.

Visual: chip "made-up scale". No fractions of invented rate numbers, no rate bars. The tube sits in the hot bath. Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 5a. the other way round: write the reaction backwards
> Now write the same reaction **backwards**: two N O two making N two O four. Going this way the reaction gives out heat, the same amount that the other way takes in. Its K is the flip of the old one. One over 0.0061 is about **160** at twenty-five degrees, and one over 0.49 is about **2.0** at a hundred. So here K **falls** when you heat it.

Visual: the equation strip flips with the C6 curved arrow to 2NO2(g) ⇌ N2O4(g) + heat (heat term in terracotta on the right). ### 5b. the reason and the rule
> Why? Heating speeds up the reaction that takes in heat more, and written this way that is the **reverse** reaction, so the reverse number grows more than the forward number. So here is the rule. If the forward reaction **takes in** heat, heating raises K. If the forward reaction **gives out** heat, heating lowers K. Cooling does the opposite.

Visual: first, on "Why?", the dials stay named forward number (blue) and reverse number (amber); the reverse dial climbs far more than the forward dial. No relabelling. Then two cards write on one at a time: "forward takes in heat: heat it, K rises" (blue up arrow, terracotta flame) and "forward gives out heat: heat it, K falls" (blue down arrow, terracotta flame); a small "cool it: opposite" tag under both. Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 6. four changes, only one moves K
> Let's put the four changes side by side in a table. The first three, adding N two O four, squeezing the container and adding a catalyst, leave K alone. Heat the tube, and the numbers change by different amounts, so **K changes**. A shift is Q moving. A change in K is the target itself moving. **Only temperature moves the target.**

Visual: a four-row change table appears one row at a time on the right two thirds (rows 1 to 3 write on in turn during "the first three", row 4 on "Heat the tube"; the reasons live in the "what moves" column, not in the narration), columns "what you do", "what moves", "K". On the last two sentences the words "shift: Q moves" and "K changes: the target moves" write on separate lines with a pen "not the same thing". The tube column on the left runs a one-beat icon for each row. Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 7a. heating gives a new K, and Q is left behind
> Link it to the reaction quotient video. Take the tube settled at twenty-five degrees, with **0.0329** moles per litre of N two O four and **0.0142** of N O two. Q is 0.0142 squared over 0.0329, which is **0.0061**, equal to K. Now drop it in boiling water. The amounts have not had time to change, so Q is still 0.0061, but **K is now 0.49**. Q is below K.

Visual: the cold settled tube with the label "settled at 25 C" and the readout [N2O4] = 0.0329 mol/L, [NO2] = 0.0142 mol/L. The C5 builder writes the Q working line: Q = [NO2]^2 / [N2O4] = 0.0142^2 / 0.0329 = 0.0061, with the tag "Q: no unit written" at the substitution (C8 policy). Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 7b. then the mixture moves to match
> Q below K means the forward reaction is the quicker one. The forward rate over the reverse rate is K over Q, so the forward rate is about **eighty times** the reverse rate, in this model. These are the rates right now, not the fixed numbers. N two O four splits, N O two builds up, and Q climbs until it reaches the new K. So heating works in two steps: **first K changes, then the mixture moves to match it**.

Visual: the rate bars, labelled "forward rate" and "reverse rate" (not "numbers"), appear with "?" and then the blue bar grows to about 80 times the amber bar (label "about 80 times, in this model"; the working "forward rate / reverse rate = K / Q = 0.49 / 0.0061 = 80" shown; the tag "rates right now, not the fixed numbers"). The Q marker climbs along the log line toward the new K as the tube browns, reaching it with a teal flash; the readouts tick to 0.0082 mol/L for N2O4 and 0.0635 mol/L for NO2 (check: 0.0635 squared over 0.0082 = 0.49; tag "settled amounts given; you can check them with the ICE table from C6"). The two steps write large in turn: "1. K changes" and "2. Q moves to the new K". Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 8. memory aid (change T, change K; heat on a side for direction)
> Here are two lines. First: **change T, change K. Change anything else, K stays.** Second, to find the direction, treat heat like a chemical on one side of the equation, but only for the direction. If the reaction takes in heat, heat is on the left, so heating pushes the mixture right and K goes **up**. If it gives out heat, heat is on the right, so K goes **down**. Unlike a real reactant, heat changes the numbers themselves, which is why K moves.

Visual: the aid line "Change T, change K. Change anything else, K stays." writes large and handwritten first, with a pen arrow to the K marker. Then two lines of equation write on in turn: "reactants + heat ⇌ products" with a terracotta flame arrow on the left and "K up when heated"; "reactants ⇌ products + heat" with the flame on the right and "K down when heated". A small tag "direction only; heat changes the numbers" sits under them. A condition tag, large enough to read in the hold: "same equation as written; heat is never put in the K expression". Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 10a. feedback, heating the tube
> The forward reaction gives out heat, so the **reverse** reaction is the one that takes in heat. ### 12. key notes (quiet, hold about 90 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Only a change in temperature changes the value of K. For a given equation at a fixed temperature, adding or removing a substance, changing the pressure or volume, or adding a catalyst does not change K.
2. Why (in a simple model): K is the forward number divided by the reverse number. Heating raises both numbers but raises the heat-absorbing direction's more, so K changes. A catalyst raises both equally, so K does not.
3. If the forward reaction takes in heat (endothermic), K rises when heated and falls when cooled. If the forward reaction gives out heat (exothermic), K falls when heated and rises when cooled.
4. A shift is Q moving toward K. A temperature change moves K first, then Q follows.
5. Reading a table: K rising with temperature means the forward reaction is endothermic. K falling means it is exothermic.
6. Reversing the equation flips K (K becomes one over K) and the heat sign. The bigger the heat of reaction, the bigger the change in K (N2O4: K grew about 80 times; H2 + I2: a slight fall).

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Change T, change K. Change anything else, K stays.* Second line: *Use heat like a chemical only to find the direction: heat on the left, heating raises K. Heat on the right, heating lowers K.* Condition tag: "same equation as written; heat never goes in the K expression."
