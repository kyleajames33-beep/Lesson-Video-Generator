- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Revision 4: no rate equations or rate constants appear in learner copy. The spoken cause, "the reaction that takes in heat speeds up more", is the C3 statement and rests on Ea(forward) = Ea(reverse) + ΔH: the endothermic direction has the higher barrier, and by the Arrhenius relation a higher barrier gives a bigger proportional rise in rate on heating. The two rates are equal just before heating and the amounts have not yet changed, so at that moment the bigger proportional rise is also the bigger rise in rate, which is what the bars show. For multi-step mechanisms the sign of the K change still follows the sign of ΔH (thermodynamics). Idealisation kept out of learner copy: in real N2O4 / NO2 the NO2 recombination has almost no barrier and its rate constant changes little with temperature (it may even fall slightly), so "heating speeds up both reactions" is the general school picture; what matters for K, that the heat-absorbing direction gains relative to the other, holds exactly (science check finding 7). (b) "A catalyst speeds up both reactions by the same factor": true for a catalyst that lowers the barrier of both directions by the same amount (one shared transition state, C4b); the rates stay equal, so the settled mixture and K are unchanged. (c) The sign of the K change follows the sign of the heat of reaction at constant pressure or volume; the simple rule "endothermic: K rises" assumes the heat of reaction does not change sign across the temperature range and uses "heat" as ΔH. (d) The size of the change depends on the size of ΔH (H2 + I2: small; N2O4: large). The video says so in the feedback and the notes. (e) K for N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2 in concentrations has units mol/L; the video writes K without a unit (C5 policy, see open decisions). (f) The C6 attempt used K = 0.0257 for N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2 at "a steady temperature" with no temperature given; that is illustrative and sits between the real 25 C and 100 C values (about 45 C by the van 't Hoff relation). It is not contradicted because that video states no temperature, and no on-screen note is needed (review nice-to-have 7). (g) Pronunciation: "N two O four", "N O two", "H two", "I two", "H I", "K" as the letter, "Q" as the letter. Say "nought point" rather than "point". Say "a hundred degrees" rather than "100 C" and always give "degrees Celsius" once at first use. (h) "About eighty times" is the K ratio 0.49 / 0.0061 = 80, spoken once in 4a and recalled in 10a and key note 6. Revision 4 no longer says anything about the size of the rate difference in 7b (no K over Q). (i) Qualitative only: no van 't Hoff, no ln, no calculation of K at a new temperature, no use of ΔG. "K is constant at a fixed temperature" is stated. (j) Naming clash with C8 is resolved by author note under Learner-visible vocabulary. (k) Spoken numerals are kept few: 0.0061, 0.49, eighty, 0.040, 18 and 79 percent, 160, 2.0, 0.0329 and 0.0142 (N2O4 scenes), 10, 49, 450 and 500 (attempt) only; the 200 and 2.5 of the old 4b and the 164 and 408 of revision 1 are gone. (l) Sources and other printed values (science check findings 1 and 2): Kc = 0.0061 and 0.49 follow from tabulated Kp (2NO2 to N2O4) = 6.7 at 298 K and 0.066 at 100 C, agree with NIST-JANAF (0.0060 and 0.485) and fit a measured Kc = 2.00 mol/L at 134 C. Other books print 0.0046 (25 C) and 0.211 (100 C); both are explained in the science check (0.0046 is about 24 percent low; 0.211 is about 2.3 times too low, and fits about 80 to 85 C) and neither is used. (m) Idealisation (finding 6) and the start concentration: at 0.10 mol/L a 25 C tube would hold N2O4 above its vapour pressure and condense. With 0.040 mol/L the cold tube holds N2O4 at about 0.0329 mol/L, a partial pressure of about 0.0329 x 24.47 = 0.80 atm (about 82 kPa), below the roughly 96 kPa vapour pressure at 20 C (higher at 25 C), so the tube stays gas. The vapour pressure at 25 C itself is unverified. (n) Quadratic, off screen: with N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2, start 0.040 mol/L and x the amount of N2O4 that reacts, K = (2x)^2 / (0.040 - x) = 4x^2 / (0.040 - x), so 4x^2 + Kx - 0.040K = 0. Worked in "Numbers to verify". (o) The reversed values: 1 / 0.0061 = 163.9 is rounded to two figures as "about 160", and 1 / 0.49 = 2.04 as "about 2.0"; the word "about" is always spoken. (p) Heat in the attempt: -9.4 kJ per mole of equation at 25 C; a learner may be told "about 10 kilojoules at room temperature" if the line is revised, but the spoken line stays "about 10".

Main question: *If you change the temperature of a mixture at equilibrium, what happens to K, and how can you tell which way it goes?*
Entry knowledge (C1 to C8 and Year 11): equal rates at equilibrium; the faster rate falls and the slower rate rises until they meet (C2); the heat-absorbing reaction needs harder collisions and speeds up more when heated (C3); the N2O4/NO2 tube (colour); K is products over reactants with coefficients as powers, the same for any settled mixture at one temperature (C5); reversing the equation flips K (C6); Q compared with K gives the direction, by comparison with a settled tube (C8); a catalyst speeds up both directions equally (C4b).
Stops after: saying that K depends only on temperature (for a given equation as written); explaining with the unequal speed-up of the two reactions why heating changes K; stating the rule (heat-absorbing forward reaction: K rises when heated; heat-releasing: K falls) and the reverse for cooling; reading the rule backwards from a K table; telling a shift caused by a change in concentration, pressure or a catalyst (K the same) from a temperature change (K different); describing a heated mixture as a new K followed by a movement of Q toward it; using the rule on an unseen reaction. No numerical K at a new temperature from ΔH, no van 't Hoff, no reading ΔH from tables beyond the one-sentence converse rule, no colourimetry, no Gibbs.

### 1. hook (does anything change K?)
> In the last few videos we did all sorts of things to a mixture at equilibrium. We added a reactant, took out a product, squeezed the container. The mixture shifted every time, but **K never moved**. Here is a sealed tube of nitrogen dioxide, the brown gas, in balance with colourless N two O four. In **hot water** it goes darker. In **ice** it fades. K is built from the settled concentrations, so if the hot tube settles with very different concentrations, **can K really stay fixed**? The question is: does K change when the temperature does, and which way?

Visual: the C3 sealed tube (N2O4 and NO2 dots, brown tint) centre-left with the equation N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g) writing on. On "K never moved" a teal K marker (the C8 number line, small, top right) holds still with a pen tick, and three small ghost icons (a plus dot, a minus dot, a squeeze arrow) pass over it. On "hot water" the tube moves into a terracotta bath and the brown deepens; on "ice" it moves into a pale blue bath and the brown fades. On "can K really stay fixed" the teal marker pulses and a "?" and a small up-down arrow appear beside it. Heading "Does temperature change K?" writes on.

### 2. why K stayed fixed
> First, why did K stay fixed? Adding a chemical or squeezing the container changes the amounts, so particles meet more or less often, and for a while one reaction runs **faster than the other**. The mixture shifts until products over reactants is **the same number again**, even though the amounts are now different. Each collision is just as likely to work as before, so the mixture settles at the same K. **Temperature is different**: heating makes collisions more likely to work, even with the same amounts in the tube, and that moves K itself.

Visual: over the tube, the C4a disturbance thumbnails (add, squeeze) pop in a row with pen arrows to the log line from C8, where a Q marker jumps away from the teal K marker on "faster than the other" and slides back to it on "the same number again" (tag "Q moves, K stays"; a small "amounts different" tag on the tube dots). On "the same K" the K marker gets a teal tick. On "Temperature is different" a thermometer icon rises beside the tube and the K marker gets a pen ring with "?" (no movement yet). Hold about 2 seconds after the last word.

### 3. how temperature changes K (the cause)
> Here is how. Heating speeds up **both** reactions, but not equally. As with the cobalt tube, the reaction that **takes in heat** needs harder collisions, so it gains the most. Going forward, N two O four splitting takes in heat, so splitting speeds up **far more** than joining. N O two builds up and N two O four is used up, so splitting slows and joining speeds up, until the rates are equal again with **more N O two** and less N two O four than before. More product on top, less reactant underneath, so **K is bigger**. A **catalyst** is different: it speeds up both reactions by the same factor, so the rates stay equal, nothing shifts, and K stays the same.

Visual: the two rate bars (blue "splitting, forward", amber "joining, reverse") stand level with the teal "equal" bracket. On "takes in heat" the C3 heat-in-the-equation chip appears: "N2O4 + heat ⇌ 2NO2" with the heat term in terracotta on the left, and the C3 collision strip replays once beside the forward arrow (tag "needs a hard collision"). On "far more" a flame icon heats the tube and both bars rise, the blue bar much further than the amber (no numbers; label "splitting speeds up more"). On "N O two builds up" brown NO2 dots build in the tube and N2O4 dots leave, while the blue bar falls and the amber bar rises until they meet again at the teal bracket on "equal again". ### 4a. the evidence: measured K and the tube

At a hundred degrees about **seventy-nine percent** has split, so it is a **deep brown**. A bigger K is what that deeper brown looks like.

### 5a. the other way round: write the reaction backwards

Its K is the flip of the old one. One over 0.0061 is about **160** at twenty-five degrees, and one over 0.49 is about **2.0** at a hundred. So here K **falls** when you heat it.

### 5b. the reason and the rule
> Why? It is the same tube going brown; only the way we write it has changed. Heating speeds up the reaction that takes in heat more, and written this way that is the **reverse** reaction, N two O four splitting. So N two O four, which is now the product, gets used up, and the settled mixture holds **less product**. K is smaller. So here is the rule. If the forward reaction **takes in** heat, heating raises K. If the forward reaction **gives out** heat, heating lowers K. Cooling does the opposite.

Visual: first, on "Why?", the tube from 4a browns once more in the hot bath with the tag "same tube, written the other way". The two rate bars return, now labelled for the backwards equation (blue "joining, forward", amber "splitting, reverse"); on "reverse" both bars rise and the amber bar rises much further than the blue bar, and on "less product" the N2O4 dots in the tube fall while the NO2 dots rise, with a pen ring on the N2O4 term, now on the top of the flipped K expression "K = [N2O4] / [NO2]^2". ### 6. four changes, only one moves K
> Let's put the four changes side by side in a table. The first three, adding N two O four, squeezing the container and adding a catalyst, leave K alone. Heat the tube, and the two reactions speed up by different amounts, so **K changes**. A shift is Q moving. A change in K is the target itself moving. **Only temperature moves the target.**

### 7a. heating gives a new K, and Q is left behind
> Link it to the reaction quotient video. Take the tube settled at twenty-five degrees, with **0.0329** moles per litre of N two O four and **0.0142** of N O two. Q is 0.0142 squared over 0.0329, which is **0.0061**, equal to K. Now drop it in boiling water. The amounts have not had time to change, so Q is still 0.0061, but **K is now 0.49**. Q is below K.

### 7b. then the mixture moves to match
> Q below K means that, compared with a tube settled at a hundred degrees, this tube holds too little N O two, so N two O four now **splits faster than it forms**. N O two builds up, and Q climbs until it reaches the new K. So heating works in two steps: **first K changes, then the mixture moves to match it**.

### 8. memory aid (change T, change K; heat on a side for direction)
> Here are two lines. First: **change T, change K. Change anything else, K stays.** Second, to find the direction, treat heat like a chemical on one side of the equation, but only for the direction. If the reaction takes in heat, heat is on the left, so heating pushes the mixture right and K goes **up**. If it gives out heat, heat is on the right, so K goes **down**. Unlike a real reactant, heat speeds the two reactions up by different amounts, which is why K moves.

### 12. key notes (quiet, hold about 90 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Only a change in temperature changes the value of K. For a given equation at a fixed temperature, adding or removing a substance, changing the pressure or volume, or adding a catalyst does not change K.
2. Why: heating speeds up both reactions, but the one that takes in heat (it needs harder collisions) speeds up more, so the rates balance again only at a new mixture, and K changes. A catalyst speeds up both by the same factor, so K does not change.
3. If the forward reaction takes in heat (endothermic), K rises when heated and falls when cooled. If the forward reaction gives out heat (exothermic), K falls when heated and rises when cooled.
4. A shift is Q moving toward K. A temperature change moves K first, then Q follows.
5. Reading a table: K rising with temperature means the forward reaction is endothermic. K falling means it is exothermic.
6. Reversing the equation flips K (K becomes one over K) and the heat sign. The bigger the heat of reaction, the bigger the change in K (N2O4: K grew about 80 times; H2 + I2: a slight fall).

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Change T, change K. Change anything else, K stays.* Second line: *Use heat like a chemical only to find the direction: heat on the left, heating raises K. Heat on the right, heating lowers K.* Condition tag: "same equation as written; heat never goes in the K expression."
