- **Why these three sources.** They differ in author, purpose, audience, date and kind of evidence, so the three checks give three different answers. S1 is Australian, current in its site footer but undated on the page, written for the public by a charity that also advocates (purpose); it carries the mechanisms and the signs. S2 is a government statistics agency, dated 2026, whose survey measured blood (HbA1c) rather than relying only on what people reported; it carries the scale and long-term outcomes. S3 is a randomised trial, the only source here that tests cause, but from 1993 and in type 1 only. S1 and S2 do not depend on S3, and S3's result fits S1's account of long-term damage (agreement across independent kinds of evidence).
- **What each source is used for (claims only as verified).** S1 type 1 page: "the body's own immune system is activated to destroy the beta cells in the pancreas"; glucose "cannot enter the body's cells"; signs "excessive thirst and urination, unexplained weight loss, weakness and fatigue and blurred vision"; "managed with insulin injections several times a day or the use of an insulin pump"; "not linked to modifiable lifestyle factors". S1 type 2 page: "the body becomes resistant to the normal effects of insulin"; the pancreas "responds by producing greater and greater amounts of insulin"; "develops over a long period of time (years)"; the insulin-producing cells "wear themselves out"; "Diabetes runs in the family" and modifiable risk factors (high blood pressure, being overweight, not being active enough, poor diet); "Many people with type 2 diabetes have no symptoms"; "no cure", managed "through lifestyle modifications and medication", with "oral medications and/or insulin injections" over time. S2: about 1 in 15 Australians (about 1.3 million, 6.5 per cent of adults) had diabetes in 2022 to 2024, 0.9 per cent newly diagnosed by the survey's measurement (HbA1c); about 7,000 lower limb amputations among people with type 2 diabetes in 2023 to 2024; diabetes the underlying cause of about 6,000 deaths and an underlying or associated cause of about 20,800 in total (11 per cent of all deaths; the 6,000 are included, not added) in 2024. S3: 1,441 people with insulin-dependent (type 1) diabetes randomly assigned to intensive or conventional therapy; mean follow-up 6.5 years; intensive therapy reduced the risk of developing retinopathy by 76 per cent, slowed its progression by 54 per cent, reduced albuminuria by 54 per cent and clinical neuropathy by 60 per cent; severe hypoglycaemia increased two- to threefold.
