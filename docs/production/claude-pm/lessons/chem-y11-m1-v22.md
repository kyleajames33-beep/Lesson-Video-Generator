Author notes (not learner copy):

(b) Melting points (site; CRC Handbook in brackets): NaCl 801 °C (800.7); MgO 2852 °C (2825); LiF 848 °C (848.2); CsI 632 °C (632); CaCl₂ 772 °C. Narration rounds MgO to "about two thousand eight hundred and fifty"; attempt 2 uses the site's 2852 °C.

### 1. hook: a crystal that won't bend
> Last time, we saw that salt is built from sodium and chloride ions, each attracting every neighbour around it. Here's a single salt crystal. It's hard, and you can't easily scratch it. But give it a sharp blow, and it doesn't bend. It breaks into pieces with flat **faces**. And it's full of charged ions, yet as a solid, it doesn't conduct electricity at **all**. Melt it, or dissolve it in water, and suddenly it **does**. One structure explains all of this.

### 2. the ionic lattice
> That structure is called an ionic lattice. It's a giant, regular, three-dimensional pattern of ions, repeating in every direction. In sodium chloride, each sodium ion is surrounded by six chloride ions, and each chloride ion by six sodium **ions**. There are no separate molecules. The attractions act in every direction at once, and the formula, N a C l, only tells you the ratio of ions, one to **one**.

Visual: board clears to v21's ion layer, which extrudes into a 3 × 3 × 3 rock-salt cube and turns slowly; tag "ionic lattice (ionic network): giant, regular, 3D". On "ions" (first) the central Na⁺ lights with its six Cl⁻ neighbours (up, down, left, right, front, back) joined by amber lines, tag "6 : 6". On the fourth sentence a grey "NaCl molecule?" chip writes and is crossed. On the fifth sentence the attraction web pulses from one ion to all its neighbours. On "one" (last) a formula card "NaCl = 1 Na⁺ : 1 Cl⁻ (a ratio)" writes.

### 3. why it melts so high
> First, melting. To melt, the ions have to break out of their fixed places, and start moving past each other. But every ion is held by strong attractions to all of its neighbours, so that takes a lot of energy. That's why salt doesn't melt until eight hundred and one degrees **Celsius**.

Visual: the cube stays at the right; its ions vibrate gently in place. On the second sentence one ion tries to leave and its amber attraction lines stretch and pull it back. On the third sentence the attraction web pulses across the whole cube. On "Celsius" a coded thermometer draws at the left, 0 to 3000 °C, with a mark "NaCl: 801 °C"; a teal property chip "high melting point" joins the column.

### 4. bigger charges, stronger hold
> The charges on the ions matter too. Magnesium oxide is made of magnesium two plus and oxide two minus ions, double the charges in sodium chloride. Bigger charges attract more strongly, so magnesium oxide melts at about two thousand eight hundred and fifty degrees, far higher than **salt**. It's even used to line furnaces. Smaller ions sit closer together, so they attract more strongly too.

Visual: a second, smaller cube appears beside the first with ions "Mg²⁺" and "O²⁻" (charges doubled in teal). On the third sentence the attraction lines in the MgO cube draw thicker than in the NaCl cube. On "salt" the thermometer adds "MgO: about 2850 °C" far above the NaCl mark. On the fourth sentence a small grey tag "used to line furnaces". On the fifth sentence two small grey tags write: "same charges, smaller ions: LiF 848 °C", "larger ions: CsI 632 °C".

### 5. the trap: don't divide the temperatures
> Here's a trap that costs marks. Two thousand eight hundred and fifty, divided by eight hundred and one, is about three and a half. But that doesn't mean the attraction is three and a half times stronger. Zero on the Celsius scale isn't zero energy, so the ratio means nothing. Just say it plainly. Higher charges, stronger attraction, higher melting **point**.

Visual: the thermometer stays. On the first and second sentences a grey working line writes "2850 ÷ 801 ≈ 3.5". On the third sentence a chip "3.5 times stronger attraction" writes and is crossed. ### 6. hardness
> Second, hardness. To scratch or dent the crystal, you'd have to push ions out of their places, against all those strong attractions. So ionic solids are **hard**.

### 7. prediction (countdown, 3 seconds)

### 8. brittleness
> Before the shift, every positive ion sits next to negative ions. After the shift, positive ions line up next to positive ions, and negative next to **negative**. Like charges repel. So instead of bending, the layers push apart, and the crystal splits along a flat plane. That's why ionic solids are brittle, and why salt breaks into pieces with flat, smooth **faces**.

Visual: on the first sentence the cross-section's vertical neighbour pairs glow (red next to blue). The top two rows slide one ion along; on "negative" the new pairs glow red-red and blue-blue. ### 9. what conducting needs
> Now electricity. An electric current is a flow of charge. For a substance to conduct, it needs charged particles that are free to move. These are called charge **carriers**. In a metal wire, the carriers are electrons. In an ionic compound, the carriers can only be the ions **themselves**.

They can vibrate in place, but they can't travel toward the electrodes. Charged, but not free to move, so there's no **current**.

### 11. molten salt: ions set free
> Now melt the salt. At eight hundred and one degrees, the lattice breaks down, and the ions can move past each other. Connect the electrodes, and the lamp lights **up**. The positive sodium ions drift toward the negative electrode, and the negative chloride ions drift toward the positive **one**. The moving ions carry the current. This needs a furnace, so it's a demonstration you'd see in footage, not a school practical.

### 12. dissolved salt: water pulls the ions apart
> Dissolving does the same job at room temperature. Water molecules have slightly charged ends. They crowd around each ion, and pull it away from the lattice, so the ions spread out through the **water**. A solution in water is called aqueous. Now the ions are free to move, and the lamp lights **again**. This one you can test safely in the lab, with a low-voltage tester.

### 13. the whole answer
> So here's the whole answer. Strong attractions in every direction make ionic solids hard, with high melting points. Shifting a layer lines up like charges, so they're brittle. And they conduct only when the lattice is broken, by melting or dissolving, because only then can the ions move. No moving charges, no **current**.

### 18. handoff: molecules and their shape
> Notice that salt has no molecules at all, just ions. But many substances are made of molecules. Last time, we saw that water and carbon dioxide both contain polar bonds. A polar bond, with its delta plus and delta minus ends, is called a bond dipole. Yet water molecules have charged ends, and carbon dioxide molecules don't. The difference is their **shape**. Next time, we'll see how shape decides whether bond dipoles **cancel**.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Ionic lattice (ionic network): a giant, regular, 3D arrangement of oppositely charged ions held by strong attractions in all directions; no molecules (NaCl: each ion has 6 neighbours; the formula is a ratio).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style).** "Calcium chloride, CaCl₂, is an ionic compound. Explain why solid calcium chloride does not conduct electricity, but an aqueous solution of calcium chloride does. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Conduction requires mobile charge carriers (1). In solid CaCl₂ the Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are held in fixed positions in the ionic lattice, so they cannot move and the solid does not conduct (1). When it dissolves, water molecules separate the ions from the lattice, so Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ are free to move toward the electrodes and carry the current (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "electrons flow through the solution".

**Attempt 2 (scene 16; original wording, HSC style).** "Sodium chloride, NaCl, melts at 801 °C. Magnesium oxide, MgO, melts at 2852 °C. Both are ionic compounds with the same type of lattice. Explain the difference in their melting points. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Melting an ionic compound requires energy to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions between ions throughout the lattice (1). MgO contains Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions, with double the charges of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in NaCl (1). Higher charges produce much stronger attractions, so more energy is needed to break down the lattice and MgO has the higher melting point (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "the attractions in MgO are 3.6 times stronger" (a ratio of Celsius temperatures).
