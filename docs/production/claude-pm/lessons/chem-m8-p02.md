## Decisions

- **Main question:** *Why would a chemist weigh out a sample whose sulfate content is already known, and analyse it anyway? How do you carry out a gravimetric sulfate analysis safely, and use recovery to judge whether the method can be trusted?* Heading on screen: "Gravimetric practical: does the method get it all?"
- **Why a recovery investigation, not just a recipe:** the dotpoint says "conduct investigations", and the route stop says "evaluate recovery". Running the method on a sample of known composition gives a true value, so accuracy can be judged directly, which an unknown sample cannot do. Comparing digested and undigested precipitates gives the practical an aim, one independent variable, controlled variables and data to judge, and it tests c02's claim that digestion helps ("bigger crystals don't slip through the filter paper"). It also keeps the video from replaying c02's wastewater example.
- **Why potassium sulfate:** K₂SO₄ is a stable, non-hygroscopic salt that can be dried and weighed accurately, with one sulfate per formula unit (1 : 1 to BaSO₄), and it is not classified as hazardous. Anhydrous sodium sulfate takes up water from the air more readily, so it is used only in attempt 1, where the card states "pure, dry". The phrase "check standard" stays off screen; the narration says "a sample whose sulfate you already know".
- **Numbers (author note a; Numbers to verify):** 0.500 g dried K₂SO₄ (M 174.26) → 2.87 × 10⁻³ mol → expected BaSO₄ 0.670 g (M 233.39). Barium chloride: 20 mL of 0.20 mol/L = 4.0 × 10⁻³ mol, about 40% excess. Example data (labelled "example data" on screen): set A (filtered straight away) 0.630, 0.638, 0.627 g, mean 0.632 g, recovery 94.3%; set B (digested about 30 minutes) 0.664, 0.661, 0.666 g, mean 0.664 g, recovery 99.1%. Spreads 0.011 and 0.005 g; gap 0.032 g.
- **The evidence for the cause:** set A's filtrate is faintly cloudy and set B's is clear (a new aligned plate pair, Assets). This makes the accuracy argument evidence-based ("the cloudy filtrate shows where the sulfate went"), not a guess, and gives students the habit of looking at the filtrate.
- **Washing test (new, practical):** c02 says wash; p02 shows how to know when washing is complete: a few drops of the washings with silver nitrate solution give white silver chloride while chloride is still coming through, and stay clear once the barium chloride solution has been washed out. It uses c01's reaction (Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl). This is the route review's silver nitrate risk row.
- **Hook analogy (considered, cut for runtime):** "like checking a kitchen scale with an unopened one kilogram bag of flour before you trust it for a recipe". Mapping: the flour's known mass is to the scale as the known sulfate is to the method; a low reading means the instrument (here, the method) is losing something. It was drafted for scene 1 and cut to bring the video under 9.5 minutes; it can return if the voiced preview runs short. Where it breaks: a scale error is usually a calibration offset, while the method's error is lost or extra solid.
- **Barium hazard contrast (a surprise with a reason):** barium sulfate is so insoluble that it is swallowed for some X-ray imaging, while dissolved barium chloride is toxic if swallowed. This explains why the risk is the reagent and the filtrate, not the dried precipitate, and links back to c02's "barely dissolves". Hedged "for some X-ray scans" (author note e).
- **Site sources used (site lesson 2):** the Safety note ("Wear safety glasses and use tongs when handling hot crucibles and drying ovens"; "Many precipitating reagents (for example silver nitrate and barium chloride) are corrosive or toxic, so avoid skin contact and wash your hands after use. Place silver and heavy-metal residues in labelled waste containers, not down the sink."; scenes 11 and the risk table); the eight-stage sequence (scenes 4 to 7, with c02's acidify step); the error table (scene 9's "lost solid reads low"); Activity A1's damp-trial idea and Short Answer Q3's "evaluate the suitability" (left to g01, which uses new numbers); the hook context (sulfate monitoring), recalled only.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) a known sample tests the method (1); (2) recovery, the aim and the variables (2); (3) the expected mass from the formula (3); (4) prepare, acidify, heat, add excess Ba²⁺ slowly, check completeness (4); (5) digest or not, the prediction (5); (6) filter, rinse, wash, test the washings, look at the filtrate (6); (7) dry to constant mass and weigh (7); (8) recovery from the means (8); (9) reliable within each set, the gap bigger than the spread; accuracy against the known value; the cloudy filtrate shows the loss; repeats can't recover lost crystals (9); (10) the comparison is valid because only digestion changed, and the method measures only sulfate; the standard must be pure and dry (10); (11) hazards and controls, waste (11); memory aid (12); attempts (13 to 16); decision rule and handoff (17); key notes (18).
- **Entry knowledge:** chem-m8-c02 (see Scope); chem-m8-c01 (Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl, white); skills-s07 (validity, reliability, accuracy; word, feature, fix); chem-m7-p05 (spread against gap; a dry, pure product weighed correctly gives a correct result; over 100% means extra mass); Year 11 (moles, molar mass, percentage).
- **Learner-visible vocabulary (plain meaning at first use):** percentage recovery (the mass you collect as a percentage of the mass you should get); expected mass (screen: the mass the known sample should give); filtrate (the liquid that runs through the filter paper); washings (the wash water after it has run through the solid); digestion, desiccator, constant mass, tare (c02 recalls, screen chips only).
- **Attempt systems (unseen, every condition visible, HSC wording, original):** (A1) "To check a gravimetric method for sulfate, a student dissolved 0.350 g of pure, dry anhydrous sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄, in water, acidified the solution and added excess barium chloride solution. The barium sulfate precipitate was filtered, washed and dried to constant mass. Its mass was 0.561 g. (a) Calculate the percentage recovery of sulfate. (b) State what this result suggests about the accuracy of the method. M(Na₂SO₄) = 142.04 g mol⁻¹; M(BaSO₄) = 233.39 g mol⁻¹." Answer: 0.350 ÷ 142.04 = 2.46 × 10⁻³ mol; 1 : 1, so expected BaSO₄ = 2.464 × 10⁻³ × 233.39 = 0.5751 g; recovery = 0.561 ÷ 0.5751 × 100 = 97.5% (a guard digit kept; 0.561 ÷ 0.575 would give 97.6%); close to 100% but a little low, so the method loses a small amount of sulfate (slightly low, not exact). Crossed mistake: "0.561 ÷ 0.350 × 100 = 160%" (masses of different substances divided directly). (A2) "Two sets of students tested whether digestion improves the recovery of barium sulfate. Set C digested the precipitate for 30 minutes and washed it with three portions of warm distilled water. Set D filtered the precipitate straight away and washed it with one portion. Set C's mean recovery was higher than Set D's. Explain why this comparison is not valid, and propose a change that fixes the problem." Answer: two variables changed (digestion and washing); less washing can leave dried-on impurities that add mass (raising D's recovery), so the difference can't be put down to digestion alone; fix: wash both sets the same way, until a few drops of the washings stay clear with silver nitrate. Crossed mistake: "repeat each set more times" (reliability, not validity; every repeat still has unequal washing; s07's crossed habit).
- **Mistake scene:** none separate. "The known sample is pointless" (1, answered); "masses of different substances divided directly" (A1, crossed); "repeats fix a systematic loss" (9, said positively); "repeat it more times fixes a validity problem" (A2, crossed); "the dried precipitate is the hazard" (11, said positively: the dissolved reagent and filtrate are).

### 1. hook (a sample whose answer you know)
> Here's a strange first step for an analysis. Weigh out half a gram of dried potassium sulfate. You already know how much sulfate is in it, because you know its formula. So why analyse it? To test your **method**. If it recovers all the sulfate you put in, you can trust it on a real sample. If not, you'll see how much it **loses**.

### 4. prepare and precipitate

Once the solid settles, a drop more into the clear liquid on top should give no new cloudiness. Then the precipitation is **complete**.

### 9. reliable, and accurate?

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Test a gravimetric method on a pure, dry sample of known composition first. % recovery = mass collected ÷ expected mass × 100. Example: 0.500 g K₂SO₄ → expected 0.670 g BaSO₄.
2. Aim: does digestion improve recovery? Independent: digestion (none, or about 30 min warm); dependent: % recovery; controlled: sample mass, acid, BaCl₂ volume and concentration, filter paper, washing, drying to constant mass.
3. Method: dissolve; add dilute HCl; heat; add excess BaCl₂ slowly with stirring; check completeness with one more drop; digest; filter through pre-weighed fine paper; rinse and wash with warm water until the washings stay clear with AgNO₃; oven, desiccator, weigh to constant mass.
4. Results: digested 99.1%, not digested 94.3% (cloudy filtrate: fine crystals lost). Reliable: repeats agree, gap bigger than spread. Accurate: close to the known value. Repeats can't recover lost solid.
5. Safety: BaCl₂ toxic if swallowed (gloves, glasses, wash hands); dilute HCl irritant; AgNO₃ stains skin and harms waterways; tongs for hot apparatus; barium and silver waste into labelled heavy-metal containers, never the sink.
