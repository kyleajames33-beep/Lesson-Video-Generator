## Decisions

- **Main question:** *A river turns green. A few weeks later, fish are dying in it. Why would extra fertiliser end in a fish kill, and what should chemists measure to see it coming?* Heading on screen: "Why monitor water? Nutrients, blooms and the oxygen crash".
- **Sustained case: nutrients to an oxygen crash (pack question 7 answered).** "Which monitoring decision anchors this video?" The decision is early warning: which measurements show a water body heading toward an oxygen crash before the fish die. L09's chain carries the explanation (scenes 2 to 6) and L06's parameter set becomes the toolkit for that decision (scenes 7 to 10), so L06 is applied, not listed. The site's three-site table (L09) is the worked read (scene 11), as the pack suggested.
- **Australian hook, cited and dated (author note a).** The 1991 Barwon-Darling cyanobacterial bloom (November and December 1991, almost 1,000 km of river, during low flow with high phosphorus; Baker and Bowling 1996; the NSW Blue-Green Algae Task Force was set up in November 1991) and the 2018 to 2019 lower Darling fish deaths near Menindee (three events, December 2018 to January 2019, estimated at over one million fish; the independent Vertessy panel (2019) linked them to low flow, heat, algal blooms and stratified weir pools with low-oxygen bottom water). Both are on screen with year and source tags. The narration says "estimated" and "linked", not "caused by".
- **Cause before name.** Algae and plants need nitrogen and phosphorus; the one in shortest supply holds growth back ("limiting nutrient", plain meaning); extra supply releases the brake; the bloom; dead algae sink; bacteria decompose them and use dissolved oxygen as they respire; oxygen is used faster than it dissolves back in from the air; fish suffocate. Only then is the word "eutrophication" given (scene 5), with its plain meaning (nutrient enrichment that leads to too much growth and an oxygen crash). The site's "Common error: the algae themselves kill the fish directly" is the crossed chip in scene 5.
- **The delayed crash, and day and night.** Living algae add oxygen by day (photosynthesis, one clause) and use it at night (respiration, one clause), so a bloom can show high oxygen at noon and low oxygen at dawn (site L09 nuance). A coded 48-hour oxygen trace on the lineGraph shows the daily swing and then the fall after the bloom dies. This sets up c06 (a single reading is a snapshot) without teaching DO measurement.
- **Warm water (memory aid bank, Insight).** "Warm water holds less oxygen" (gases are less soluble when warm; think of a warm soft drink going flat) is said in scene 6 with the Year 11 callback, and used again for temperature as a physical parameter (scene 8). The bank's soft drink picture is kept because chem-m5 videos used a sealed soft drink (author note c).
- **Memory aid (bank, Mnemonic):** "Feed, bloom, die, decay, suffocate", shown after the chain is explained (scene 6), with the bank's own correction kept: the fish die mainly because bacteria use the oxygen, not because of the algae directly. Assumption checked (Memory aid check): it describes the oxygen-depletion route; toxin-producing blooms are a separate harm (grey tag).
- **Why monitor (dotpoint, "analyse the need").** Four reasons, said as causes, not a list: by the time fish die it's too late, so nutrients and oxygen give early warning; the source decides the fix, so samples must find the source (upstream against downstream); a standard needs a measured number; and after a fix, only measurement shows whether the water responded. The fourth is one clause in scene 12.
- **Parameter set (L06), with what each can't show.** Physical: temperature (warm water holds less oxygen), turbidity (cloudiness from suspended particles; blocks light; doesn't measure dissolved toxins or microbes). Chemical: pH, dissolved oxygen (named here; measured in c06), conductivity (dissolved ions, but not which ones), nitrate and phosphate. Biological: E. coli (a sign of faecal contamination: sewage or animal waste), algal cell counts under a microscope. Each parameter gets one plain "shows / can't show" pair on screen, spoken only for turbidity and conductivity (the site's own "what it cannot establish alone").
- **E. coli, not total coliforms (site correction 1; route review's L06 item).** The site's table calls coliform bacteria the "indicator of faecal contamination". Total coliforms include environmental bacteria; E. coli is the specific faecal indicator in the ADWG. The video says "E. coli" throughout.
- **Measuring nitrate and phosphate.** One sentence each: colourimetry (a reagent turns the ion into a coloured compound, read against standards as in c04; phosphate's molybdenum blue named on screen only) and ion chromatography (plain meaning: a column separates the ions, and each one is measured as it comes out). The site's claim that the syllabus names ion chromatography is logged (site correction 5) and not repeated. Nitrate methods that use cadmium reagents are not shown (author note e).
- **Nutrient mass basis (pack question 7: "Which nutrient mass basis will remain consistent?").** Every nutrient number in this video is mg/L **as the ion** (as nitrate, NO₃⁻; as phosphate, PO₄³⁻), matching the site's L09 table. Screen headers say "mg/L (as NO₃⁻)" and "mg/L (as PO₄³⁻)". The narration doesn't compare with nitrogen-basis guideline values. A small grey tag in scene 12, "check the basis: as NO₃⁻ or as N", is the only mention (author note f).
- **Standards depend on use (L06 card 4, g02 recall).** Drinking water against an ecosystem: the ADWG health value for nitrate is 50 mg/L as nitrate (set to protect bottle-fed babies), while far lower nutrient levels can feed a bloom in a river (ANZG and its ANZECC 2000 default trigger values are named on a grey tag, without a number spoken). This is the cause of "a value acceptable in one context may be concerning in another".
- **Treatment link (route review change 3).** One sentence in scene 12: monitoring tells a treatment plant what it must remove (algae and their toxins at a drinking water intake; nitrogen and phosphorus in sewage before it is released). No treatment process is named or shown.

### 5. die, decay, suffocate
> Then the bloom runs out of something, light or nutrients, and the algae die and sink. Bacteria decompose the dead algae, and as they respire they use up dissolved oxygen, the oxygen gas dissolved in the water. In this oxygen crash, bacteria do most of the oxygen-consuming **work**. The danger can continue after the bloom dies, which is why it's easy to **miss**.

### 12. why monitor?

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Nitrogen and phosphorus are needed nutrients. Growth is held back by the nutrient in shortest supply for what the algae need (the limiting nutrient; often phosphorus in fresh water). Sources: fertiliser runoff (diffuse), sewage effluent (point), urban stormwater.
