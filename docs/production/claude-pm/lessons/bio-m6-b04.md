### 2. what a chromosomal mutation is
> A **chromosomal mutation** changes the structure of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes. A point mutation, from the last video, changes one or a few bases in one gene. The large ones can even be seen down a microscope, while a point mutation can't.

### 4. deletion and duplication: content and dosage
> In a **deletion**, a segment is lost, along with every gene on it. A large deletion is often lethal, because the cell is left with only one copy of each of those genes. In a **duplication**, a segment appears twice. Its genes are all still there, but in extra copies, and the number of copies of a gene, called **gene dosage**, affects how much product the cell makes. In fruit flies, a duplicated stretch of the X chromosome narrows the eye into a bar.

### 5. inversion and translocation: order and position
> In an **inversion**, a segment breaks out, flips, and rejoins the same chromosome back to front. In a **translocation**, a segment moves onto a different, non-matching chromosome, often as a swap between two. If no DNA is lost or gained, every gene is still there, so a carrier is often healthy. But a break can land inside a gene and split it, or join two genes together. In one leukaemia, a single blood-forming cell gets a swap between chromosomes nine and twenty-two, joining two genes into one whose protein is always active, so white blood cells keep dividing.

### 7. nondisjunction: a change in number
> Now the other kind, a change in chromosome number. In meiosis, partner chromosomes normally separate, one to each new cell. Sometimes a pair fails to separate, which is called **nondisjunction**. One gamete then gets both copies of that chromosome, and another gets neither. If the gamete with the extra copy is fertilised, the zygote has three copies, called a **trisomy**. In humans, every cell of the new organism then has forty-seven chromosomes.

### 8. trisomy 21: normal genes, extra dosage
> That's how most cases of Down syndrome arise, with three copies of chromosome twenty-one. Every gene on it is normal, but there are three copies instead of two, so the cell makes roughly half as much again of hundreds of products. That's why the effects reach many parts of the body. It also helps explain why a baby with this trisomy can survive. Chromosome twenty-one is the smallest, with relatively few genes. An extra copy of most other numbered chromosomes stops development before birth.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Chromosomal mutation: a change in chromosome structure or number. It can affect many genes at once; large ones are visible on a karyotype.
- Structure: deletion (segment lost), duplication (segment repeated; extra gene dosage), inversion (segment reversed), translocation (segment moved to a non-matching chromosome). Causes: breaks rejoined wrongly (e.g. ionising radiation) and unequal crossing over.
- Balanced inversions and translocations keep every gene, so carriers are often healthy, but breakpoints can disrupt genes, and a carrier's gametes can be unbalanced.
- Number: nondisjunction in meiosis gives gametes with an extra or missing chromosome. Trisomy 21 (47 chromosomes) causes Down syndrome: normal genes, extra dosage.
- Point mutation: one gene's base sequence changes. Chromosomal mutation: the number, order or position of many genes changes. The effect depends on which genes are affected, not size alone.

Memory: a chromosome is a book. Deletion: chapter lost. Duplication: printed twice. Inversion: back to front. Translocation: moved to another book. Nondisjunction: fail to dis-join.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (19 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Griffiths et al., Introduction to Genetic Analysis; Strachan and Read, Human Molecular Genetics) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **1959 discovery.** Lejeune J, Gautier M, Turpin R (1959) "Étude des chromosomes somatiques de neuf enfants mongoliens", Comptes Rendus de l'Académie des Sciences 248:1721 to 1722: 47 chromosomes with an extra small acrocentric chromosome in cultured fibroblasts from nine children. Gautier did the cell culture and counting; her role was later acknowledged. The extra chromosome was numbered 21 later (Denver convention, 1960). "Dividing cells" (cultured cells in mitosis): correct. "Later named chromosome twenty-one": correct. Paris (Hôpital Trousseau): screen tag.
- **Human chromosome 21.** About 46.7 million base pairs, the smallest human autosome (chromosome 22 is about 50.8 million), with about 220 to 235 protein-coding genes (Ensembl, GRCh38). Screen tag (Revision 2) "about 200 to 300 genes", matching MedlinePlus Genetics; Ensembl GRCh38 annotates about 230 protein-coding genes; narration "relatively few genes" and "hundreds of products". Chromosome sizes for "hundreds of genes, sometimes thousands": chromosome 1 has about 2,000 protein-coding genes. Correct.
- **Down syndrome causes.** About 95 per cent free trisomy 21 from nondisjunction (about 90 per cent of these maternal, mostly meiosis I), about 3 to 4 per cent Robertsonian translocation, 1 to 2 per cent mosaicism (CDC; NHGRI; Hassold and Sherman reviews). "That's how most cases of Down syndrome arise": correct. "Roughly half as much again": trisomic genes are expressed on average about 1.5-fold, with gene-to-gene variation and some dosage compensation (Mao et al. 2003; reviews). "Roughly" is spoken; science check to confirm the wording.
- **Survivability.** Trisomy 21 is the commonest autosomal trisomy at birth; trisomies 13 and 18 also reach birth but with severe effects; most other autosomal trisomies (for example trisomy 16, the commonest in miscarriage) are lost before birth. Revision 2 wording (science check finding 1): "It also helps explain why a baby with this trisomy can survive" and "An extra copy of most other numbered chromosomes stops development before birth". The Revision 1 "most larger chromosomes" was unsafe because the X is large and an extra X (XXX, XXY) is survivable, thanks to X inactivation. "Numbered chromosomes" (autosomes, B9) excludes the sex chromosomes; "most other" allows for trisomies 13 and 18, which can reach birth. "Can survive" allows for the many trisomy 21 conceptions that miscarry.
- **Microscope visibility.** Standard G-banded karyotypes resolve changes of about 5 to 10 million base pairs; smaller changes need FISH or microarray; point mutations need sequencing. "The large ones can even be seen down a microscope, while a point mutation can't": correct.
- **Unequal crossing over.** Misaligned homologues crossing over give one duplication and one reciprocal deletion product (Sturtevant 1925, Bar; NAHR in human genomic disorders). Correct. Scene 3 geometry (Revision 2): to duplicate and lose two genes (C D), the partners must be out of step by two genes.
- **Bar eye.** *Drosophila* Bar is a tandem duplication of X chromosome band 16A (Bridges 1936; Muller, Prokofyeva-Belgovskaya and Kossikov 1936); double Bar (three copies of 16A on one X) gives a narrower eye with fewer facets than Bar; Sturtevant (1925) showed Bar reverts and double Bar arises by unequal crossing over. The narrowing is linked to the duplicated region's effect on the *BarH1* and *BarH2* genes, not a simple product dose (author note); the narration says only that copies narrow the eye, which is the observed dosage series. Position effect (author note): Sturtevant (1925) found that double Bar over a normal X is narrower than Bar over Bar, although both have four copies of 16A, so arrangement matters as well as number; the narration claims only the observed duplication effect and draws no "more product, narrower eye" link. Revision 2 cuts the double-Bar line and plate for length.
- **Deletion lethality.** Large deletions leave one copy (haploinsufficiency for many genes) and are often lethal; viable examples exist (for example 5p deletion, cri-du-chat). "Often lethal": correct. "From the partner chromosome": the remaining copy is on the homologue. Correct.
- **Balanced carriers.** Balanced reciprocal translocations occur in about 1 in 500 to 1 in 600 people, carriers are usually healthy; a breakpoint can disrupt a gene (a minority of carriers are affected). At meiosis the four chromosomes form a cross-shaped quadrivalent; alternate segregation gives balanced or normal gametes, adjacent segregation gives unbalanced gametes, causing recurrent miscarriage or offspring with partial trisomy or monosomy. Correct (site correction 5).
- **Inversions.** Many inversions are carried without effect (for example the common pericentric inversion of chromosome 9). Not named in narration. Correct.
- **CML.** The Philadelphia chromosome, t(9;22), joins *BCR* on chromosome 22 to *ABL1* on chromosome 9, making a BCR-ABL1 fusion tyrosine kinase that is constantly active, driving myeloid cell proliferation (Nowell and Hungerford 1960; Rowley 1973). It arises as a somatic mutation in a haematopoietic stem cell. "Joins two genes into one whose protein is always active, so white blood cells keep dividing": correct. "In one blood-forming cell" (screen tag).
- **Nondisjunction.** Failure of homologues (meiosis I) or sister chromatids (meiosis II) to separate; gametes n + 1 and n - 1; fertilisation with a normal gamete gives trisomy (2n + 1 = 47 in humans) or monosomy (45). In the scene 7 meiosis I case, all four gametes are abnormal (two n + 1, two n - 1), as drawn. Correct.
- **Polyploidy tag.** Bread wheat (*Triticum aestivum*) is hexaploid (six sets, 2n = 6x = 42). Correct.
- **Attempt 2 facts.** Sickle cell: HBB codon 6 substitution, Glu to Val, with two copies haemoglobin polymerises at low oxygen (b03, B7). Trisomy 21 as above. Correct.
