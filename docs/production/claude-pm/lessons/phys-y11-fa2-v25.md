### 2. light bends towards the thick part
> First, what does curved glass do to light? Slice a lens into thin strips. The middle strip has parallel faces, like the glass block, so a ray goes straight through. Strips further out have faces that tilt towards each other, like a wedge. Light slows as it enters the wedge and bends towards the normal. It speeds up as it leaves and bends away from the normal. Both bends turn the ray the same way, towards the thick end of the **wedge**. So for a glass lens in air, light bends towards the thick part.

Visual: camera continue: v24's convex lens outline grows into a large coded converging lens cross-section, centre left (`LensStrips`); its two meeting rays fade. On "Slice" seven horizontal strip lines cut across it. On "middle strip" the centre strip outlines as a slab with a tiny glass block icon tag "like v20's block"; a ray passes straight through it. On "Strips further out" the top strip lifts out and enlarges at the right as a wedge (thick end towards the lens's centre, that is, downward). On "enters" `RayRefract` (n = 1.50) draws the ray into the wedge with its dashed normal and the ray bending towards it; on "leaves" the second normal and the bend away from it. On "same way" two amber turn arcs on the ray, both turning towards the thick end; on "wedge" the thick end glows. On the last sentence the handwritten line "light bends towards the thick part" writes under the lens.

### 3. converging lens: focal point and focal length
> In a lens that's thicker in the middle, every outer strip is a wedge with its thick end pointing in. So outer rays bend inwards, and the further out a ray hits, the steeper its wedge, and the more it **bends**. Parallel rays all meet at one point, the focal point, F. The distance from the centre of the lens to F is the focal length. It's a converging lens: it brings rays together. A more curved lens has steeper wedges, so it bends more, and its focal length is **shorter**.

Visual: the wedge slides back into the lens; the strips close. `ThinLensRays`: five parallel rays from the left (tag "thin lens drawing", grey, first time only). On "bend down" the top ray bends down; on "bend up" the bottom ray bends up; on "bends" the outer rays' turn arcs show larger than the inner ones'. The loose converging lens plate (`phys-y11-lens-converging-v1.png`) appears small in the right column with the label "converging lens". On "more curved" the coded lens bulges more; on "shorter" F slides towards the lens and the bracket shortens (readout "f shorter").

### 4. diverging lens: a virtual focal point
> Now flip the shape. A lens that's thinner in the middle has wedges with their thick ends pointing out. So the rays bend outwards. It's a diverging lens. Trace the spreading rays backwards, and they seem to come from one point on the same side as the incoming light. No light actually passes through that point, so it's a virtual focal **point**. A screen behind this lens just shows a growing patch of light.

Visual: the coded lens morphs to a diverging cross-section (thin centre, thick rims); the strips flash once with their wedges flipped (thick ends outward). On "spread apart" the five parallel rays bend outward. Gloss "diverging: spreads rays apart". On "point" a gloss "virtual: the rays only seem to come from here". ### 5. the eye: a converging system with a screen
> Your eye is a converging system with a screen at the back. Light first meets the cornea, the clear curved front, which does about two thirds of the bending. The lens inside adds the rest. The screen is the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back. Light from a distant object arrives as nearly parallel rays, and in a healthy, relaxed eye, they meet exactly on the **retina**.

### 6. why a missed focus looks blurred
> Why does a wrong focus look blurry? Every point on an object sends out its own set of rays. If they meet on the retina, that point lands as a point. If they meet in front of the retina, or behind it, they arrive as a small patch. Thousands of overlapping patches make a blurred **picture**.

Visual: camera continue on the eye. A single point source "one point on the object" sends a narrow cone into the eye; it focuses on the retina as a dot. On "in front of" the coded retina line slides back a little (the focus now in front): the cone crosses and spreads again, and a soft gold disc (`BlurPatch`) shows where it meets the retina line (inset: the disc seen face on). On "behind it" the retina slides forward of the focus: a disc again. On "picture" three object points side by side make three overlapping discs in the face-on inset, smearing into a blur beside a sharp three-dot version (tags "focused", "missed focus"). The retina returns to its place.

### 7. prediction: a page up close
> Now bring the object close, like a page twenty-five centimetres away. Its rays reach the eye already spreading. If the eye changed nothing, where would they meet? In front of the retina, on it, or **behind** it?

Visual: the parallel rays fade; a point source "page, 25 cm" sits close on the left; its rays reach the cornea spreading (a widening cone). The eye's lens stays as in scene 5. On "behind" chips "in front?", "on it?", "behind?" and a 3-second countdown ring.

### 8. near light needs more bending
> Behind it. Spreading rays need more bending to come together, so with the same bending, they meet further back. A healthy eye fixes this itself. Tiny muscles let the lens inside become fatter and more curved, so it bends more, and the focus moves forward onto the **retina**. But the lens can only change so much.

### 10. correcting short sight: a diverging lens

## Teaching checks
