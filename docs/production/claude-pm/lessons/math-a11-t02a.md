### 1. hook: how high is the cliff?
> Here's the yacht, forty metres out from the foot of the cliff. Looking up from the water to the clifftop, the line of sight rises fifty-two degrees above the horizontal. That's the angle of elevation, measured up from the horizontal to where you're looking. The cliff is vertical and the sea is flat, so there's a right angle at the foot. Now choose the ratio from the two sides involved, the one you know and the one you want. We know the side next to the angle, forty metres, and we want the side opposite it, the height. Opposite and adjacent means **tangent**. So tangent fifty-two degrees is h over forty. h is on top, so multiply both sides by forty: h is forty times tangent fifty-two degrees, about fifty-one point two **metres**. So how do angles of elevation and depression find heights and **distances**?

### 2. drifting back: finding an angle
> Now the yacht drifts another twenty-five metres out. What's the new angle of elevation? The height hasn't changed, and the side next to the angle is now the whole sixty-five metres, not twenty-five. Keep the unrounded height in your calculator, fifty-one point one nine seven and so on. Then tangent of the angle is that height over sixty-five, about zero point seven eight **eight**. To turn a ratio back into an angle, use inverse tangent. It gives about thirty-eight point two degrees, so thirty-eight **degrees**. That makes sense, because from further away the clifftop looks lower. Using twenty-five metres as the adjacent side is the **slip**. It gives an angle bigger than fifty-two degrees, from further away.

### 3. looking down: alternate angles
> Now look down from the clifftop. The angle of depression is measured down from the horizontal at your eye. Here's the useful part. The horizontal at the top is parallel to the sea, and the line of sight cuts across both. So the angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from below. They're alternate angles, the Z-shaped pair, and they're always **equal**. So looking down at the yacht from up here, the angle of depression is thirty-eight **degrees** too.

Model answer: the angle of elevation from the buoy is also 12° (alternate angles). tan 12° = 30/d, so d = 30/tan 12° ≈ 141.1, about 141 m. Classic mistake (crossed): "30 tan 12° ≈ 6 m".

**Attempt 2 (scene 8; original, HSC style, 2 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A vertical tree is 15 m tall. From a point on level ground 40 m from its foot, find the angle of elevation of the top of the tree, to the nearest degree."
Model answer: the height is opposite the angle and 40 m is adjacent, so tan θ = 15/40 = 0.375 and θ = tan⁻¹(0.375) ≈ 20.6°, about 21°. Classic mistake (crossed): "tan⁻¹(40/15) ≈ 69°" (the angle at the top of the tree).

## Memory aid check

- **"Elevation looks up, depression looks down: both from the horizontal."** Original wording of a standard rule. Meaning, as said in scene 5: an angle of elevation is measured upward from a horizontal line at the observer, and an angle of depression downward from a horizontal line at the observer; neither is measured from the vertical. Assumptions checked: the observer's horizontal and the ground or sea are parallel (true for level ground and the sea over short distances), which is what makes the two angles equal. It helps a new case: from a 30 m cliff, a depression of 12° is also the elevation at the buoy (A1).
- **Second line: "Unknown on top: multiply. On the bottom: divide."** Meaning: in a ratio equation such as tan 52° = h/40 or tan 27° = 18/d, an unknown in the numerator is found by multiplying both sides by the denominator, and an unknown in the denominator by multiplying by it and then dividing by the ratio. Assumptions: one unknown, a non-zero ratio. Introduced after scenes 1 and 4, which show both.
