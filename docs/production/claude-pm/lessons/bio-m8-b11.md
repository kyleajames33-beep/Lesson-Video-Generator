### 4. judge the method: four steps
> To judge any method, use four steps: design, strength, limitation, judgement. The design is case-control. Its strength is speed, and matching made age and sex more comparable. Its limitation is that smoking was remembered, not recorded at the time. That opens the door to **bias**, a flaw in the method that pushes results one way, which more data can't cancel. The judgement: a strong association, but not yet proof of cause.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Observational studies watch what people already do; intervention studies assign it. In a randomised controlled trial, chance decides the groups, which helps balance other differences. Harmful exposures can't ethically be assigned.
- Case-control: start with the disease, look back at exposure (quick, good for less common diseases; recall bias). Cohort: record exposure, follow forward (exposure recorded before the outcome; slow, costly).
- Bias: a flaw in the method that pushes results one way. Confounder: a factor linked to both the exposure and the disease.
- Evaluate a method: design, strength, limitation, judgement (association or cause?). Cause is supported by strength, dose-response, exposure first, consistency, a mechanism and lower risk when the exposure is removed.
- Evaluate a benefit: finding → decision → action reaches people → outcome checked. Doll and Hill: smoking identified as a changeable risk factor → tobacco control → smoking and lung cancer rates fell, slowly and unequally.

Memory: Cohort goes forward; case-control looks back.

Strength: it is quick and efficient for a less common disease, and matching reduced differences in age and sex between the groups; using two controls per case adds precision (1 mark).
