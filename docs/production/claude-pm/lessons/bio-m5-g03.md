### 1. hook (seventy-two in a hundred)
> A collaborative study from several countries followed nearly 10,000 women who carry a harmful version of a gene called BRCA1 or BRCA2. Among the BRCA1 carriers, about 72 in every 100 were estimated to get breast cancer by age 80. It's exactly the kind of large shared project we met in the last video. So does carrying that variant mean a woman will get breast cancer? And can population genetics predict anything with real accuracy?

### 2. a group proportion, not one person's future
> Here's what that number really says. Picture 100 carriers as 100 dots. About 72 would develop breast cancer by 80, and about 28 wouldn't. The study predicts that proportion well, because it was measured across thousands of people. Follow another large group of carriers, and you'd expect close to the same proportion again. That's a risk pattern, a trend in how likely something is across a group. What it can't say is which dots are which. For one woman, it's a probability, not a forecast.

### 3. why the gene doesn't decide on its own
> So why can't the gene decide on its own? BRCA1 makes a protein that helps repair damaged DNA. A carrier inherits one faulty copy, but her other copy still works. Cancer usually starts only if a cell also loses that working copy, and whether that happens depends on chance damage, other genes and other factors, like hormones and age. So the variant raises the risk. It doesn't fix the outcome.

### 4. probability isn't certainty
> So a higher probability isn't a certain outcome. And it works the other way too. So testing negative for a BRCA variant isn't a guarantee either. Genetic risk is not genetic destiny.

### 5. a population prediction that works
> Population predictions can be very accurate, though. Here's one that health services use. Cystic fibrosis is recessive, so carriers look healthy and can't be counted by looking. But affected babies can be counted. Say one baby in 2,500 is affected. An affected baby needs the allele from both parents, so the allele's frequency times itself is one in 2,500. Take the square root, and the allele's frequency is 0.02, so the normal allele's is 0.98. Carriers have one of each, and there are two ways to get that, from the mother or the father. So carriers are two times 0.98 times 0.02, about one person in 25. This is the Hardy-Weinberg principle, and it shows how a rare disease can have common carriers.

### 6. when population predictions hold
> But that estimate only holds if the population is large, mates at random, and isn't changed by selection, migration or new mutations. Real populations rarely meet all of these, and that's useful too. If the real numbers differ from the prediction, something is acting on the population. For example, if DNA tests give the allele's frequency, and more babies are affected than it predicts, relatives may be having children together. In a large population over a short time, predictions come close. In a small population, chance can push allele frequencies either way, so long-range predictions there fail.

### 7. risk is shared with relatives
> Genetic risk is also shared within families. If a woman carries a BRCA1 variant, each of her children has a one in two chance of inheriting it, and so does each of her brothers and sisters, because one of her parents almost certainly carries it too. So genetic data are never only about one person. In 2003, Iceland's Supreme Court upheld a woman's claim that her late father's health records shouldn't go into a planned national database, because data about him could reveal information about her.

### 8. strong and weak wording
> So in an exam, match your claim to your evidence. Strong answers say the data suggest a trend, indicate increased risk, or make an outcome more likely in this population. Weak answers say the data prove every person will be affected, guarantee an outcome, or remove all uncertainty. So don't write that carriers will get the disease. Write that carriers are more likely to get it. Be precise and cautious at the same time.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Population data predict group patterns well (risk patterns, carrier frequencies, allele distributions), not one person's outcome. Example: about 72 in 100 BRCA1 carriers are estimated to develop breast cancer by age 80.
- A variant raises risk without guaranteeing the phenotype (losing the working copy is a common route; other genes, other factors, chance). Higher probability is not certainty; testing negative is not a guarantee.
- Hardy-Weinberg (recessive condition): affected = q squared, so q = square root; p = 1 minus q; carriers = 2pq. E.g. 1 in 2,500 affected gives carriers about 1 in 25.
- It holds for large, randomly mating populations with no selection, migration or mutation. A gap between prediction and data means something is acting. Small populations: chance makes long-range prediction fail.
- Genetic results inform relatives (each child: 1 in 2). Use cautious wording: "suggests", "indicates increased risk", "more likely".

2. (10c, calculate and explain; bank method; site Explain and Evaluate items.) A recessive condition affects 1 in 10,000 babies. (a) Assuming Hardy-Weinberg, estimate the carrier frequency. (b) Explain why the estimate would be less reliable in a small, isolated village.
   Model: (a) q squared = 1/10,000 = 0.0001, so q = 0.01; p = 1 minus 0.01 = 0.99; carriers = 2pq = 2 x 0.99 x 0.01 = 0.0198, about 1 in 50. Classic mistake crossed out: "carriers = 1 in 10,000" (that is the affected frequency, q squared). Other wrong chains named in the review: q = 1/10,000 without the square root (gives 1 in 5,000 carriers); carriers = q only (forgets the two ways and p).
