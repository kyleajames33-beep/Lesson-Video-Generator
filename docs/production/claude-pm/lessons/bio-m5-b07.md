### 2. what proteins do
> Most of a cell's jobs are done by proteins. **Enzymes** speed up reactions. **Transport** proteins carry things, like haemoglobin carrying oxygen in red blood cells. **Structural** proteins give strength, like the keratin in your hair. **Receptors** on a cell's surface pick up signals, and **antibodies** grab onto germs. Every one of these is a polypeptide chain, folded into its own shape.

### 3. why a chain folds
> So why does a chain fold into one particular shape? Because the twenty amino acids are different. Some avoid water, so they tuck into the middle of the chain, away from the watery cell. Some carry a charge, and opposite charges attract. And two amino acids called **cysteine** can lock together with a strong link called a **disulfide bond**. The order of amino acids decides which parts end up next to each other, so it decides the shape.

### 4. shape decides the job
> Shape matters because proteins work by **fitting**. An enzyme's substrate fits into a pocket, its active site, like a key in a lock, though the protein can flex a little as it binds. Change one amino acid in that pocket, and the shape can change so the substrate no longer fits. But not every change matters. A swap far from the pocket, or to a similar amino acid, may leave the shape working. And many base changes don't change the amino acid at all.

### 5b. from the protein to the trait
> Glutamate carries a charge, but valine avoids water. The fibres bend the red blood cells into a **sickle** shape. Sickle cells are stiff, so they block small blood vessels and cause pain, and they break down quickly, so there are too few red cells. That whole pattern is the trait, sickle cell disease.

### 6. no shortcut: genotype to phenotype
> Notice how many steps that took. Genes don't become traits directly. A gene codes for a polypeptide, which folds into a protein, which changes what a cell does, and that shows up as a trait. Your set of alleles is your **genotype**. The traits you can observe, from a cell's shape to the colour of a flower, are your **phenotype**. And most traits involve many proteins, so many genes, at once.

### 9. why polypeptide synthesis matters
> That's why making polypeptides matters so much. Almost everything a cell does depends on its proteins, and every protein is made by transcription and translation. Your cells all carry the same genes, but each kind of cell makes its own set of proteins, which is why a red blood cell and a muscle cell look and work so differently. If the sequence is wrong, or the protein isn't made, the job doesn't get done.

### 11a. attempt one (the Himalayan rabbit)

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Proteins do most of a cell's jobs: enzymes, transport (haemoglobin), structure (keratin), receptors, antibodies.
- The amino acid order sets the fold (water-avoiding amino acids tuck in, charges attract, cysteines form disulfide bonds), and the shape sets the job. Levels: 1 order, 2 local coils, 3 whole fold, 4 more than one chain.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (17 Oct) against the standard genetic code (ported `codonToAA`), standard texts (Campbell Biology; Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell), OMIM and the sources named.
- **Codons (standard code):** GAG Glu, GUG Val, GAA Glu, CAU His, CAC His, CCU Pro, GUA Val. All checked against the standard table.
- **Sickle cell.** HBB codon 6 GAG to GTG on the coding strand (mRNA GUG), Glu6Val in the traditional numbering of the mature beta chain (HGVS p.Glu7Val counts the initiator methionine, which is removed; author note). Ingram identified the single amino acid difference (1956 to 1957). The adult beta chain has 146 amino acids; haemoglobin A is two alpha and two beta chains. The first seven residues of mature beta globin are Val-His-Leu-Thr-Pro-Glu-Glu (checked; residue 6 is Glu). Deoxygenated HbS polymerises because Val6 binds a hydrophobic pocket (involving Phe85 and Leu88) on a neighbouring molecule; polymer fibres distort and stiffen the cell; sickled cells occlude small vessels (pain crises) and are destroyed early (lifespan about 10 to 20 days against about 120), causing anaemia. Sickle cell disease (HbSS) needs two copies; carriers (HbAS) are usually healthy (kept in learner copy as "two copies" because its omission would teach a wrong idea). "Glutamate carries a charge, valine avoids water": glutamate side chain is negatively charged at body pH; valine is nonpolar. Correct.
- **Folding.** Hydrophobic side chains cluster in the interior in water; ionic attractions and hydrogen bonds stabilise the fold; disulfide bonds form between cysteine side chains (mostly in secreted and cell-surface proteins). "The order decides the shape": Anfinsen's principle (sequence determines native structure; some proteins need chaperones to fold, author note).
- **Four levels.** Primary sequence; secondary (alpha helix, beta sheet) by backbone hydrogen bonds; tertiary 3D fold of one chain; quaternary several chains (haemoglobin four). Matches the bank.
- **Protein jobs.** Enzymes catalyse; haemoglobin transports oxygen; keratin is the main structural protein of hair; actin and myosin (in smooth muscle too) cause contraction; receptors bind signals; antibodies bind antigens. Correct. "Most of a cell's jobs are done by proteins": standard textbook phrasing (RNAs and other molecules also have roles, author note).
- **Codon redundancy.** 61 sense codons for 20 amino acids; substitutions in third positions are often synonymous. "Many base changes don't change the amino acid": correct.
- **Hydrangea.** In *Hydrangea macrophylla*, blue sepal colour needs aluminium ions complexed with the anthocyanin delphinidin-3-glucoside (with co-pigments); aluminium is soluble and taken up in acidic soils (about pH below 5.5) and much less available in neutral to alkaline soils, where the sepals are pink (Yoshida et al., reviews in Phytochemistry and Plant and Cell Physiology). The pigment is red to pink without aluminium: correct. "Petals": the showy parts are sepals botanically. Revision 2 (review decision): learner copy says "flowers", which is true and needs no botany; "sepals" stays in this note. White cultivars do not change (no pigment); the plates are coloured cultivars.
- **Height.** Polygenic (thousands of variants); heritability about 0.8 in well-nourished populations; childhood undernutrition causes stunting (WHO). "Children who don't get enough food don't reach the height their genes allow": correct at population level.
- **ABO.** Set by alleles at one gene; not changed by diet or climate. Rare exceptions (bone marrow transplant, acquired B antigen in some infections) are author notes.
- **Himalayan rabbit.** The Himalayan allele (c^h) of the tyrosinase gene gives a temperature-sensitive enzyme that makes melanin only in cooler skin; extremities are cooler; classic shaving and cooling experiments (early twentieth century; Schultz, Iljin) show regrown fur under cold is dark. Pigment is deposited as hair grows, so the patch must regrow. Correct. No temperature number is used.
- **Snapdragon item.** Hypothetical changes in a real kind of gene (red anthocyanin pigment in *Antirrhinum* needs pathway enzymes, for example dihydroflavonol 4-reductase encoded by *pallida*); codon numbers are not given, so nothing is falsely specific. "Two copies" keeps the recessive loss-of-function condition. A histidine to proline change in an active site would very plausibly disrupt it (proline also disrupts local backbone structure); learner copy says the shape "changes" and the enzyme "can't hold its substrate", which is the item's stated premise.
- **NESA feedback.** 2022 Q31(b): link mutation through protein structure and function to the effect; avoid a generic DNA-to-trait arrow (pack). 2023 Q25(b): use the supplied codon chart directly. Both applied.
