### 2. what are the major issues? Two sources, three tests
> Start by working out the issues, using two sources, because each is blind to something. Local data compares the community with the state, splits it by group, and shows conditions, service use and the causes, like income, housing and transport. But it can't see why, or small groups that get averaged away. The community's own account sees the bus that stops at six, the building people won't enter, and what's been tried before and failed. But it misses slow trends, conditions people don't know they have, and issues nobody wants to raise. So use both. Where they disagree, that's a finding in itself. Then shortlist with three tests. How much harm does it cause? How unequally is it spread within the community? And can anything be changed at the level you can act? The second test often changes the priority.

### 4. how do you know it may be effective?
> The syllabus asks how you know strategies may be effective, before they run. So it's about the reasoning. Six tests. One, is there a stated mechanism, step by step? Without one, it's just a hope. Two, has that mechanism worked elsewhere, allowing for what was local? Three, does it act where the problem is? If the problem is cost, education won't touch it. Four, will it reach the people carrying the burden, or just the already engaged? A better average can hide a wider gap. Five, is it funded and staffed past the first cycle? And six, are the measures set in advance: a **baseline**, **process measures** for now, **outcome measures** for later, split by group. Match them to the **lag time**, the delay before health changes. Cheaper medicines can show up in months. School completion won't show in adult diabetes for twenty years. Judge that on a two-year health outcome, and you'll wrongly call it a failure.

### 5. six ways a good strategy fails
> Now be critical. Well-meant strategies fail in six common ways. They treat a symptom, not the cause, like healthy eating classes where fresh food is unaffordable and forty minutes away. They reach the wrong people, because voluntary programs are taken up first by those already advantaged. They end too soon, with two-year funding for a twenty-year problem. They're designed without the community, and nobody uses them. They measure nothing, or demand outcomes too early. And they depend on one person, so they stop when that person leaves. So before any strategy starts, ask one question. What would have to be true for this to fail? The answer tells you what to design against.
