### 6. dose
> So what decides how much risk? First, **dose**: how much of the agent gets in, over how long. For smoking, it's counted in **pack-years**, packs per day multiplied by years smoked. One pack a day for twenty years is twenty pack-years. For cancer, think of each exposure as a ticket in a lottery you don't want to win. Most tickets lose, because the damage is repaired. But more tickets mean more chances that one cell collects several unlucky changes.

### 7. latency, and the asbestos answer
> Second, time. Those changes take years to build up, so there's usually a long gap between exposure and diagnosis, called **latency**. Asbestos shows it clearly. Its fibres are long and thin, and once breathed in, they can lodge in the lining around the lungs. Immune cells called macrophages try to swallow them, can't, and keep releasing reactive chemicals that damage nearby cells and their DNA. That slow damage can take twenty to fifty years to become mesothelioma, which is why the 2003 ban hasn't stopped new cases yet.

### 8. risk, not certainty
> So why do many heavy smokers never get lung cancer, while some people who've never smoked do? Because exposure raises the probability, not the certainty. DNA repair works better in some people than others. The immune system destroys some damaged cells. And which cell collects which mutation is partly chance. Never-smokers can be exposed in other ways too, through second-hand smoke, radon gas or polluted air. ### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
