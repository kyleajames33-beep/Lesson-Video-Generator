### 1. hook (same speed, wildly different waves)
> Radio waves, light and X-rays are all electromagnetic waves, travelling at c. But look how different they are. A radio wave can be longer than a football field. A gamma ray is smaller than an atom. If they all travel at the same speed, here's a question. A wave with a higher frequency: does it have a longer wavelength, or a **shorter** one?

Visual: opens on v13's last frame (three lanes: radio mast, filament lamp, "X-ray tube" chip; level fronts; "all at c"; the chips and the "Next" chip). The chip becomes the heading: "The electromagnetic spectrum". On "football field" a scale chip "can be longer than 100 m" pins to the top lane's wavelength bracket; on "smaller than an atom" a fourth thin lane slides in at the bottom ("gamma rays") with crests too close to separate, and a magnifier ring with a coded atom circle bigger than one wavelength (tag "not to scale"). On "shorter" chips "longer?" and "shorter?" and a 3-second countdown ring.

### 2. one family, in order of frequency
> These are all one family, called the electromagnetic spectrum. The only thing that changes along it is the frequency. At the low frequency end are radio waves. Then come microwaves, infrared, and visible light, from red to violet. Then ultraviolet, X-rays, and at the high frequency end, gamma **rays**. Visible light, everything our eyes can detect, is just a thin slice in the **middle**.

Visual: the four lanes slide together and lay their waves along one horizontal band: radio at the far left, light in the middle, X-rays and gamma at the right (lanes-to-strip morph); three more regions fill in as they're named; each region's name writes on its band (`SpectrumStrip`, no scales yet; gloss "electromagnetic spectrum: the whole family of electromagnetic waves, in order of frequency"). A thin coded wave runs along the strip, its crests getting closer from left to right (tag "not to scale"). An arrow under the strip: "frequency increases →". On "middle" the visible band opens into a rainbow, red at its left edge and violet at its right, and a bracket "visible: a thin slice".

### 3. why higher frequency means shorter wavelength
> Here's why the wavelength shrinks. Every part of the spectrum travels at c. Picture two light waves, side by side, at the same speed. Double the frequency of one, so twice as many crests pass each second. They can't go any faster, so they have to be packed closer together, at half the spacing. That's c equals f lambda, with c fixed. Green light, at five point seven times ten to the fourteen hertz, has a wavelength of c over f, five point three times ten to the minus seven metres. That's five hundred and thirty **nanometres**.

Visual: board change: two lanes stacked (`EMWave3D` side view, one clock, tag "slowed down"), leading fronts level; a finish line at the right with a crest counter on each lane. On "double the frequency" the lower lane's crests pack to half the spacing; its counter counts twice as fast; navy λ brackets on both, the lower one half as long. Readout under each lane: "f × λ = c" with the same value. Equation strip "c = fλ (c fixed)" (grey "v05, v10: the same reasoning for sound"). On the green sentence a row: "λ = c / f = 3.0 × 10⁸ / 5.7 × 10¹⁴ = 5.3 × 10⁻⁷ m"; on "nanometres" "= 530 nm" with gloss "nm, nanometre: a billionth of a metre" and the pointer dropping onto the green part of the strip.

### 4. reading a spectrum diagram
> Real spectrum diagrams pack all of this onto one strip, so here's how to read one. First, the scales jump in powers of ten. Each mark is ten times the one before. Second, frequency rises one way, and wavelength falls the same way, so the two scales run in opposite **directions**. Third, pick any point. Its frequency times its wavelength is always c. At ten to the ten hertz, we're in the microwaves, and the wavelength is three times ten to the minus two metres, three **centimetres**. And the edges between regions are only approximate.

Visual: the strip returns across the upper third and gains its scales: frequency above, "10⁴ Hz" to "10²² Hz", one mark per power of ten; on "ten times" two neighbouring marks pulse with a "× 10" hop between them (gloss "powers of ten: each mark ten times the last"). On "directions" the wavelength scale draws underneath, "3 × 10⁴ m" at the left to "3 × 10⁻¹⁴ m" at the right, with arrows "frequency →" above and "← wavelength" below. On "Third" a pointer slides along the strip with live readouts "f", "λ" and "f × λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹" (the last never changes). On "centimetres" the pointer stops at 10¹⁰ Hz in the microwave band: "f = 1 × 10¹⁰ Hz, λ = 3 × 10⁻² m = 3 cm". On "approximate" the region edges soften and X-ray and gamma visibly overlap (tag "edges are approximate").

### 5. the energy of a photon: E = h f
> Now the energy. Light doesn't hand over its energy smoothly. It arrives in tiny packets, called photons. Each photon's energy is E equals h f, where h is Planck's constant, a tiny fixed number, six point six three times ten to the minus thirty-four joule seconds. Experiments show this, and you'll meet the evidence in Year 12. So the energy of each photon is proportional to its frequency. A violet photon has a higher frequency than a red one, so it carries more **energy**. Radio photons carry tiny amounts. X-ray photons carry huge **amounts**.
