### 1. hook (is ln x its own derivative?)
> Last video, our ratio points traced this curve, y equals the natural log of x. Here's a tempting guess. E to the x is its own derivative, and the natural log undoes e to the x. So is the natural log its own derivative too? Look at x equals a **half**. The curve is below the axis there, so the natural log of a half is negative. But the curve is still **climbing**. A climbing curve has a positive gradient, so the guess fails. Then what is the gradient of this curve, and **why**?

### 2. measure the gradient
> Measure it, starting at x equals one, where the curve crosses the axis. Slide a second point in from the right, and the secant's slope settles: point eight one, point nine five, point nine nine five. The tangent's slope is **one**. Now move along. ### 7. the chain rule proof
> Now the proof the syllabus asks for, with the chain rule. For every positive x, e to the natural log of x equals x, because the natural log is the power you raise e to, to get **x**. Differentiate both sides. On the right, x has derivative **one**. On the left, the power is an inside part, the natural log of x. The chain rule says copy e to that power, then multiply by the inside part's derivative, the very gradient we're **after**. So e to the natural log of x, times the gradient, is one. But e to the natural log of x is just **x**. So x times the gradient is one, the pattern our readout showed. Divide by x, which is fine because x is positive. The derivative of the natural log of x is one over **x**. Not one over the natural log of x, which doesn't even exist at x equals one.

- Worked 1: d/dx ln(3x) = 1/x (also 3/(3x)); tangent at (2, ln 6): y = (x − 2)/2 + ln 6; at k: point (2, ln 2k), slope 0.5 for all k > 0.
- Worked 2: d/dx(3 ln x) = 3/x; at 2: 1.5; tangent y = 1.5(x − 2) + 3 ln 2 passes (4, 5.079442) (window top 6).

## Memory aid check
