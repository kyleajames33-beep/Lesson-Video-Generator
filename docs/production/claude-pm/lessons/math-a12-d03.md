- **Anchor: a new app's users, and "how long until?" (painted phone and stopwatch plates).** The natural log answers "how long until" questions, so the anchor is the inverse question. A new app has 1,000 users, and each user brings in one new user a week, so after x weeks it has eˣ thousand users (growth rate = users, as in d01, now with people's phones instead of cells). Turn the question around: the time to reach x thousand users is ln x weeks. The anchor carries the meaning of every quantity in the lesson:
  - on y = eˣ: x is weeks, the height is users (thousands), the slope is new users per week, and slope = height;
  - on y = ln x: x is users (thousands), the height is weeks, and the slope is weeks per extra thousand users;
  - the reflection in y = x swaps the two questions ("how many after this long?" and "how long until this many?"), so the tangent triangle "across 1 week, up 2 thousand users" becomes "rise 1 week, run 2 thousand users": half a week per thousand. That is why the slope of ln x is 1/x: with x thousand users, new users arrive at x thousand a week, so the next thousand takes about 1/x of a week.
  The anchor appears in scene 1 (narrated), labels the reflection pairs and the triangle legs in scenes 4 and 5 (screen tags), and returns, narrated, when the maths answers the question in scene 6 ("with four thousand users ... the next thousand takes about a quarter of a week").
- **Why this anchor.** "How long until?" is the everyday question a logarithm answers, and app users growing by referral is a familiar population of people (owner list: phones and apps; populations of users). It explains the cause, not just the rule: more users means faster arrivals, so less time per thousand. It differs from d02 (money) and d04 (music and sound). No biology.
- **Idealising assumptions (author notes, not learner copy).** (i) Every user brings in exactly one new user a week on average, at a steady rate, with no one leaving; real referral growth slows as the market fills. (iv) Negative times (ln x for x < 1) mean before the count started: the app had 500 users about 0.69 weeks earlier.

- **Site corrections (new; quoted from site lesson 3, read 1 Nov; for `../site-corrections-maths-a12.md`):**

### 7. the chain rule proof
> Now the proof the syllabus asks for, with the chain rule. For every positive x, e to the natural log of x equals x, because the natural log is the power you raise e to, to get **x**. Differentiate both sides. On the right, x has derivative **one**. On the left, the power is an inside part, the natural log of x. The chain rule says copy e to that power, then multiply by the inside part's derivative, the very gradient we're **after**. So e to the natural log of x, times the gradient, is one. But e to the natural log of x is just **x**. So x times the gradient is one, the pattern our readout showed. Divide by x, which is fine because x is positive. The derivative of the natural log of x is one over **x**. Not one over the natural log of x, which doesn't even exist at x equals one.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why ln x is not its own derivative: it climbs where it is negative (1). Why the gradient is 1/x: ln x is e^x reflected in y = x; the reflection swaps each tangent's run and rise, so the slope at the matching point becomes its reciprocal; on e^x the slope equals the height, and the reflection turns that height into ln x's x (4 to 6). Why the proof works: e^(ln x) = x, and the chain rule makes the unknown slope the inner derivative; e^(ln x) is x, so x × slope = 1 (7). Why x > 0: e^x is always positive, so its reflection only covers positive x (8). Why ln(3x) has the same derivative: ln 3 is a constant, so the curve only shifts vertically (9). Why 3 ln x has 3/x: an upward stretch by 3 multiplies every rise (10).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** "d/dx(ln x) = ln x" (1, by the picture); "1/ln x" (7, said positively with the x = 1 check; notes); "ln(3x) gives 3/x" (9, crossed); "2 ln x is ln 2x" (13, crossed); "ln(x³) gives 1/x³" (15, crossed); "the rule extends to x ≤ 0" (8, notes).

## Memory aid check

If an app's users grow as e^x, the time to reach x thousand users is ln x. e^x is its own derivative, and ln x undoes e^x. So why is the derivative of ln x equal to 1/x? Measure the slope of ln x: at every point, gradient × x = 1, and the gradients trace y = 1/x.
