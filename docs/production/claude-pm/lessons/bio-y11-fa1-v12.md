### 2. the slope: what active transport is
> Think of a concentration gradient as a slope. Particles spread down it on their own, and that's diffusion. To move particles up the slope, from low concentration to high, the cell has to do **work**. That's active transport: moving a substance against its concentration gradient, using a carrier protein and energy from ATP. ATP is the cell's energy currency, a small molecule made mostly in mitochondria. Breaking it down releases the energy the protein **uses**.

### 3. the pump, step by step

That changes the protein's **shape**. It closes to the outside and opens to the inside, and the ion is **released** where it's already more concentrated. Then the phosphate comes off, and the protein snaps back, ready for the next **ion**.

### 4. no ATP? (a quick prediction)
> Quick prediction: if the cell runs out of ATP, what happens to the gradient? It runs **down**. The pumps stop changing shape, but ions still leak back slowly through channels, down their gradient. Bit by bit, the difference between inside and outside disappears. So a gradient isn't built once and kept for free. The cell pays for it **continuously**, one ATP at a time. That's one reason a dead cell can't hold on to its gradients.

### 5. why pay? one: keeping the cell's size steady

Remember, water follows salt, so water would move in by osmosis, and the cell would **swell**. Pumping keeps the cell's size **steady**.

### 7. active transport versus facilitated diffusion
> Now compare this with facilitated diffusion. Both use a carrier protein, and both carry only particular substances. The difference is direction, and that decides the energy. Facilitated diffusion only moves a substance down its gradient, so it needs no ATP, and its net movement stops when the concentrations are equal. Active transport moves a substance against its gradient, so it needs ATP, and it keeps going long after the inside is more concentrated. A memory hook: downhill is free, uphill needs fuel. Ask the direction before the **protein**.

### 8. why so many mitochondria?
> Each cycle of a pump like that uses one ATP, and a busy cell runs thousands of pumps nonstop. So cells that do a lot of active transport need a lot of ATP. Most of it is made by aerobic respiration in the **mitochondria**. That's why these cells have many mitochondria, like root cells taking in minerals, and the cells lining the kidney tubules and the small intestine. The structure, many mitochondria, supports the **job**.

### 11. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Active transport: movement of a substance against its concentration gradient (low to high), using a carrier protein and energy from ATP.
2. The pump: the substance binds to a specific site; ATP is broken down and a phosphate group attaches to the protein; the protein changes shape and releases the substance on the high side; the phosphate comes off and the protein resets.
3. If the ATP supply falls, energy-dependent uptake slows and the gradient runs down by leaking, so the cell pays continuously. (For ions, electrical forces matter too; here we consider concentration alone.)
4. Why pay: the sodium-potassium pump keeps sodium out, so water doesn't follow it in and the cell keeps its size; cells collect scarce ions (for example nitrate into root cells).
5. Facilitated diffusion uses a carrier too, but moves down the gradient with no ATP. Cells doing a lot of active transport have many mitochondria to supply ATP.

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Ion concentrations (scene 1, screen chip; A2).** Typical mammalian cells: potassium about 140 mmol/L inside and about 5 outside (about 28 times, spoken "nearly thirty times"); sodium about 10 to 15 inside and about 145 outside. A2 uses 12 and 145. Codex to confirm against a standard text.
- **Sodium-potassium pump (scene 5).** 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP (screen chip only); a P-type pump: a phosphate group from ATP attaches to the pump, driving the shape change (scene 3, site finding 1).
- **Cell volume (scene 5).** When the pump is inhibited, sodium accumulates, water enters by osmosis and many animal cells swell (the pump-leak idea). Codex to confirm the wording "Pumping keeps the cell's size steady" as a fair Year 11 statement.
- **Leak (scene 4).** Without ATP the gradients run down as ions leak through channels. Correct in substance.
- **Root cells (scene 6).** Root cells take up mineral ions such as nitrate against their gradient by active transport (secondary active transport in reality; author note in Decisions).
- **Thousands of pumps (scene 8).** Animal cells carry from hundreds to millions of sodium-potassium pumps depending on type; "a busy cell runs thousands of pumps" is conservative. Codex to confirm.
- **Mitochondria (scene 8).** Most ATP in aerobic cells comes from aerobic respiration in mitochondria; kidney tubule and intestinal lining cells are rich in mitochondria. Correct.
- **A1 data (illustrative).** A potassium rise against a 0.1 mmol/L pond concentration and a slow fall after a respiratory inhibitor match classic algal uptake experiments in shape; the numbers are illustrative and labelled so.
