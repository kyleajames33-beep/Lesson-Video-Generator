### 2. the slope: what active transport is
> Think of a concentration gradient as a slope. Particles spread down it on their own, and that's diffusion. For ions, electrical forces matter as well, but here we'll follow concentration alone. To move particles up the slope, from low concentration to high, the cell has to do **work**. That's active transport: moving a substance against its concentration gradient, using a carrier protein and energy from ATP. ATP is the cell's energy currency, a small molecule made mostly in mitochondria. Breaking it down releases the energy the protein **uses**.

### 3. the pump, step by step
> Here's a carrier protein that pumps one kind of ion into the cell. First, it's open to the outside, where the ion is scarce. An ion fits into a binding site shaped for it, so this pump carries only that kind of ion. Next, a molecule of ATP is broken down, and a phosphate group from it attaches to the protein. That changes the protein's **shape**. It closes to the outside and opens to the inside, and the ion is **released** where it's already more concentrated. Then the phosphate comes off, and the protein snaps back, ready for the next **ion**. Each cycle moves one more ion uphill, and uses up one more ATP.

### 4. no ATP? (a quick prediction)
> Quick prediction: if the cell runs out of ATP, what happens to the gradient? It runs **down**. The pumps stop changing shape, but ions still leak back slowly through channels, down their gradient. Watch the two counts: the inside one falls and the outside one rises, until the difference has almost disappeared. So a gradient isn't built once and kept for free. The cell pays for it **continuously**, one ATP at a time. Give the cell its ATP back, and the counts pull apart again. That's one reason a dead cell can't hold on to its gradients.

### 5. why pay? one: keeping the cell's size steady
> So why pay? Here's one big reason. A real pump in animal cells, the sodium-potassium pump, pushes sodium ions out and potassium ions in, both against their gradients, every time it uses an ATP. Sodium keeps leaking in, and the pump keeps throwing it back out. If the pumping stopped, sodium would build up inside. Remember, water follows salt, so water would move in by osmosis, and the cell would **swell**. Pumping keeps the cell's size **steady**.
