### 2. why minus means right: everything happens later
> Here's why. Runner B does at time t exactly what runner A did at time t minus two. At four seconds, B has run what A ran at two seconds, four metres. At six seconds, B matches A at four seconds, sixteen **metres**. So every point on A's graph turns up on B's graph two seconds later, two units to the right. The same reason works for y equals x minus three, all squared. Call the part in the **bracket** that replaced x the inside. For y equals x squared, the inside is just x, and the old vertex happens where the inside is zero. The new graph only reaches an inside of zero when x minus three is zero, at x equals **three**. Everything happens three later, so the graph slides right by three. That's a horizontal translation, a **slide** sideways. Minus three inside means right three. Plus three would mean left three.

### 3. a head start: replacing y by y − 10
> Now a head start. Runner C starts ten metres up the track, and runs the same way as A. Every distance is ten more, so C's distance, minus ten, equals t squared. In the equation, d has been replaced by d minus **ten**. Solve for d, and d equals t squared plus ten, which is A's graph slid up ten. So replacing y by y minus ten slides the graph up ten. That's a vertical translation. Notice it's the same rule on both axes. Subtract three from x, and the graph moves three the positive way, right. Subtract two from y, and it moves two the positive way, **up**. Usually we write the vertical slide solved for y, so y minus two equals x squared becomes y equals x squared plus two. Outside the bracket, plus two means up two, just as it's **written**.

### 4. in a graphing app

### 6. worked example: write it, then find the intercepts
