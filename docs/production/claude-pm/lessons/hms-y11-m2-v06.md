### 2. one tube, three helpers
> Fuel starts in one long tube, with helpers alongside. Digestion is mechanical, breaking food into pieces, and chemical, using enzymes to split the molecules. The oesophagus pushes food down by waves of muscle, called **peristalsis**. The stomach churns it with acid, then releases it slowly. The small intestine does most digestion and almost all **absorption**, across millions of **villi**: a huge folded surface with thin walls and blood close by, like the alveoli. The large intestine takes back water. The liver makes bile and stores glycogen, the gallbladder releases bile to split fat into droplets, and the pancreas adds enzymes.

### 3. what food becomes
> Carbohydrate becomes glucose, carried in the blood or stored as **glycogen** in muscle and liver, the main fuel at moderate and high intensity. Protein becomes amino acids, mostly to build and repair muscle, and only a small share of fuel. Fat becomes fatty acids and glycerol, the main fuel at rest and low intensity. It packs about thirty-seven kilojoules a gram, against seventeen for carbohydrate, but releases energy slowly, and only with oxygen. That's why running out of glycogen can end a race, while the runner still carries plenty of fat.

### 5. a second control system
> Now control. The **endocrine system** is a set of glands that release hormones into the blood. A hormone reaches every cell, but only acts on cells with a matching **receptor**. Compare nerves. They send electrical signals to one target in milliseconds. Hormones take seconds to hours, reach anywhere, and last minutes to days. The hypothalamus and pituitary are the control centre, releasing growth hormone, and antidiuretic hormone, which saves water. The thyroid sets your metabolic rate. The adrenal glands release adrenaline, cortisol, and aldosterone, which saves sodium. The pancreas releases insulin and glucagon. And the ovaries and testes release oestrogen and testosterone.

### 6. the pancreas, and the glucose thermostat
> So the pancreas belongs to both systems. It digests carbohydrate into glucose, then releases the hormones that decide what happens to that glucose: supply and control in one organ. After a meal, blood glucose rises, so the pancreas releases **insulin**, which moves glucose into cells. Glucose falls, and insulin stops. When glucose drops too low, **glucagon** releases more from liver glycogen. That's **negative feedback**: a change triggers a response that reverses it, like a thermostat.

### 7. one training session, in hormones
> Follow one training session. Before and at the start, adrenaline raises heart rate, releases glucose, and opens the taps to muscle while closing those to the gut. So a meal still in your stomach stops being digested, and that's why you feel sick. During the session, glucagon holds blood glucose steady as muscles use it, and in long sessions cortisol frees fat for fuel. Antidiuretic hormone and aldosterone hold onto water and salt as you sweat. Afterwards, when you eat, insulin refills glycogen, and growth hormone repairs tissue, mostly in deep sleep. That's why sleep is part of training.

### 8. stress: useful, then harmful
> That answers the race question too. Nerves before a race release the same adrenaline, pulling blood from the gut. That's **acute stress**, and it's useful, because it gets you ready to move. The problem is **chronic stress**, the kind that doesn't switch off. Then the gut gets too little blood for too long, causing nausea and cramps. And cortisol stays high, keeping the body breaking down when it should be rebuilding. Muscle protein breaks down, recovery slows, illness comes more often, and sleep suffers, which cuts growth hormone. Poor sleep raises stress again, so the loop feeds itself.

### 9. other factors
> Other factors work the same way. Fat, fibre and protein slow the stomach, so pre-training meals are light, mostly carbohydrate. Dehydration slows absorption, just when you need fluid most. Poor sleep cuts growth hormone and raises cortisol. The serious one is **low energy availability**: eating less than training needs. The body quietly cuts spending on hormones, bone and immunity, so it shows up as stress fractures and repeated illness. Weight loss isn't a good sign here. It's a reason to see a doctor.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Tract (2).** Organ functions as the site; most digestion and almost all absorption in the small intestine; bile emulsifies fat; the pancreas secretes enzymes for all three macronutrients. No surface-area number on screen (site correction 17).
- **Macronutrients (3).** Fat about 37 kJ/g; carbohydrate and protein about 17 kJ/g (carbohydrate 16 to 17). Carbohydrate the main fuel at moderate and high intensity; fat at rest and low intensity; protein a minor fuel. Fat oxidation requires oxygen.
- **Micronutrients (4).** Iron (haemoglobin); calcium (bone; triggers contraction); vitamin D (calcium absorption); B vitamins (coenzymes in energy release); sodium and potassium (fluid balance, nerve and muscle excitability; lost in sweat, mostly sodium); vitamins C and E (antioxidants).
- **Endocrine (5).** Hormone actions as the site; nervous against endocrine speeds (milliseconds against seconds to hours) and durations; ADH conserves water; aldosterone conserves sodium; thyroxine sets metabolic rate.
- **Glucose control (6, 7, 11d).** Insulin lowers, glucagon raises (from liver glycogen); negative feedback. During prolonged exercise insulin falls and glucagon rises; cortisol mobilises fat (and protein); adrenaline releases glucose; growth hormone pulses in slow-wave sleep.
- **Stress (8).** Acute sympathetic and adrenaline response with gut vasoconstriction; chronic stress effects on gut blood flow and motility; chronically raised cortisol: protein catabolism, impaired recovery, immune suppression, sleep disruption.
- **Factors (9).** Fat, fibre and protein slow gastric emptying; dehydration slows gastric emptying and absorption; low energy availability effects (menstrual disturbance, bone density, immunity, stress fractures) as the site.
