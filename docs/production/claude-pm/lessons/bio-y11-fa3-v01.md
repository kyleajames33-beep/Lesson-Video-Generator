### 2. variation, and a prediction
> Start with the differences. Each beetle was born pale or dark, because beetles carry different alleles, different versions of the colour gene. You've seen where those come from. Rare copying mistakes make new alleles, and meiosis and fertilisation shuffle them into new combinations. That's genetic variation, and it arises by chance, not because a beetle needs it. Now add birds that hunt by sight. Prediction: on pale bark, which beetles get eaten more?

Visual: the first chain chip writes at the top: "variation exists". On "different alleles" a small cream or umber allele dot appears inside each beetle (grey tag "allele: a version of a gene"). On "Rare copying mistakes" a coded DNA ladder icon (the FA1 style, small, at the left) shows one rung changing colour (tag "mutation: a new allele"); on "meiosis and fertilisation shuffle them" two small coded gamete circles carrying dots merge into one (tag "new combinations: FA1"). On "arises by chance" a gloss writes: "genetic variation: inherited differences, already there by chance"; a `WrongChip` "made because it's needed" appears and takes a scribble cross. The allele dots fade. On "add birds" `y11-shrike-thrush-v1.png` lands on a branch stub at the upper right of the bark. On "Prediction" a "Predict" chip writes; a pale and a dark beetle each take a "?"; **a 3 s countdown ring** runs.

### 3. the pressure changes the odds
> The dark ones, because they stand out against the bark. That's a selective pressure: anything in the environment that makes some variants survive and reproduce more than others. But notice, it doesn't kill every dark beetle, or spare every pale one. A few pale beetles get eaten too. The pressure changes the odds, and across a whole population, the odds are what count.

Visual: on "The dark ones" the dark beetles show a faint contrast halo (coded) that the pale ones lack. The second chain chip writes: "a pressure acts". On "selective pressure" a gloss: "selective pressure: makes some variants survive and reproduce more" and a grey tag "variant: one form of a trait". On "doesn't kill every dark beetle" the bird takes beetles one by one with a peck flash: six of the ten dark beetles fade, and on "A few pale beetles get eaten too" two of the ten pale beetles fade. A tally chip counts as they go: "taken: 2 of 10 pale · 6 of 10 dark" (grey tag "illustrative numbers"). On "changes the odds" two coded odds bars appear under the bark: "pale: 8 in 10 survive", "dark: 4 in 10 survive". At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 4. survivors leave more young
> Now the survivors breed. Eight pale beetles survived, but only four dark ones, so pale beetles leave more young. That's called differential reproductive success: some variants leave more offspring than others. And that's what fitness means in evolution: how many surviving offspring you leave. It doesn't mean strongest or fastest. Here, a pale beetle is fitter simply because it blends in.

Visual: the twelve survivors gather slightly. The third chain chip writes: "survivors breed more". On "leave more young" small beetle sprites (the same plates at a smaller uniform scale) appear beside each survivor in pairs, more beside the pale group because there are more pale parents (no eggs or mating shown; tag "young"). On "differential reproductive success" a gloss writes: "differential reproductive success: some variants leave more offspring". On "fitness" a chip "fitness = surviving offspring" writes; on "strongest or fastest" a `WrongChip` "fitness = strongest" takes a scribble cross. On "blends in" one pale survivor pulses against the bark. At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 5. inheritance carries it forward
> Here's where inheritance comes in. Colour is passed from parents to offspring through their alleles, so the young share their parents' colours. That means the next generation starts with more pale beetles: thirteen pale and seven dark, instead of ten and ten. If colour weren't inherited, the birds would still sort the beetles every generation, but nothing would carry forward.

Visual: on "passed from parents to offspring" the allele dots reappear in the survivors and copy across, coded, into their young (cream to cream, umber to umber; grey tag "simple model: young share their parent's colour"). On "the next generation" the young grow to full sprite size and the bark repopulates to twenty: thirteen pale, seven dark. The `GenerationLadder` opens at the right: strip "generation 1: 10 pale · 10 dark" (share bar half and half) and below it "generation 2: 13 pale · 7 dark". On "If colour weren't inherited" a ghost version of the generation 2 strip briefly shows "10 · 10" again with a grey tag "no inheritance: no carry-over", then fades. At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 6. generations, and evolution
> Run it again. The birds still take more of the dark beetles, so the shift continues: sixteen pale in the third generation, and eighteen in the fourth. Each generation, the pale allele makes up a bigger share of the population's colour alleles. That change, in a population's inherited characteristics across generations, is evolution. When it happens because some variants out-reproduce others, it's evolution by natural selection.

Visual: the fourth chain chip writes: "the population shifts". On "Run it again" the `SpriteField` runs one fast cycle (peck flashes, survivors, young, repopulate) in about two seconds per generation; the ladder adds "generation 3: 16 pale · 4 dark" and "generation 4: 18 pale · 2 dark". On "a bigger share" the four share bars glow in turn, the cream portion growing (labels 50%, 65%, 80%, 90%; grey tag "allele frequency: the share of one allele"). On "evolution by natural selection" the four chain chips light left to right and an arrow loops from the last chip back to the first (tag "repeat each generation"). At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 7. no beetle changed
> Now follow one beetle. This one was born dark, and it stays dark its whole life. The birds can't make it paler. So no beetle evolved. The population did, because its mix changed. Selection acts on individuals, but evolution happens to populations. That's why "the beetles turned pale to hide" is wrong. Selection is a sieve, not a sculptor. It only sorts what's already there.

Visual: the `Cam` pushes in on one dark beetle from generation 1; a coded lifetime bar runs under it and it stays dark throughout (tag "born dark, dies dark"). On "The population did" the ladder's four share bars glow together. On "acts on individuals" the single beetle rings; on "happens to populations" the whole generation strip rings; a rule chip writes "selection acts on individuals; populations evolve". At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 8. a change in life isn't passed on
> Here's a twist. A dark beetle crawls through pale ash, and the ash coats its shell. Now it looks pale, so the birds miss it, and it survives to breed. But the ash hasn't changed its alleles, so its young hatch dark. A change gained during life isn't in the eggs or sperm, so it can't be passed on. For natural selection to change a population, the trait has to be heritable, which means it can be passed on.

On "crawls through pale ash" a coded ash patch on the bark; a dark beetle crosses it and cross-fades to `y11-beetle-dark-ash-v1.png` (same pose, dusted pale grey). On "the birds miss it" the bird looks past it (a coded gaze line skips it). At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 9. three ingredients, and a prediction
> So natural selection needs three ingredients: variation, inheritance and a selective pressure. Remember them as VIP. No VIP, no natural selection. Prediction: if every beetle were the same colour, what could the birds change?

Visual: the chain shrinks to the top. Three large ingredient cards write one per cue at the centre: "V: variation", "I: inheritance", "P: pressure" (each with a small icon: two beetle colours, an allele dot copying, the bird). On "VIP" the three initials glow (tag "the site's memory hook"). On "Prediction" the bark shows twenty beetles all pale; a "Predict" chip writes; **a 3 s countdown ring** runs.

### 10. take one away
> Nothing. With no variation, every beetle has the same odds, so there's nothing to favour. With no inheritance, like the ash, the advantage is lost each generation. And with no pressure, say no birds at all, pale and dark beetles breed equally, so the mix doesn't shift in any steady direction. Take away any one of the three, and natural selection can't change the population.

Visual: on "Nothing" the all-pale field holds; the bird takes two at random; the next strip is still all pale (tag "no variation: nothing to favour"). The "V" card dims and returns. On "no inheritance" the "I" card dims; the ash beetle from scene 8 and its dark young flash back small (tag "advantage lost each generation"). On "no pressure" the "P" card dims; the bird leaves; a mixed field breeds and the share bar wobbles a little either side of half (tag "no steady direction"). On "Take away any one" all three cards light, then each dims in turn with the chain greying out each time. At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 11. the limits
> A discuss answer also needs the limits. Selection can only work with variation that's already there. If no beetle could blend in, the birds would just eat more beetles, and the population might shrink instead of changing. And if the bark changed faster than beetles could breed, the population couldn't keep up. It's like the tolerance range from last time: if conditions move beyond what every variant can cope with, there's nothing to favour.

Visual: a chip "discuss: give the limits" writes at the top left. On "variation that's already there" the generation 1 field returns. On "If no beetle could blend in" the bark plate darkens to a coded dark tint while both beetle colours show contrast halos; the bird takes beetles of both colours and the count chip falls "20 → 9" (tag "illustrative"); the field thins. On "changed faster than beetles could breed" a coded timeline shows the bark tint stepping quickly while generation ticks lag behind. On "the tolerance range from last time" v33's curve thumbnail returns small at the upper left and its marker dot slides past the amber band into the terracotta gradient (tag "like a tolerance range: beyond what any variant copes with, nothing to favour"). Beetle colours are not plotted on the temperature axis. At most three bright items. Hold one second.

### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Genetic variation: inherited differences (different alleles) that already exist by chance, from mutation and from meiosis and fertilisation. Not made because they're needed.
- Selective pressure: anything in the environment that makes some variants survive and reproduce more than others. It changes the odds.
- Natural selection: variation exists → a pressure acts → better-suited variants survive and reproduce more (differential reproductive success) → they pass on their alleles → the population shifts over generations.
- Fitness = number of surviving offspring, not strength. Individuals don't evolve; populations do. A change gained in life is not inherited.
- All three are needed (VIP: variation, inheritance, pressure). Limits: selection works only on existing variation; too fast or too extreme a change can leave nothing to favour.

Memory: VIP, no VIP, no natural selection. Selection is a sieve, not a sculptor.
