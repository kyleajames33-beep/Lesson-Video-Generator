## Decisions

- **Main question:** *A class prepares pentyl ethanoate, the banana-smelling ester, and gets about 60% of the most it could make. Is that a failed practical? How do you prepare an ester safely, calculate its yield, explain what limits it, and design a fair test of whether excess acid helps?* Heading on screen: "Making an ester: is 60% a fail?"
- **Why pentyl ethanoate (and pentan-1-ol):** c10 used ethyl ethanoate throughout, so a new ester keeps the practical from being a replay while recalling the same steps. Pentyl ethanoate is a common school preparation (site L15 lists it, and c10 names it as the banana ester), its alcohol and ester are only flammable (H226), not highly flammable (H225) like ethanol and ethyl ethanoate, and the ester barely dissolves in water (about 0.2 g per 100 mL), so the layers separate cleanly with the ester on top (density 0.876 g/mL). The mixture's boiling point is well above 100 °C, so reflux needs an electric heating mantle; a boiling water bath cannot bring it to reflux. This replaces the route review's "water bath for ester reflux" (written with ethanol in mind), which the narration addresses in one clause (author note b). The hook links to c10's fruit plate (banana).
- **Why an investigation, not just a recipe:** the dotpoint says "investigate". A yield comparison between a 1:1 and a 2:1 ratio of acid to alcohol gives the practical an aim, an independent variable, controlled variables and data to judge, and it tests c10's Le Chatelier prediction in the lab. Pentan-1-ol is the limiting reactant in both, so both yields are measured against the same amount of alcohol.
- **Numbers (author note a; Numbers to verify):** worked group (2:1): 4.41 g pentan-1-ol (about 5.4 mL) → 0.0500 mol; about 6.0 mL glacial ethanoic acid (about 0.10 mol, 2:1); maximum 0.0500 mol pentyl ethanoate × 130.18 g/mol = 6.51 g; collected 3.90 g (vial 12.36 g, vial + ester 16.26 g; screen only) → 59.9%, said "about sixty per cent". Class data (screen and scene 8): 1:1 runs 41, 38, 44% (mean 41%); 2:1 runs 60, 57, 63% (mean 60%). Ceilings from K about 4 (c10's value; author note d): 1:1 about 67%, 2:1 about 85%. Each ratio sits about 25 points below its ceiling (67 − 41 = 26; 85 − 60 = 25), which carries the validity argument in scene 9. All yield data are labelled "example data".
- **Site sources used (site lesson 15):** the conditions panel ("REAGENT: Carboxylic acid + alcohol (at least one in excess for higher yield)"; "CONDITIONS: Heat under reflux"; "YIELD: <100% at equilibrium"); the Safety note (concentrated sulfuric acid "highly corrosive and reacts violently and exothermically with water, add it dropwise to the cooled mixture, wear goggles and gloves"; "heat with an electric heating mantle or water bath rather than a naked flame, add anti-bumping granules, and never seal the apparatus"; "work in a well-ventilated area"; scenes 3 and 11); the Isolation Procedure (cold water in the separating funnel; "Identify the aqueous layer from density data or a drop test rather than assuming it is always lower"; the carbonate wash with frequent venting "away from people"; anhydrous Na₂SO₄ or MgSO₄ that "clumps as it hydrates"; "Collect the fraction at the known boiling point"; scenes 5 and 10); Worked Example 2 (use an excess; reflux without a naked flame; reflux "improves rate while retaining volatile components"; scenes 2 and 3); the common esters list and Q6 (pentyl ethanoate, banana). Q8's yield question uses "55% yield" without a calculation; this video adds the calculation the site does not show.
- **Wash reagent:** the site and c10 use sodium carbonate solution. This design uses sodium hydrogen carbonate solution (about 5%), which does the same job (turns leftover carboxylic acid into a salt in the water layer, releasing CO₂), is the c09 fizz-test reagent students already know, and is not classified as an eye irritant (sodium carbonate is H319). The key notes say "NaHCO₃ (or Na₂CO₃) solution" so the two do not look contradictory (Open decisions).
- **Main teaching move:** the yield is limited twice. Equilibrium sets the ceiling; the work-up decides how close you get. Losses push every group's yield low (so no single yield is accurate and repeats cannot fix it), but because both ratios go through an identical work-up they lose about the same share, so the comparison between ratios is still valid. A yield above 100% is a red flag for a wet or impure product, not a better reaction.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) 60%: fail or not? (1); (2) the aim and the limiting reactant (2); (3) reflux to approach equilibrium without losing volatile liquids; why a mantle (3); (4) one variable changed, the work-up kept identical (4); (5) get the ester out and weigh it, recalled in one pass (5); (6) percentage yield from moles of the limiting reactant (6); (7) the equilibrium ceiling and work-up losses (7); (8) repeats agree, and the difference between ratios is larger than the spread (8); (9) not accurate, but a valid comparison; over 100% means a wet product (9); (10) identify the product without sniffing it (10); (11) hazards and controls (11); memory aid (12); attempts and brief feedback (13 to 16); decision rule and handoff (17); key notes (18).
- **Entry knowledge:** chem-m7-c10 (esterification equation with conditions and ⇌; names; a catalyst speeds both directions and does not change the yield; reflux keeps volatile liquids in; about two thirds from equal amounts, about nine tenths with a threefold excess of alcohol; the separating funnel; carbonate wash and venting; dry and distil; the boiling point is a rough check). chem-m7-c09 (hydrogen carbonate fizz test). skills-s07 and chem-m7-p03 (validity, reliability, accuracy; word, feature, fix; a loss that pushes every trial the same way). chem-m7-p01 (the organic waste container). - **Attempt systems (unseen, every condition visible, HSC wording, original):** (A1) "A student heated 3.70 g of butan-1-ol with excess ethanoic acid and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid under reflux. After washing and drying the product, she collected 3.19 g of butyl ethanoate. (a) Calculate the percentage yield of butyl ethanoate. (b) Explain TWO reasons why the yield is less than 100%." (4 marks.) Molar masses on the card: butan-1-ol 74.12 g mol⁻¹; butyl ethanoate 116.16 g mol⁻¹. New alcohol and ester. Answer: n(butan-1-ol) = 3.70 ÷ 74.12 = 0.0499 mol; 1:1, so the maximum n(ester) = 0.0499 mol; maximum mass = 0.0499 × 116.16 = 5.80 g; yield = 3.19 ÷ 5.80 × 100 = 55.0%. Reasons: the reaction is reversible and reaches equilibrium, so not all the alcohol reacts; ester is lost during transfers, washing and drying. Crossed mistake: "3.19 ÷ 3.70 × 100 = 86%" (dividing masses directly, the moles step skipped). (A2) "A student prepared propyl ethanoate from propan-1-ol and excess ethanoic acid. ### 3. the reflux set-up
> Measure the pentan-1-ol and the ethanoic acid into a round-bottom flask, and add a few anti-bumping granules. Your teacher then adds about ten drops of concentrated sulfuric acid, the **catalyst**. Fit the condenser upright, and leave its top **open**. This mixture boils well above a hundred degrees, so a water bath isn't hot enough. Heat it with an electric heating mantle for about forty minutes. The condenser sends the vapours back, so nothing **escapes**.

### 5. getting it out and weighing it
> After cooling, pour the mixture into a separating funnel with cold water. The ester barely dissolves, so it forms its own **layer**: on top here, because it's less dense than water. Wash it with sodium hydrogen carbonate solution to remove the leftover acid, and vent the funnel often, away from people, to release the carbon **dioxide**. Then dry the ester with anhydrous magnesium sulfate, pour it into a weighed vial, and **weigh** it.

### 6. the yield, step by step
> Now the yield, for one two to one group. Four point four one grams of pentan-1-ol, divided by its molar mass, is zero point zero five zero zero **moles**. One mole of alcohol makes one mole of ester, so that's also the most ester they could make. Times the ester's molar mass, that's six point five one grams. They collected three point nine zero grams. Divide, times a hundred: about **sixty per cent**.

### 7. two limits: the ceiling and the losses
> So where did the rest go? First, equilibrium. With two to one, roughly eighty five per cent of the alcohol could become ester, at best. That's the **ceiling**. Second, losses. Some ester stays on the glassware, some goes in the washes, and some is left on the drying agent. Those **losses** take the result from the ceiling down to about sixty.

### 8. reliable results, and the answer to the aim
> Three groups ran each ratio. One to one gave about forty per cent each time, and two to one about sixty. Within each ratio, the results **agree** within a few per cent, so they're reliable. ## Site corrections (new; quoted from site lesson 15, read 28 Oct)

c10 (Revision 3) already records site lesson 15 corrections 1 to 5 (the pineapple ester, the pentyl ethanoate smell and boiling point, the avocado fats link, reflux and "maximum yield") and its notes on concentrated sulfuric acid as a water remover. P05 does not repeat them. New:

1. Card 3, "Maximising Yield": "Remove ester as it forms distil off continuously; permanently shifts equilibrium right". Distilling the product off removes it from the mixture, so the system keeps shifting right while that continues; "permanently" suggests a lasting change in the equilibrium itself. It also cannot be done during reflux, which the page's own conditions require, and it only works when the ester is the lowest-boiling component. Correct: "Distil off the ester as it forms (possible only when it is the most volatile component): the system keeps shifting right to replace it." Severity: should fix.
2. Card 1, common esters list: "C₂H₅COOC₅H₁₁ → Pentyl propanoate pear". The ester usually cited for pear in school tables is propyl ethanoate (c10 site correction 1 already notes this); pentyl propanoate is usually described as fruity or apricot-like. Correct: "CH₃COOC₃H₇ → propyl ethanoate, pear" or keep pentyl propanoate described as "fruity". Severity: note (smell descriptions vary; Codex to confirm against a flavour reference).
3. Card 3 Safety note: "heat with an electric heating mantle or water bath rather than a naked flame". A boiling water bath (100 °C) cannot bring many school ester mixtures to reflux (for example pentan-1-ol or butan-1-ol with ethanoic acid, which reflux well above 100 °C). Correct: "heat with an electric heating mantle (a water bath only for low-boiling mixtures such as ethanol and ethanoic acid), never a naked flame". Severity: note.
4. Short Answer Q8 and its answer: "(c) ... naming the reagent used to remove residual butanoic acid from the ester layer" with the answer "Step 1: add cold water ... Step 4: dry ... then distil for purity. (2 marks)". Methyl butanoate (bp about 102 °C) is noticeably soluble in water (about 1.5 g per 100 mL), so washes lose more ester than the page implies; this does not make the answer wrong. Not a correction; recorded as a note for the science check, because the video's A2 uses propyl ethanoate (solubility about 2 g per 100 mL) and its feedback does not claim no loss.
