### 2. one method for every problem
> Every force problem yields to the same four steps. One, draw a free-body diagram of one object, showing every force on it. Two, choose a positive direction. Three, add up the forces along each direction. Four, ask the key question: is the object accelerating? If it isn't, it's at rest or moving at a constant velocity, and the net force is **zero**. That's called equilibrium. If it is accelerating, the net force equals m **a**. So the deciding question isn't "is it moving?" It's "is it accelerating?"

Visual: the recipe rail draws at the far left, one step per spoken number: "1 free-body diagram (one object)", "2 positive direction", "3 add the forces in each direction", "4 accelerating?". Under step 4 a fork: on "zero" the left branch "no: F_net = 0 (equilibrium: at rest or constant velocity)"; on "a" the right branch "yes: F_net = ma". On the last sentence a chip "moving?" is crossed and replaced by "accelerating?".

### 3. at rest and at constant velocity
> Back to the crane. When the load hangs still, it isn't accelerating, so the forces balance. Take up as positive. The tension up minus the weight down is zero, so the tension equals the weight, m g. That's two hundred times nine point eight, nineteen hundred and sixty **newtons**. And if the crane lifted the load at a steady speed, the answer would be exactly the same. Constant velocity is still zero acceleration, so nothing extra is needed to keep it **going**.

Visual: the load hangs still; rail lights steps 1 to 4 in turn. Its free-body diagram builds beside it (author note a scale): grey "W = mg" down, amber "T" up, equal; "+ up" arrow. Givens (navy): "m = 200 kg; g = 9.8 m s⁻²", grey tag "teaching data, exact values". Rows: "T − mg = 0"; on "newtons" "T = mg = 200 kg × 9.8 m s⁻² = 1960 N" (boxed). On "steady speed" the load rises at constant speed (teal velocity arrow of fixed length); the two force arrows stay exactly equal, tag "constant velocity: a = 0". On "going" a chip "moving up needs extra force" fades in and is crossed.

### 4. speeding up
> Now the crane speeds the load up, at zero point five oh metres per second squared, upwards. Now there must be a net force upwards, so the tension has to beat the weight. Tension minus weight equals m a. So the tension is the weight plus m a, nineteen sixty plus two hundred times zero point five oh, which is two thousand and sixty **newtons**. The cable pulls harder than the weight only while the load is speeding **up**.

Visual: givens add "a = 0.50 m s⁻² up"; the rail's step 4 takes the "yes" branch. The load's teal velocity arrow lengthens steadily; an orange acceleration arrow appears. The amber T arrow grows past the grey W arrow; the outlined net-force arrow fills the gap, labelled "100 N". Rows: "T − mg = ma"; on "newtons" "T = mg + ma = 1960 + 200 × 0.50 = 2060 N" (boxed). ### 5. two directions, one crate
> Here's a problem with both cases at once. A twenty-five kilogram crate is pulled across level ground by a horizontal rope, with a tension of ninety newtons. The coefficient of kinetic friction is zero point two oh. Up and down, the crate isn't accelerating, so F N balances the weight. Twenty-five times nine point eight, two hundred and forty-five **newtons**. Friction is zero point two oh times that, forty-nine newtons, **backwards**. Along the ground, the crate is accelerating. The net force is ninety minus forty-nine, forty-one newtons, so a is forty-one over twenty-five, one point six metres per second **squared**. Equilibrium in one direction, F equals m a in the other.

### 6. an angled pull changes F N
> But F N doesn't always equal the weight. A fifteen kilogram sled is pulled with sixty newtons, by a rope angled twenty-five degrees above the horizontal. Split the pull into parts, as we did with vectors. Along the ground, sixty cos twenty-five, fifty-four point four newtons. Upwards, sixty sine twenty-five, twenty-five point four **newtons**. That upward part lifts some of the sled's weight off the snow. Up and down, the sled isn't accelerating, so F N plus twenty-five point four equals the weight, a hundred and forty-seven newtons. So F N is only a hundred and twenty-one point six **newtons**.

### 7. finish the angled pull
> With mu k at zero point two oh, friction is zero point two oh times a hundred and twenty-one point six, twenty-four point three **newtons**. Along the ground, the net force is fifty-four point four minus twenty-four point three, about thirty newtons. So a is thirty over fifteen, two point oh metres per second **squared**. If you'd assumed F N equals the weight, you'd get too much friction, and only one point seven. Always find F N from the vertical forces. Never **assume** it.

### 8. a static sign on two cables
> Now a static problem with angles. A twenty kilogram sign hangs from two cables, each at thirty degrees above the horizontal, one on each side. Upwards, each cable gives T sine thirty, and together they hold up the weight, a hundred and ninety-six newtons. So two times T times zero point five equals a hundred and ninety-six, and T is a hundred and ninety-six **newtons**. Each cable pulls as hard as the whole weight. The flatter the cables, the smaller their upward parts, so the bigger the tension. At ten degrees, each cable would need about five hundred and sixty-four **newtons**.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Method: free-body diagram of one object, choose a positive direction, add the forces in each direction, then F_net = 0 if it is not accelerating, or F_net = ma if it is.
2. Equilibrium: zero net force and zero acceleration, in every direction. The object is at rest or moving at constant velocity. (A load hung on two angled cables: the flatter the cables, the larger the tension.)

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 10; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks, not an official marking scheme).** "A 50 kg crate rests on bathroom scales on the floor of a goods lift. Model answer: Take down as positive. mg − F_N = ma, so F_N = m(g − a) = 50 × (9.8 − 1.2) = 430 N. The scales read 430 N (less than the crate's weight, 490 N, because the net force must point downwards). Classic mistake (crossed): "490 N".
