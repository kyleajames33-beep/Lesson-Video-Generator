Main question: *How does pressure at the glomerulus separate filtrate from blood?*
Entry knowledge: see above. Students have watched FA1 and FA2 v01 to v22.
Stops after: each kidney holds about a million nephrons; each nephron begins with a glomerulus (a knot of capillaries) inside Bowman's capsule (a cup), which leads into the tubule; blood enters the glomerulus through a wide vessel and leaves through a narrower one, so pressure inside the knot is high (unlike most capillaries); higher pressure forces more fluid out; the filter (pores in the capillary wall, slits in the capsule's lining) lets water and small particles through (glucose, amino acids, salts, urea) and holds back cells, platelets and plasma proteins; it sorts by size, not usefulness, so useful glucose leaves with urea; the filtrate is like plasma without its proteins, with small particles at the same concentrations, because pressure carries everything small along by bulk flow (not diffusion); the capsule collects the filtrate and funnels it into the tubule; about a fifth of the plasma is filtered per pass, so blood leaves with all its cells and proteins in less water, into a second capillary net round the tubule; a sieve and beads models the size filter and the capsule's collecting role but not charge selection, pressure as the driver, or a filter swept clean by flowing blood; about 180 litres of filtrate a day against about 1.5 litres of urine.
