### 1. hook (an unknown, and a machine that weighs pieces)
> The stockroom has a bottle of unknown liquid. All you know is that each molecule contains carbon, hydrogen and one oxygen atom. You can't put one molecule on a balance. But a mass spectrometer does something cleverer. It breaks molecules into charged pieces and weighs every piece. The pattern of those masses is a mass spectrum, and each peak can rule possible structures **out**.

Visual: heading writes on: "What do the pieces weigh?". `p03-dropper-bottle-v1.png` (clear dropper bottle, blank label) at left with a coded "?" on its label and a coded chip "C, H and one O". On "out" a short candidate strip of four grey "?" boxes writes along the top. Bright at the end: the heading, the axes, the strip.

### 2. how it weighs them (five stages)
> Here's how it weighs them. The sample is turned into a gas. A beam of fast electrons knocks one electron off each molecule, so each molecule becomes a positive ion. An electric field speeds the ions up, and then a magnetic field bends their **path**. Light ions bend more than heavy ones, so the instrument can sort them by mass and count each kind. Only charged particles are steered, so a neutral piece is never **detected**.

Visual: the exterior plate cross-fades to the new `m8-ms-cutaway-v1.png` (Codex asset brief item 1) at full width. Coded tags write one per clause on the plate's five zones, left to right: "1 vaporise", "2 ionise", "3 accelerate", "4 deflect", "5 detect" (the current tag bright, earlier ones dim). Ion flight: grey dots enter the inlet; an electron streak crosses the ionisation chamber and the dots turn "+" (grey chip under it: "M + e⁻ → M⁺ + 2e⁻"); dots speed up between the plates; on "path" three streams curve through the magnet, the lightest bending most (tags "light" and "heavy"); on "count" the detector flashes once per stream. On "detected" one grey neutral dot drifts straight on, misses the detector and fades (tag "neutral: not steered"). Bright at the end: the five tags (one item), the neutral tag.

### 3. reading the chart
> The detector's counts become a bar chart. Across the bottom is m over z, the mass of each ion divided by its charge. Almost every ion here carries a charge of one plus, so you can read m over z as the ion's **mass**. Up the side is relative abundance: how many ions of each mass arrived, compared with the most common **one**.

Visual: the cutaway shrinks to a thumbnail at top right. The empty stick-spectrum axes return at reading size. On "m over z" the x axis label writes "m/z" with a grey chip "mass ÷ charge"; on "mass" the chip adds "charge 1+ → m/z = mass". ### 4. the molecular ion
> Here's the spectrum of our unknown. Start at the right. The last significant peak is at seventy-two. That's the molecular ion: the whole molecule, minus one electron. An electron's mass is tiny, so seventy-two is the molecular mass. One oxygen accounts for sixteen, which leaves fifty-six, and that fits four carbons and eight hydrogens: C four H eight **O**. Ignore the tiny bump at seventy-three. It comes from molecules that contain one carbon thirteen atom, not from a heavier **molecule**.

Visual: the butanone stick spectrum (Numbers to verify) draws bar by bar from left to right, all bars teal, no labels yet. On "Start at the right" a soft spotlight sweeps to the right end. On "seventy-two" the 72 bar turns gold with label "72: M⁺ (molecular ion)" (bright). A grey chip under the card: "without the one O: 72 could also be C₅H₁₂". On "seventy-three" the tiny 73 bar gets a grey label "73: M+1 (some ¹³C)" and a short grey "not M⁺" chip. Bright at the end: the gold 72 bar, "C₄H₈O".

### 5. same mass, different molecules
> But a mass of seventy-two doesn't finish the job. From the isomers lesson, C four H eight O fits butanone, butanal and several other structures, and every one of them weighs seventy-two. The molecular ion can't tell isomers apart. For that, you need the smaller **peaks**.

Visual: the candidate strip fills: butanone, butanal, 2-methylpropanal and but-3-en-2-ol as coded skeletal structures, each with a small "72" chip (grey chip "c02: isomers"). On "can't tell isomers apart" all four stay lit and a bracket writes under them: "all 72". On "peaks" the smaller bars of the spectrum pulse teal once. Bright at the end: the strip and the bracket.

### 6. a bond breaks
> Inside the instrument, many molecular ions carry so much energy that a bond breaks. One piece keeps the positive charge, and the other leaves as a neutral piece. The charged piece is detected and makes its own peak. The neutral piece is never seen. So the gap between the molecular ion and a fragment peak is the mass of the piece that was **lost**.

Visual: right card: break-and-weigh on a generic coded chain "M⁺": it snaps; the left piece gains "+" and drops onto a teal bar (tag "fragment ion: detected"); the right piece greys and floats up with a "?" mass chip (tag "neutral piece: not detected"). On "lost" a gap bracket draws between the gold 72 bar and a teal bar, chip "gap = mass lost". Bright at the end: the two tags and the bracket chip.

### 7. gaps you'll see often
> Some gaps turn up again and again. A gap of fifteen is a methyl group, C H three. Seventeen is an O H. Twenty-nine is an ethyl group, C two H five, but it could also be C H O, which has the same mass. And forty-five is C O O H. So a gap narrows the choices, and sometimes it leaves **two**.

Visual: the right card becomes a four-row table, each row writing as spoken: "15: CH₃", "17: OH", "29: C₂H₅ or CHO", "45: COOH", each with a tiny coded fragment drawing. ### 8. the base peak
> Now look at the tallest peak. It's at forty-three, not at seventy-two. The tallest peak is called the base peak, and the chart sets it to one hundred per cent. It's the most abundant ion, often a fragment that's stable enough to survive the trip. The molecular ion is the last significant peak, the base peak is the tallest, and they're often different **peaks**.

Visual: the table shrinks to the right edge. On "tallest" the 43 bar brightens and a dashed line runs from its top to the 100 tick (label "43: base peak, 100%"). On "not at seventy-two" the gold 72 bar pulses. On "often different" a chip "tallest = M⁺" fades in and is crossed. On "peaks" two labels sit together: "last significant: M⁺ (72)" gold and "tallest: base peak (43)" teal. Bright at the end: the two labels.

### 9. reading butanone's pieces
> Let's read the pieces. Seventy-two minus forty-three is twenty-nine, so the molecule lost an ethyl group, or C H O. Seventy-two minus fifty-seven is fifteen, so it also lost a methyl group. Butanone has a methyl group on one side of its C equals O and an ethyl group on the **other**. Break either bond and you get forty-three, C H three C O plus, or fifty-seven, C two H five C O plus. The small peak at twenty-nine is the ethyl ion itself. Every peak fits butanone. But butanal gives peaks at seventy-two and forty-three too, so this spectrum narrows the list without proving it. The next techniques test what's **left**.

On "other" butanone draws large in the right third, its two C–C bonds beside the C=O ringed. On "ethyl ion" the 29 bar gets "C₂H₅⁺". On "left" a grey chip "IR, NMR: next" slides in. Bright at the end: the two fragment labels, the glowing pair.

### 10. chlorine and bromine leave a signature
> Some elements leave a signature. Chlorine atoms come in two masses, thirty-five and thirty-seven, roughly three to one. So chloromethane shows its molecular ion twice: at fifty, and at fifty-two, about a third as tall. Two peaks two apart, three to one, points to one chlorine. Bromine's two isotopes are nearly equal, so one bromine gives two peaks of about the same **height**. The higher peak isn't a heavier molecule. It's the same molecule, carrying the heavier isotope.

Visual: the board clears to the chloromethane stick spectrum (Numbers to verify). Grey chip at top: "Cl: ³⁵Cl 75%, ³⁷Cl 25% (about 3 : 1)". On "same molecule" the 52 label adds "same molecule, heavier isotope", and a grey chip "peaks use isotope masses (35, 37), not 35.5". Bright at the end: the Cl chip, the Br inset.

### 11. what mass spectrometry can't tell you
> So what has the spectrum told us? The molecular ion gave the mass. The fragments showed which pieces can break off, and an isotope pair can flag chlorine or bromine. But it hasn't shown which functional group is present, or exactly how the atoms connect. Mass spectrometry narrows the list. Infrared and NMR finish the **job**.

Visual: the butanone spectrum returns small at left. A three-row card writes: "mass ✓ (M⁺)", "pieces ✓ (gaps)", "Cl or Br ✓ (isotope pairs)"; then two grey rows: "functional group ✗", "how atoms connect ✗". On "job" a chip row writes: "MS: mass → IR: groups → NMR: map" (grey chip "c08: tests show groups too"). Bright at the end: the two grey rows and the chip row.

### 12. memory aid

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Mass spectrometer: vaporise, ionise (an electron is knocked off), accelerate, deflect (magnetic field; lighter ions bend more), detect. Only ions are detected; neutral pieces are not.
2. Molecular ion M⁺: the last significant peak; its m/z is the molecular mass. A tiny M+1 peak just after it comes from ¹³C. Mass alone doesn't give the structure: isomers share it.
3. Base peak: the tallest peak, set to 100%. Often a stable fragment, often not M⁺.
4. Gap from M⁺ to a fragment = the neutral piece lost: 15 CH₃, 17 OH, 29 C₂H₅ or CHO, 45 COOH. Cl: two peaks 2 apart, about 3 : 1. Br: about 1 : 1.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the molecule becomes an ion: an electron is knocked off (2). Why ions sort by mass: the field bends light ions more (2). Why neutral pieces are missing: only charged particles are steered (2, 6). Why m/z reads as mass: charge 1+ (3). Why M⁺ equals the molecular mass: the electron's mass is tiny (4). Why the M+1 bump: carbon-13 (4). Why M⁺ can't separate isomers: same mass (5). Why a gap is the lost piece: the neutral piece isn't detected (6). Why 29 is ambiguous: C₂H₅ and CHO weigh the same (7).
