### 2. four-verbs
> Here's what NESA's glossary says, in short. **Analyse** means break something into parts, show how they connect, and work out what follows. **Assess** means judge the value, quality, outcomes, results or size of something. **Evaluate** means judge against criteria, or work out the value. **Justify** means give support for an argument or a conclusion. Three of them end in a **judgement**, a call you make or support. All four need **reasons**.

### 5. three-parts
> An assess or evaluate answer needs three parts. A **verdict**: your judgement, how good or how suitable. **Criteria**: what you're judging by. Here that's safety, what an excess does, and knowing when to stop. And **evidence**: the details from the question that show each one. Here's the memory aid: no verdict without a reason, and no reason without **evidence**.

### 16. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, hold about 50 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Analyse: break into parts, connect them, work out implications. Assess: judge value, quality, outcomes, results or size. Evaluate: judge against criteria, or determine value. Justify: give support for an argument or conclusion (NESA glossary, paraphrased).
2. An assess or evaluate answer has three parts: the verdict, the criteria you judge by, and evidence from the question for each. Finish with the verdict, linked to your reasons.
3. Evaluate: judge against criteria; weighing strengths against limitations is the safe habit (HSCScience tip). Assess: say how much or how good, and support it. Justify: support the argument or conclusion with reasons from the case. Analyse: show the parts, their relationships and what follows.
4. A verdict on its own earns little, and reasons without a verdict can miss the top marks. Give both.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *No verdict without a reason, no reason without evidence.*

**Worked question (Chemistry, evaluate, 4 marks).** "About 50 mL of 1 mol L-1 hydrochloric acid has been spilled on a laboratory bench. Two substances are available to neutralise it. [Table] Hazard: CaCO3 powder low; 1 mol L-1 NaOH(aq) corrosive to skin and eyes. Solubility in water: insoluble; soluble. pH of the mixture if too much is added: about 8; above 12. What is seen during neutralisation: fizzing, which stops when the acid is used up; no visible change. Speed of neutralisation: slower; fast. Evaluate the use of calcium carbonate, rather than sodium hydroxide solution, to neutralise this spill. (4 marks)"
- Annotated reading (scene 4): verb "Evaluate" (highlighter: judge against criteria, then decide); "(4 marks)" (badge); constraint "rather than sodium hydroxide solution" (teal brackets: weigh one against the other); the five row labels underlined as evidence.
- Model answer (verdict, three criteria with evidence, a weighed limitation, the linked verdict):
  1. (Verdict) Calcium carbonate is the more suitable choice for neutralising this spill.
  2. (Safety) CaCO3 is low hazard, whereas NaOH solution is corrosive to skin and eyes, so CaCO3 is safer to apply during the clean-up.
  3. (Excess) CaCO3 is insoluble, so any excess remains as a solid and the mixture stays near pH 8; excess NaOH would leave a strongly basic, corrosive solution above pH 12.
  4. (Endpoint) CaCO3 fizzes (CO2) as it reacts, and the fizzing stops when the acid is used up, showing when neutralisation is complete; NaOH gives no visible sign, so it is easy to add too much. CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g).
  5. (Limitation weighed, verdict linked) CaCO3 neutralises more slowly and releases CO2, but for a 50 mL spill the CO2 released is small (about 0.6 L), so these matter less than safety and control, and CaCO3 is the better choice.
- Mark ticks (illustrative, 4): safety with evidence; excess with evidence; endpoint with evidence; the supported verdict (limitation weighed and linked to the opening verdict). The opening verdict line earns its tick only when the last line supports it.
- Weak answer shown: "Calcium carbonate is better than sodium hydroxide because it is safer and it is a weak base." Illustrative 1 of 4, for a relevant judgement. It loses 3: criteria not shown with evidence from the table, no limitation weighed.

**Drill 1 (Chemistry, justify, 2 marks).** "A buffer solution with a pH of about 4.8 is needed. Two weak acids are available, each with its conjugate base. [Table] ethanoic acid, CH3COOH / ethanoate ion, CH3COO-: pKa 4.74; ammonium ion, NH4+ / ammonia, NH3: pKa 9.25. Justify which weak acid, with its conjugate base, should be used to make this buffer. (2 marks)"
- Model answer:
  1. Use ethanoic acid and ethanoate. A buffer resists pH change best when its pH is close to the weak acid's pKa, because the weak acid and its conjugate base are then present in similar amounts, so it can neutralise both added acid and added base.
  2. pKa 4.74 is within 0.1 of the required pH 4.8, whereas pKa 9.25 is about 4.5 units away; at pH 4.8 an NH4+/NH3 mixture would be almost entirely NH4+, with almost no NH3 to react with added acid.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** In the content: why calcium carbonate's excess is harmless (it is insoluble, so excess stays solid and the pH stays near 8), why fizzing signals the end (CO2 forms only while acid remains), why a buffer works best near its pKa (similar amounts of acid and conjugate base), why a silent substitution leaves the protein unchanged (more than one codon for an amino acid) and why a nonsense substitution shortens it (a stop codon ends translation). In the skill: why a verdict alone earns little (no criteria or evidence), why reasons without a verdict miss marks (the judgement criterion is not met), and why a fair evaluation weighs a limitation (looking both ways is advice, not the glossary meaning).
- **Science (to check):**
  - CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Excess CaCO3 in water open to the air settles near pH 8.3; while CO2 from the reaction is still dissolved the mixture is closer to neutral. "About 8" is the fair round value for the card (Revision 1's "about 9" was high: only a sealed, CO2-free suspension reaches about 9 to 10). The teaching point is "only mildly basic". Excess 1 mol L-1 NaOH mixed into the spill would give pH above 12 for any substantial excess.
  - Calcium carbonate powder is low hazard (a mild dust irritant at most); 1 mol L-1 NaOH is corrosive (skin and eye damage). Both neutralisations release heat; NaOH's is larger per mole of acid. Heat is not on the card and not needed.
  - Laboratory acid-spill kits commonly use a carbonate or hydrogen carbonate because of the visible fizz and self-limiting excess. The video does not claim a standard; it judges from the card.
  - Ethanoic acid pKa 4.74 (from Ka 1.8 x 10^-5, as taught in C10 and C11; the literature value 4.76 is not used, so the series stays consistent). NH4+ pKa 9.25 (Ka 5.6 x 10^-10, as in C11's attempt A1; Kb 1.8 x 10^-5 would give 9.26). At pH 4.8, [NH3]/[NH4+] = 10^(4.8 - 9.25), about 1 to 28,000.
  - GAA and GAG both code for glutamic acid; UAA is a stop codon. A silent substitution leaves the amino acid sequence unchanged (author note: codon-usage or splicing effects exist but are beyond Year 12 and are not needed).
