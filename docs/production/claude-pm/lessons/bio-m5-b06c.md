### 1. hook (four letters, twenty building blocks)
> Last time, a gene was copied into mRNA, a message written in just **four** bases, A, U, C and G. But your proteins are built from **twenty** different amino acids. So how does a four-letter message spell out a chain made of twenty kinds of building block? The answer is in how the message is read, and in a small molecule that acts as a **translator**.

### 2. why three bases
> Suppose each base stood for one amino acid. Four bases could name only **four**. Pairs of bases do better: four times four is **sixteen**, still not enough. Groups of three give four times four times four, which is **sixty-four**, more than enough for twenty. So the code is read in threes, the **codons** you met last time, one after another with no gaps. With sixty-four codons for twenty amino acids, most amino acids have more than one codon, and three codons mean stop.

### 3. the ribosome
> This reading is called **translation**, and it happens at a **ribosome**, in the cytoplasm. A ribosome has two parts, a **small subunit** and a **large subunit**, built from proteins and a kind of RNA called ribosomal RNA, or **rRNA**. The ribosome holds the mRNA and moves along it one codon at a time, but it doesn't choose the amino acids. For that it needs another RNA.

### 4. tRNA, the adaptor
> That RNA is **transfer RNA**, or tRNA, a short strand of RNA folded into a compact shape. At one end it carries one particular **amino acid**. At the other end it has three exposed bases, the **anticodon**, which pairs with a codon on the mRNA, using the same rules: A with U, C with G. So tRNA is the **adaptor** between two languages: one end matches the code, and the other holds the amino acid. There are many kinds of tRNA, and enzymes load each kind **only** with its own amino acid.

### 5. codon meets anticodon (direction)
> Pairing only works if the two strands run in **opposite directions**, just as in DNA. So a codon reading G A A from its 5' end pairs with an anticodon reading C U U from its **3' end**. Written the tRNA's own way, 5' to 3', that anticodon is U U C. Keep the ends on every sequence, and you won't confuse the two.

### 6. reading the codon chart
> Which amino acid does each codon give? The answer is the **codon chart**, worked out by scientists in the 1960s. Always look up the **mRNA codon**, never the anticodon or the DNA. Take G A A. Find the first base, G, down the side. Find the second base, A, across the top. In that box, find the third base, A. So G A A codes for **glutamate**, written Glu. And A U G, the **start codon**, codes for methionine, Met.

### 7a. start

### 7c. repeat, then stop

The result is Met, Glu, Ala, a chain of amino acids called a **polypeptide**.

### 9. why both RNAs matter
> So how important are the two RNAs? mRNA carries the gene's sequence out to the ribosome, and its codons set the **order** of the amino acids. tRNA is the molecule that links each codon to its amino acid, so it makes the code **readable**. Each is essential. Without mRNA, there's no message at the ribosome. Without the right tRNAs, codons can't become amino acids.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Translation happens at ribosomes in the cytoplasm. The ribosome (small and large subunits, protein and rRNA) reads the mRNA one codon (three bases) at a time from its 5' end.
- Each tRNA carries one specific amino acid. Its anticodon pairs antiparallel with the codon: codon 5' GAA 3', anticodon 3' CUU 5'.
- Start at AUG (methionine). Each round: a matching tRNA arrives, rRNA forms a peptide bond, the ribosome moves one codon. A stop codon adds no amino acid; the polypeptide is released, then folds.
- mRNA carries the sequence and sets the order; tRNA links each codon to its amino acid. Both are essential.
- Use the codon chart with mRNA codons only.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (16 Oct) against the standard genetic code (the ported legacy table, cross-checked codon by codon), standard texts (Alberts et al.; Campbell Biology) and the sources named.
- **Codons used (standard code, all checked with the ported `codonToAA` and against the standard table):** AUG Met (start), GAA Glu, GCU Ala, UAA stop, AAG Lys, CCU Pro, UGA stop, UAC Tyr, UUC Phe, GGA Gly, ACU Thr, GUA Val (scene 9 case), GGU, GGC, GGA, GGG all Gly (scene 2), UAG stop.
- **Worked message.** 5' AUG GAA GCU UAA 3': Met, Glu, Ala, stop. Anticodons: AUG 3' UAC 5'; GAA 3' CUU 5' (= 5' UUC 3'); GCU 3' CGA 5' (= 5' AGC 3'). Site's own pair (AUG GAA with UAC CUU) and example (5' GCA 3' with 3' CGU 5') are both reproduced with ends.
- **Attempt 1.** Template 3' TAC TTC GGA ACT 5'. Pairs: T-A, A-U, C-G; T-A, T-A, C-G; G-C, G-C, A-U; A-U, C-G, T-A. mRNA 5' AUG AAG CCU UGA 3'. Met, Lys, Pro, stop: three amino acids. Second codon 5' AAG 3', anticodon 3' UUC 5' = 5' CUU 3'. Wrong method (DNA letters with T to U): UAC UUC GGA ACU = Tyr, Phe, Gly, Thr (all in the excerpt, checked). Anticodon looked up (UUC) = Phe (in the excerpt).
- **Why three.** 4, 16, 64 combinations; 20 amino acids; 61 codons for amino acids and 3 stops; 18 of the 20 amino acids have more than one codon (Met and Trp have one each), so "most" is correct.
- **Ribosome.** Two subunits of rRNA and protein; the peptide bond is catalysed by rRNA in the large subunit (the ribosome is a ribozyme; Nissen et al. 2000, Science 289:920). Free ribosomes and ribosomes on rough ER in eukaryotes: correct.
- **Initiation.** In eukaryotes the small subunit binds near the 5' cap with the initiator tRNA and scans to the first AUG; the large subunit then joins. The narration's order (small subunit grips near the 5' end, slides to AUG, Met-tRNA pairs, large subunit closes) was a simplification; Revision 2 narration now puts the methionine tRNA on the small subunit before it slides to AUG, which matches eukaryotic scanning (science check finding 1). In bacteria the small subunit binds at a ribosome-binding sequence just before AUG instead of scanning (author note; the video's cell is eukaryotic).
- **Elongation.** The incoming aminoacyl-tRNA enters the A site; the peptidyl transferase centre transfers the growing chain onto the new amino acid; translocation moves the ribosome one codon 5' to 3'; the empty tRNA exits (E site) and is recharged by its aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. The "two slots" drawing is the P and A sites; the E site is shown as the tRNA leaving.
- **Termination.** No tRNA recognises UAA, UAG or UGA in the standard code; a release factor (a protein) binds the stop codon and the polypeptide is released; the subunits separate. Correct.
- **Polypeptide length.** Typical human proteins are a few hundred amino acids long (median about 375 in reviews of the human proteome). "Usually hundreds long" is safe.
- **Streptomycin.** Aminoglycoside that binds the bacterial 30S subunit at the decoding site, causing misreading (mistranslation) and also blocking initiation; bactericidal. It does not bind effectively to eukaryotic cytoplasmic 80S ribosomes. Mitochondrial ribosomes resemble bacterial ones; aminoglycoside ototoxicity is linked to mitochondrial 12S rRNA (the A1555G variant increases susceptibility), and streptomycin has been shown to bind the mitoribosome small subunit ([PMC6011804](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6011804); [UNC systematic review of A1555G](https://cdr.lib.unc.edu/concern/scholarly_works/zc77ss072?locale=en)). The stem's "binds to the small subunit ... makes them misread codons" and the feedback's "can bind" to mitochondrial ribosomes match this; a 2010 letter disputes one structural detail of the mitochondrial binding site, so the learner copy says "can affect" and "can bind", not how. Human mitochondrial ribosomes make only 13 proteins (all for the respiratory chain); the cytoplasmic ribosomes make the rest, which is why the stem says the patient's cells keep making "most of their proteins normally". Mitochondrial effects are linked to aminoglycoside ototoxicity (hearing loss), not spoken. Cell death from aminoglycosides involves more than misfolded enzymes (mistranslated membrane proteins, membrane damage); "many fold wrongly and do not work ... so it dies" is a fair HSC-level chain (author note).
- **The 1960s decoding.** Nirenberg and Matthaei (1961) decoded UUU as phenylalanine with poly-U RNA; the table was complete by about 1966. "Scientists worked this out in the 1960s" is correct.
