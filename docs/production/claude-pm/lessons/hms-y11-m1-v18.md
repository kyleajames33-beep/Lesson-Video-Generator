### 2. localisation, and why councils matter
> The goals were agreed by national governments, but the United Nations itself points out that most of the targets can't be reached without local government. Look at what a council controls: parks, pools and lighting, footpaths and crossings, planning near schools, libraries and youth spaces, community grants, and food and water safety. So **localisation** means adapting the goals to local priorities, and reporting local data against them. It runs both ways. Downwards, a council asks what the goals mean here. Upwards, it reports local results, and some Australian councils publish their own review.

### 3. awareness is not localisation
> So, the school. The fair verdict has two parts. Awareness has real value: students learned, the school signalled that these issues matter, and money went somewhere useful. But it changed nothing on the second list, and that's where its own students lose health. It treated the goals as a topic, not a tool. Awareness starts from the goals, and is measured by who turned up. Localisation starts from a local need, and is measured by change from a baseline. And labelling existing activity with goal numbers, when no decision changed, is called **SDG-washing**. The test is simple: would any decision have been different without the goals?

### 4. six steps from a need to a proposal
> Here's the method, and you start at step one, not step three. One: establish the need, with the community, and reach beyond the students already in sport or leadership. Two: name the goals it sits under, with mechanisms, not icons. Three: find the assets before the deficits: a hall, a club, a bus, a trusted organisation. Four: design across at least three Ottawa action areas, with who acts and roughly what it costs. Five: set a baseline and an indicator, reported for the groups most at risk. Six: say how it survives. The goals tell you what matters and how to measure it. The Charter tells you what kind of action to take.

### 6. three features of a good proposal
> Three features make this proposal strong. Every row names who acts, and most of them aren't the school: the council, a transport authority, a community organisation, a club. And two rows are built to outlive the funding: the coaching passes to a club, and the reference group gets a standing place. That's why it would still exist in three years.

### 7. judging a proposal, including your own
> Before you submit a proposal, ask five questions. Who decided? If young people were surveyed but had no vote, that's consultation. Who does it reach, and who does it miss? Programs run through schools and clubs miss young people who've left both. What changes, and how would you know: a baseline, an indicator, results by group? Who acts outside health? And what survives? Then a sixth, harder question. Is this the biggest thing you could do, or just the easiest? Lighting a ground is popular. Changing a bus timetable isn't, and it may matter more.
