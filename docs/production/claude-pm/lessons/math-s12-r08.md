### 3. units first, then the volume
> The area is in square metres, so the depth must be in metres too. A millimetre is a thousandth of a metre, so divide by a thousand. Twenty-five millimetres is zero point zero two five **metres**. The roof's area is twelve times eight, ninety-six square metres. So the volume is ninety-six times zero point zero two five, two point four cubic **metres**. A cubic metre is a box one metre each way, and it holds a thousand litres. So that's two thousand four hundred **litres**, from one storm, on one roof. Writing twenty-five millimetres as zero point two five metres is the classic slip. That divides by a hundred, and it makes ten times too much **water**.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 7; original, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A farm shed has a flat roof 14 m long and 9 m wide. ## Memory aid check

- **"Rain is a very flat box: V = Ah."** with key line **"mm ÷ 1000 = m. m³ × 1000 = L."** From the site's card 06 ("the water is just a very flat box"), with the syllabus letters (MS-37). Meaning, as said in scene 6: rain that stands at an even depth over an area fills a box with that area as its base and the depth as its height, so its volume is area times depth; the depth in millimetres becomes metres by dividing by 1000, and cubic metres become litres by multiplying by 1000. Assumptions checked: "if none drained away" (the model assumes all the rain on the area is caught, which the questions state: "all the rain runs into the tank"); the area is the area seen from above (a sloping roof catches rain over its plan area, so "seen from above" is on every roof card); A in square metres. It helps a new case: 18 mm on a 60 m² carport is 60 × 0.018 = 1.08 m³ = 1080 L.
- **Considered, not used:** "Millimetres are tiny, metres are big" (vague; it doesn't say ÷ 1000).
