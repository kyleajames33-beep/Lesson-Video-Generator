### 2. a muscle can only pull
> Of the three kinds of muscle, cardiac muscle works the heart, and smooth muscle lines vessels, airways and the gut, both without your control. Skeletal muscle is the kind you control. It attaches to bone through a **tendon**, and when it contracts, it shortens and pulls the bone towards itself. It can't push the bone back. So muscles work in opposing pairs. The biceps pulls the forearm up, and only the triceps can pull it straight again, unless gravity does that job for free. Remember that, because it solves the bag.

### 4. slow and fast twitch, and why the traits cluster
> Every muscle is a mix of **fibres**, the muscle cells, in two main types. **Slow twitch** fibres contract slowly with low force, but resist fatigue for hours. **Fast twitch** fibres contract quickly with high force, but tire within seconds to minutes. Now notice that the slow twitch traits all point the same way. These fibres work with oxygen, so they need capillaries to deliver it, mitochondria to use it, and myoglobin to store it. Fast twitch fibres work mainly without oxygen, so they need less of each.

### 5. which fibres, for which job
> Light effort recruits slow twitch fibres first, and fast twitch fibres join as the force needed rises. So a marathon runner relies on slow twitch fibres, and a sprinter or a weightlifter on fast twitch. The proportion in each person is largely inherited. Training doesn't turn slow fibres into fast ones, but it does improve the fibres you have.

### 6. three ways to contract
> Now, three ways a muscle can contract. In an **isotonic concentric** contraction, the muscle shortens under tension, because its force beats the resistance, like lifting a biceps curl. In an **isotonic eccentric** contraction, the muscle lengthens while still under tension, because the muscle lets the resistance win, slowly, while still pulling against it. It isn't switching off. It's braking, like lowering the curl, or lowering yourself into a chair. And in an **isometric** contraction, the muscle is tense but doesn't change length, so the joint doesn't move, like holding a plank.

### 7. the bag, solved
> That solves the bag. As you lower it, your elbow extends, but gravity is doing the extending for free. The biceps works eccentrically, braking the fall. So don't ask which muscle makes that joint action. Ask what is resisting the movement. When gravity makes the movement, the working muscle is the one on the other side, lengthening to control it. A muscle can brake a heavier load than it can lift, and eccentric work causes the most soreness a day or two later.

### 8. agonist, antagonist, stabiliser
> Because muscles only pull, they work in three roles. The **agonist**, or prime mover, contracts to produce the movement. The **antagonist**, on the opposite side of the joint, relaxes and lengthens to let it happen. And a **stabiliser** contracts, usually without moving, to hold a bone still, so the agonist has a firm base to pull from. These are roles, not muscles. Curling up, the biceps is the agonist and the triceps the antagonist. Pushing a weight away, they swap. But lowering a curl slowly doesn't swap them. The biceps is still the agonist, now working eccentrically.

### 9. both systems, in one squat
> Put both systems together in a squat. Going down, the hip and knee flex, and the quadriceps work eccentrically, braking against gravity. Coming up, the quadriceps are the agonist. They contract concentrically and pull on the tibia through the patellar tendon, so the knee extends. The gluteus maximus extends the hip, the hamstrings are the antagonist at the knee, though at the hip they help the gluteus maximus, and the trunk muscles hold still as stabilisers. And because the knee is a hinge, that pull can only produce extension. The muscles supply the force, and the skeleton decides what it can do.
