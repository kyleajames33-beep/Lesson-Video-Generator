### 2. turn the claim into its numbers
> Start by turning the claim back into numbers. Four out of five of twenty-five volunteers is twenty. So twenty of twenty-five said they slept better. That's a relative frequency, twenty over twenty-five, zero point **eight**. But it comes from a sample, the people actually asked. Twenty-five is a small sample, so it could be well off. And these people were volunteers. They chose to take part, probably because they already wanted the app to work. That's called a self-selected sample. A random sample picks people by chance from everyone, so it's much more likely to look like the whole **group**. These volunteers may not. And who ran the trial? The app's own **maker**, who wants you to buy it. That doesn't make the claim false, but it's a reason to look harder.

### 3. the media: halved, or one in a hundred?
> Now a headline. Screen protectors halve cracked screens! Say two per cent of phones without one crack each year, and one per cent with one. Two down to one is halved, so the headline is **true**. That's a relative change, compared with where it started. But the absolute change, the plain difference, is just one percentage point, one phone in every **hundred**. Halved sounds huge. One in a hundred sounds much smaller. And the same word, halved, could mean one fewer, or twenty fewer in every hundred. So always ask, halved from **what**?
