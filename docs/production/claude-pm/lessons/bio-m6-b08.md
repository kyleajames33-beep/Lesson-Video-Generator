### 2. two gene pools, and how to count them
> Start with their gene pools. A gene pool is every allele in a population, and an allele's frequency is its share of all the copies of that gene. Each panther carries two copies, so twenty-five panthers hold fifty copies. If ten of those copies are one allele, its frequency is ten out of fifty, or zero point two. The Florida pool was small and cut off, because the pumas that once lived between Florida and Texas had been wiped out. And it had lost alleles that the Texas pool still had. So what changes a gene pool?

### 3. genetic drift: chance
> The first answer is chance. Each generation, only some animals happen to breed, and each parent passes on one allele of each pair by chance. So the next generation's alleles are a random sample of this one's. That's **genetic drift**, a change in allele frequency by chance. It's like tossing a coin for each copy passed on. Toss it ten times, and seven heads isn't unusual. Toss it a thousand times, and you'll land close to half. And each generation's frequency becomes the starting point for the next, so the changes add up. So in a small population, drift can make an allele common, or lose it completely, whether it helps or not.

Visual: one basin with 40 rings, half open, half grey (tag "20 animals"). On "only some animals happen to breed" a few animal icons above the basin light; on "by chance" `SampleScoop` lifts a random handful of rings (computed, seeded) into a new basin below (tag "next generation: a random sample"); its `FillBar` reads a different value (computed, for example 0.6). ### 4. bottleneck and founder effect
> Drift is strongest when numbers crash. Hunting and habitat loss squeezed the panthers down to a few dozen, a **bottleneck**. The survivors carried only a sample of the old gene pool, and in so few animals, harmful alleles became common just by chance. With so many relatives breeding, more kittens got two copies of a harmful allele, so its effect showed. The same thing happens when a few individuals leave to start a new population, called the **founder effect**. Either way, few individuals means the gene pool is a random sample.

### 5. mutation: one new copy
> Could mutation repair the damage? A mutation adds a brand-new allele, but only as a single copy. In twenty-five panthers, that's one copy in fifty. In a population of a million, it's one in two million. Mutations at any one gene are rare, so rebuilding lost variation this way would take a very long time. And in a small population, drift loses most new alleles before they spread. Mutation is the source of new alleles, but on its own it changes a gene pool slowly.

### 6. gene flow: alleles arrive
> What helped was **gene flow**, alleles moving between populations when individuals move and breed. The Texas females carried alleles the Florida pool had lost, so their kittens had more variety, with fewer copies of the harmful alleles. Kinked tails became less common, the kittens survived better, and the population grew. Gene flow didn't make any new alleles. It moved existing ones, and it made the two gene pools more alike. Animals that leave a population take their alleles out, too.

### 7. which matters most?
> So which process matters most? It depends on the population. In a small, isolated population, drift usually has the biggest short-term effect. Where populations swap many migrants, gene flow can change frequencies fast, and keep the pools similar. And only mutation makes genuinely new alleles, so it matters most over long times. Selection is different again. It changes frequencies for a reason, because some alleles help survival or breeding, while drift has no direction.

### 10. decision rule and handoff
> So when a question shows a gene pool changing, ask what happened to the alleles. Did a brand-new allele appear? That's mutation. Did alleles arrive or leave with moving individuals? ### 11. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Gene pool: all the alleles in a population. Allele frequency = copies of that allele ÷ total copies of the gene (two per individual for an autosomal gene).
- Genetic drift: a random change in allele frequency, because each generation's alleles are a chance sample. Strongest in small populations; it can lose or fix an allele whatever its effect. Bottleneck (a crash to a few survivors) and founder effect (a few individuals start a new population) are drift.
- Mutation: the only source of new alleles. Each starts as one copy (1 in 2N), so mutation alone changes a gene pool slowly, and drift often loses new alleles in small populations.
- Gene flow: alleles move between populations with migrants that breed. It can add alleles a population lacked and makes populations more alike, but creates no new alleles.
- Which matters most depends on the population: drift in small, isolated ones; gene flow where migration is high; mutation over long times. Selection is non-random; drift is random.

Memory: drift is luck, and luck matters more in small groups. Gene flow makes populations alike; drift and selection pull them apart.
