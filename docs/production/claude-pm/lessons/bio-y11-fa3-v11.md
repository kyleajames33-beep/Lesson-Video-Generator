### 3. why a leaf can't seal itself
> Because it would starve. With the stomata shut, carbon dioxide can't get in, so photosynthesis stops making sugar. The stomata are the door. Water leaves by it, and carbon dioxide comes in by it. So in dry places, natural selection didn't favour sealed leaves. It favoured structures that cut water loss, but still let some carbon dioxide in.

### 4. the cuticle, and a common idea
> Start with the surface. A common idea is that water leaks out evenly all over a leaf, so a waxy coat makes little difference. In fact, while the stomata are open, most water leaves through them. So on a hot afternoon, when the stomata close, very little water escapes anywhere.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (3 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Opening (scene 1).** A thin soft leaf wilting within about 30 minutes in sun while a eucalypt leaf stays firm for hours: illustrative (the clock chip carries "illustrative"); the site says "wilts within minutes" for a fern leaf. Detached privet leaves in sun wilt within tens of minutes; acceptable as illustrative.
- **Transpiration and stomata (scene 2).** Water evaporates from mesophyll cell walls into air spaces and diffuses out through the stomata; stomata are mostly on the lower surface in many dicot leaves (true; eucalypt adult leaves are often amphistomatous, see site error 4). Carbon dioxide enters through the stomata (true).
- **Cuticle (scene 4).** With stomata open, most transpiration is stomatal (typically about 90 per cent or more); cuticular transpiration is a small fraction and becomes the main loss when stomata close (standard; Riederer and Schreiber 2001). A thick cuticle lowers cuticular conductance (true, though wax composition matters more than thickness alone; author note, not raised). Eucalypts have thick, waxy cuticles (true for many species).
- **Sunken stomata and hairs (scene 5).** Hakea: many species have stomata sunk in grooves or pits; Banksia: stomata in hair-lined crypts on the lower surface in many species (for example Banksia serrata, B. marginata) (true; science check to confirm the named genus-level wording "many Hakea and Banksia leaves"). The mechanism (a still, humid layer over the pore increases diffusion resistance and lowers the gradient at the pore) is standard. Note: the quantitative effect of crypts is debated in research (they may matter more for other functions); Year 11 standard explanation kept; author note only.
- **Spinifex (scene 6).** Triodia leaves are tightly rolled (involute) and the stomata are confined to grooves; in many species the stomata lie in furrows on the abaxial (outer) surface, protected by papillae or hairs, while some ("soft") species differ. The narration says "the stomata sit in deep, narrow grooves, out of the wind" (true for both arrangements) and does not say "inside the roll"; the grey tag "stomata position varies between species" covers the site's "stomata face into an enclosed, humid channel". Science check to confirm.
- **Hanging leaves (scenes 7, 8).** Adult leaves of many eucalypts hang vertically (pendulous, isobilateral), so the blade is edge-on to the high midday sun and intercepts less radiation, lowering leaf temperature and transpiration demand (site; standard, for example King 1997). True. "absorbs less and stays cooler": true at midday. Small, narrow and needle-like leaves reduce surface area (and, by being narrow, thin the boundary layer, which aids cooling; not raised).
- **Cost (scene 9).** Sunken stomata, hairs and rolled leaves increase diffusion resistance for carbon dioxide as well as water (true). Vertical leaves intercept less light at midday (true; they intercept more in the morning and afternoon, not raised). Cuticular conductance to carbon dioxide is negligible (true), so cuticle thickness costs little gas exchange (it costs carbon to build; not raised).
- **Selection (scene 10).** Heritable variation in leaf traits; drought selection over generations (standard). "Nothing grows an adaptation because it needs one" (v10's hook): true (individuals can show some plastic change in cuticle and leaf thickness with light and water, which is acclimatisation in v10's sense, not evolution; author note). Rainforest plants with thin cuticles and broad leaves suited to abundant water and low light (site SA Q3).
- **Attempt 1 (12a).** Allocasuarina and Casuarina: leaves reduced to whorls of tiny scale-like teeth; photosynthesis in green branchlets (cladodes); stomata in longitudinal grooves between ridges, protected by hairs (true; standard botany). Many species grow in dry and semi-arid Australia (for example desert she-oak, Allocasuarina decaisneana) (true).

Finish with two exam-style questions and brief feedback. All questions are written for HSCScience in the HSC style; they are not official NESA questions.
