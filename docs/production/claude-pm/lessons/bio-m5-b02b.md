## Decisions

- **Split proposal (decided here, for Claude to confirm).** The route lists B2b as "delivered as six focused videos": flower to seed, strawberry runner, mould, yeast, bacteria, protists. Mould already exists (take02, rendered). A runner pilot (about 4 minutes, silent design pilot, owner review pending) already exists. Putting all of plants, fungi, bacteria and protists into one video would give four organism groups and about nine mechanisms in 6 to 10 minutes, which breaks the one-question rule and the reading-load rule. So:
  - **B2b (this design): flowering plants.** One thread: how a flower makes a seed (pollination, pollen tube, fertilisation, embryo, seed and fruit), then the other plant route (vegetative propagation, runner a one-line recall of B1), then when each suits. Page order follows the site (flowering plants first). About 1,307 words.
  - **B2c (not designed here): yeast budding, bacterial binary fission, protists.** One thread: how one cell becomes two without any joining ("one cell, two cells"), with mould spores recalled in two sentences, bacterial conjugation as the trap (DNA exchange is not reproduction), and named protist examples with organism limits. Target about 7 minutes. B2c needs new art (nothing exists for bacteria or protists).
  - Handoff chain: B2a, B2b, B2c, then b03a (mammals). The mould video and the runner pilot remain separate releasable pieces and are recalled, not re-taught.
- **Site alignment:** mirrors `site-extracts/bio-m5-l03.md` section 1 (flowering plants): pollination (anther to stigma, wind, water or animals), pollination is not fertilisation, pollen tube, fertilisation in the ovule gives a zygote, ovule becomes seed, ovary becomes fruit in many species, seeds protect, store food and help dispersal. Vegetative propagation: runners, bulbs, tubers, cuttings, offspring usually identical apart from mutation. Condition matching (stable paddock: runners or tubers; new disease: sexual; need to reach new places: seeds). The site's 3-mark "Outline" question is the worked example. Some plants can make seeds without fertilisation, and some can pollinate themselves. These stay in author notes. ## Script and shots

### 1. hook (one plant, two routes)
> A gardener has one strawberry plant, and wants more. There are two ways the plant can make them. It can send a runner along the ground, and a new plant grows at the end. Or it can flower. Now picture a bee landing on one of those flowers and brushing off a little yellow dust, called pollen. Has the plant made a seed yet? Not yet. The seed hasn't started. So how does a flower make a seed, and when is that a better route than a runner? By the end, you'll be able to outline the whole journey in an exam answer.

Visual: the strawberry runner plate (existing, alpha) fills the board; on "runner" the connecting stem highlights with a draw-on stroke from the parent to the daughter. On "Or it can flower" a push-through transition to the strawberry flower plate. On "bee landing" the bee plate flies in over the flower and a few coded yellow pollen specks shake off onto the centre. On "Has the plant made a seed yet?" a hand-drawn question mark and a small empty "seed?" outline appear beside the flower and the board holds still on the question mark for about two seconds before "Not yet." Heading writes on: "How does a flower make a seed?"

### 2a. the male side (stamens, anthers, pollen grain)
> Here is a typical flower, cut in half. The plant has a problem. The cells that will become sperm are in one place, and its egg cells are somewhere else. Around the centre of the flower, the stamens hold the anthers, and the anthers make pollen. Each tiny pollen grain carries the cells that will become sperm. So the male side is up here.

Visual: a switch from the strawberry to the flower cut-away plate (new, see Codex brief), with the words "typical flower" written beside it. Camera starts on the whole flower, then pushes in to the stamens on "stamens", a scribble circle on the anthers on "anthers" and a coded pollen grain pops out of one, with a small plain cell inside it and no sperm dots, on "pollen grain carries the cells that will become sperm". Each name draws on with a leader line as it is spoken, then the leader line fades and the name drops into a **small stacked name key** at the side of the flower (stamen, anther, pollen grain). The key persists through scenes 2b, 3, 4 and 5. Small tag beside the stamen: "STAMEN has MEN in it" (written on, held for scene 12). A pin marker lands on the anthers on "up here".

### 2b. the female side (carpel, stigma, style, ovary, ovules, egg cell)
> The female part is the carpel. Its sticky top is the stigma, and below it a narrow stalk, the style, leads down to the ovary. Inside the ovary are small structures called ovules, and each ovule holds an egg cell. So the egg is buried deep inside the flower. The sperm of a flowering plant can't swim, so how will they ever get there?

Visual: the camera travels down the carpel following the words stigma, style, ovary. Each name draws on with a leader line as it is spoken, then fades into the stacked name key (stigma, style, ovary, ovule, egg), which now holds all the names from 2a and 2b. The shot ends on the ovule close-up crossfade (aligned pair) with the egg cell dot highlighted on "egg cell", then pulls back to the whole flower. On "buried deep" a second pin lands on the egg. On "can't swim" a dotted red measure line runs from the anther pin to the egg pin, with a "no swimming" crossed-out sperm doodle.

### 3. pollination
> The first step is **pollination**. Pollen moves from an anther to a stigma. Plants can't walk, so they use carriers. A bee visits for nectar, and pollen sticks to its hairy body. At the next flower, some of it rubs off onto the stigma. Grasses use the wind instead, and they release huge clouds of light pollen. Some water plants use water. But look at what has happened so far. The pollen has only arrived. The cells that will become sperm are still in the pollen grain, and the egg is still deep inside. So pollination is not fertilisation. It only puts the pollen in the right place.

Visual: the bee flies from flower A to flower B (coded motion path over two copies of the flower plate), yellow specks stuck to its legs, a few drop onto the stigma on "rubs off". Then a grass-flower plate with wind lines (coded) and a pollen cloud drifting on "wind"; a small coded water ripple icon on "water". Each carrier icon (bee, wind, water) carries the tag **"carries pollen"** in one colour (amber). Return to the flower cut-away with the name key in view: pollen grains lit on the stigma, the plain cell still inside each grain (no sperm dots yet), the egg still low in the ovule, a bracket drawn between them labelled "still far apart". Amber doodle on "not fertilisation": "pollination = arrival" (a tick on arrival, a cross on "= seed").

### 4. the pollen tube
> What happens next takes place inside the flower. The pollen grain lands on the sticky stigma and takes in water. Then it grows a thin tube, the **pollen tube**, down through the style, guided by chemical signals from the ovule. The two sperm cells form inside the tube as it grows, and they travel along it, so they are protected, and they don't need to swim. When the tube reaches the ovule, it grows in through a small opening and releases the sperm cells right beside the egg cell. So the tube solves the plant's distance problem. It carries the sperm to the egg.

Visual: camera at the stigma, name key in view. On "takes in water" a few blue droplet doodles move into one grain and the grain swells. On "grows a thin tube" the tube draws on (coded path from the aligned overlay or a coded path, see brief) down the style, the camera following the tip, at an even speed over about 8 seconds. On "guided by chemical signals from the ovule" faint dots drift from the ovule up the channel and the tube tip bends towards them. On "the two sperm cells form inside the tube" the plain cell moves into the tube behind the tip and splits into two sperm dots, which then travel along inside it. On "small opening" the tube enters the ovule and the two sperm dots spill out next to the egg, with a scribble circle round the egg. A "pollen tube" label draws on with a leader line on its word.

### 5. fertilisation and the embryo
> Now comes **fertilisation**. One sperm cell joins the egg cell. That joining makes one new cell, the zygote, the first cell of a new plant. Just as in the first video of this series, one set from the sperm plus one set from the egg make a full two sets. The zygote then divides again and again and grows into a tiny young plant, called an **embryo**. So this is the moment the new plant actually begins, and it happens only because the tube took the sperm to the egg.

Visual: the ovule close-up. One sperm dot moves into the egg and the two dots merge into a single circle labelled "zygote" on that word (the other sperm fades quietly, not mentioned). B1 callback chip "one set + one set = two sets". On "divides again and again" the single circle splits to two, four, then a small cluster that grows to a tiny coded embryo outline (doubling pulses, about 6 seconds). Label "embryo" draws on. A sequence strip starts at the bottom rail: "land" (grain on stigma icon), "tube" (tube icon), "join" (sperm into egg icon); each icon lands on its cue word as the strip is built from scenes 3, 4 and 5.

### 6a. seed and fruit
> After fertilisation, the ovule changes. It becomes a **seed**: a protective package holding the embryo, a store of food and a tough outer coat. The food is there because the embryo has no leaves yet to make its own. In many plants, the ovary around the ovules grows into a **fruit**, like a pea pod, and it protects the seeds inside.

Visual: crossfade pair, ovary cut open with three ovules (plate) to the pod open with three seeds (aligned plate), the ovules becoming seeds on "becomes a seed" and the ovary wall becoming the pod on "grows into a fruit"; the words "pea pod" write on the plate on that phrase, and "fruit" and "seeds inside" draw on with leader lines. Then the bean seed cut-away plate (new, see brief) with three leader lines, each drawn on its word and left in place: "embryo shoot and root" (the small shoot and root at the base), "food store (cotyledons)" (the two large halves) and "seed coat" (the thin outer skin). Sequence strip adds "seed" on "becomes a seed".

### 6b. travelling and waiting (dispersal and germination)
> Seeds are made for waiting and for travelling. The coat protects the embryo from drying out, and wind, water or animals can carry seeds away from the parent. That is seed dispersal, which is a different job from pollination. Later, when it has water, oxygen and a suitable temperature, the embryo germinates, which means it starts to grow roots and leaves.

Visual: the bean seed cut-away stays small at the side as the anchor. The carrier icons (wind, water, animal) return in a second colour (blue-green, not the amber of scene 3) with the tag **"carries seeds"** on "carry seeds away", then "seed dispersal" writes on beside them with a small "not pollination" tick-and-cross against scene 3's tags. Three small chips (water drop, oxygen bubbles, a thermometer) draw on at "water, oxygen and a suitable temperature". On "germinates" the bean seedling plate (new, see brief) fades in over the seed, with the roots drawing downward and the leaves lifting.

### 7. plants without flowers: vegetative propagation
> Plants have a second way of making new plants, with no flowers at all. A piece of the parent grows roots and leaves and becomes a separate plant. This is called **vegetative propagation**. You saw one example at the start, the strawberry runner. An onion bulb grows small new bulbs from the side of the parent bulb. A potato tuber sprouts new shoots from its eyes. And a gardener's cutting grows roots from the cut end of a stem. In every case, no sperm joined an egg. So this is asexual reproduction, and the new plant is a clone of the parent, apart from rare copying errors. It also doesn't need a bee or the wind to carry pollen.

Visual: the strawberry runner plate (recall, a quick draw-on of the connecting stem), then a push-through to a row of three plates, one at a time: onion bulb with small new bulbs, potato tuber with sprouts, **mint cutting in a glass with new roots**. On each name the new part highlights with a scribble circle (small bulb, sprouting eye, new roots). A coded strip "piece of parent, roots and leaves, new plant" runs under each plate. Then a "no sperm, no egg" doodle: sperm and egg dots crossed out. On "clone" the plate duplicates into two identical plants. On "a bee or the wind to carry pollen" a small bee icon and a wind line are crossed out.

### 8. when are seeds the better route?
> So when is making seeds the better route? It depends on the conditions, just as in the first video. In a steady paddock with plenty of water, a plant that is already doing well can spread fast by runners, with no carrier needed. But if a new disease arrives, a field of clones has the same weakness everywhere, and the plants can fail together. Seeds come from sexual reproduction, so the seedlings vary, and some may survive. Seeds can travel far, so the plant can reach new places. Runners are fast and keep the plant alike, close by. Seeds make varied plants, and they can travel far.

Visual: split board "steady paddock" (sun, runner plate spreading, clone row healthy, tick) and "new disease" (the b1 clone row with disease plate: every plant spotted; then the varied seedlings plate with a few circled survivors). On "reach new places" a seed icon arcs over a fence to a bare patch. Small amber tag: "alike and close" under runner, "varied and far" under seed.

### 9. worked outline (building an HSC answer)
> Let's build a model answer. The question: outline sexual reproduction in flowering plants from pollination to seed formation. The command word outline means give the main steps, in order, so we use sequence words. First, pollen is transferred from an anther to a stigma, by wind, water or animals. Then the pollen grain grows a pollen tube down the style to an ovule, carrying sperm cells. Next, a sperm cell joins the egg cell in the ovule. That is fertilisation, and it forms a zygote that develops into an embryo. Finally, the ovule develops into a seed holding the embryo, and in many plants the ovary develops into a fruit.

Visual: the question card writes on, "outline" scribble-underlined with margin note "main steps, in order". Four full written sentence cards build one at a time, each landing on its sequence word ("First", "Then", "Next", "Finally", each underlined in a second colour). Each card carries a small icon from the sequence strip (land, tube, join, seed) and the key terms (pollination, pollen tube, fertilisation, zygote, embryo, seed, fruit) are highlighted as they land. Earlier cards shrink to the top rail and stay. Margin: the site's actual marking guide for this question, three marks of one each, shown as brackets beside the cards: card 1 "1 mark: pollination"; cards 2 and 3 "1 mark: fertilisation" (the tube takes the sperm to the egg, and a sperm joins the egg); the zygote-to-embryo clause and card 4 "1 mark: embryo and seed formation". The brackets appear as each card lands. A small tag reads "marking guide from the site's model answer".

### 13. key notes (quiet)
- Pollination is pollen moving from an anther to a stigma (by wind, water or animals). Fertilisation is a sperm cell joining an egg cell in an ovule to make a zygote. Pollination is not fertilisation.
- The pollen grain grows a pollen tube down the style to an ovule. The tube carries the sperm cells to the egg.
- The zygote develops into an embryo. The ovule becomes a seed (embryo, food store and coat). In many plants the ovary becomes a fruit.
- Vegetative propagation (runners, bulbs, tubers, cuttings) is asexual: a piece of the parent grows into a new plant, so the offspring are usually clones.
- Seeds come from sexual reproduction, so they vary and can travel. Clones are fast and alike, but share the same weaknesses.
- Memory aid (large): Land, tube, join, seed.

## Memory aid and assumptions

Aid: **"Land, tube, join, seed"** (written for this lesson) with the bank's **"Pollination is not fertilisation"** trap and **"STAMEN has MEN in it"**.
Assumptions checked:
- "STAMEN has MEN in it": the stamen (anther plus filament) is the male part and makes pollen. The aid does not say "carpel is female" in a way that suggests all flowers have both (some flowers have only one kind). Not stated in learner copy.
- "Land, tube, join, seed": the order is correct for flowering plants. It leaves out fruit, which is "in many plants" in the notes. "Join" means a sperm cell with an egg cell, one pair, and is not the whole of double fertilisation (author note only). "Seed" covers the ovule becoming a seed.
- "Pollination is arrival, fertilisation is joining" is true for flowering plants. It would not be true as stated for the plants outside this video (they are not mentioned).
- Self- and cross-pollination are not taught here (scene 7 of revision 2 was cut). Attempt item one states that the bagged flowers have no pollen of their own (anthers removed), because many flowers can drop their own pollen on their own stigma, which would make the item disputable.
- The mnemonic for the stamen is optional. Keep it only if it fits the scene 12 time budget.

## Review notes for Claude (author, not learner copy)

- Finding 3 (fact check): the strawberry cut-away is replaced by a new bean seed cut-away. In a bean seed the two cotyledons are the food store and are also part of the embryo with the shoot and root; the labels say "embryo (shoot and root)" and "food store (cotyledons)", which is a school-level split. The strawberry plate is not used anywhere in the video except the opening hook (runner and flower).
- Strawberry is not used for the ovary-to-fruit step. A strawberry's red "fruit" is mostly the swollen flower base, and each seed-like dot is a small fruit containing a seed, so the clean "ovary becomes fruit" story would be false or confusing.
