### 2. energy travels, the particles stay
> Something else. Watch one glider, marked in purple. When the pulse arrives, it moves forward a little, then back, and it ends up right where it started. Every glider does the same. They each **oscillate**, moving back and forth about a home position, called the rest position. Yet at the far end, the last glider knocks over this card **flag**. So something did arrive: energy, the ability to make things move. That's what a wave is. It carries energy through a material, called the medium, while each particle of the medium stays near **home**.

### 3. why the energy moves along
> So how does the energy get from one end to the other? Each glider can only push or pull its neighbour. The launcher pushes glider one. Its spring squeezes, and that pushes glider **two**, a moment later. Glider two squeezes the next spring and pushes glider three, and so on down the row. Each glider passes its motion on, then gets pulled back to its rest position by the springs. The motion is handed along, and the energy goes with it, but no glider makes the **journey**.

### 4. longitudinal: oscillating along the energy's path

When particles oscillate parallel to the energy's direction, it's called a longitudinal **wave**. A long spring makes a better model, a simpler thing that moves the way the real particles do. Push its end in and out, and the coils oscillate along the spring. Some regions get **squeezed** together, and some get stretched apart, and those regions travel along while the coils stay near home.

### 5. transverse: oscillating across the energy's path
> Now a rope, fixed to the same driver, turned to move up and down. The energy travels along the rope, to the right. But a ribbon tied to the rope moves straight up and down, across that **direction**. When particles oscillate perpendicular, at right angles, to the energy's direction, it's a transverse wave. Why does the shape travel? The rope is stretched, so it's under tension, a pull along its length. When one piece of rope lifts, the tension drags the next piece up after it, and then pulls the first piece back **down**. Once again, each piece passes its motion on and stays near home.

### 6. one spring, both types
> Here's the surprise. The same spring can carry both kinds. Shake its end sideways, and the coils move sideways while the energy goes **along**: transverse. Push and pull its end, and the coils move along the spring: **longitudinal**. Nothing about the spring changed. What changed is the direction the source moves, compared with the direction the energy travels. So never decide a wave's type from a wavy drawing. Compare the particle's motion with the energy's **direction**.

### 8. classifying a wave step by step
> Transverse. East and west is at right angles to **north**. Here's the method to use every time, with a rope laid out east to west. A piece of tape on it moves only up and down. Step one: find the particle's motion first. The tape moves **vertically**. Step two: find the energy's direction. The energy travels along the rope, east to **west**. Step three: compare them. Vertical is perpendicular to east and west, so this is a transverse **wave**.

Visual: on "north" a right-angle mark appears between the teal arrow and the energy arrow; the "transverse?" chip fills. Grey tag "site worked example".

### 9. why sound in air is longitudinal
> Why does it matter? To carry a longitudinal wave, the particles only have to push back when they're **squeezed**. Solids, liquids and gases all do that. A transverse wave needs more. When one layer slides sideways, it has to drag its neighbours back, like the rope does through its tension. Solids can do that, but layers of air or water just slide past each other **freely**. So sound in air is longitudinal. Earthquakes send both kinds of wave through the Earth, but the transverse ones can't get through its liquid outer **core**.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. A wave transfers energy through a medium without transferring the medium. Each particle oscillates about its rest position and stays there.
2. The energy moves because each particle pushes or pulls its neighbour a moment later.
3. Transverse wave: particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (a rope or string; a spring shaken sideways).
4. Longitudinal wave: particles oscillate parallel to the direction of energy transfer, making squeezed and stretched regions (a spring pushed and pulled; sound in air).
5. Classify a wave by comparing the particle's motion with the energy's direction, not by the shape of a drawing. Air and water carry only longitudinal waves through their bulk, because their layers slide freely.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A long spring lies on the floor from south to north. Its south end is moved repeatedly east and west. (a) State the direction in which energy is transferred along the spring. (b) Describe the motion of a coil marked with tape. (c) Classify the wave."
Model answer: (a) North, along the spring, away from the source. (b) The coil oscillates east and west about its rest position; it is not carried along the spring. (c) Transverse, because the oscillation (east and west) is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (north). Classic mistake (crossed): "the tape travels north with the wave".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "The first wave from an earthquake travels north to a seismometer 100 km away. As it arrives, the ground at the seismometer shakes north and south. (a) Classify the wave, giving a reason. (b) Explain whether this type of wave could travel through the Earth's liquid outer core."
Model answer: (a) Longitudinal: the ground oscillates north and south, parallel to the direction of energy transfer (north). (b) Yes. A longitudinal wave needs only a medium that pushes back when squeezed, and liquids do, so it can travel through the liquid outer core (unlike a transverse wave, which needs layers that pull each other back when slid sideways). Classic mistake (crossed): "transverse, because every wave moves things up and down".
