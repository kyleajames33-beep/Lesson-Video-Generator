### 4. the message is the order

### 6. the pairing rule, and why
> The bases don't pair at random. A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G. This is complementary base pairing. Here's why. A and G are large bases with two rings, while T and C are small, with just one ring. Every rung pairs one large base with one small base, so the ladder stays the same width all the way along. And only A and T line up their bonding points, just as only C and G do.

### 7. hydrogen bonds: weak alone, strong together
> What holds a pair together? Hydrogen bonds, weak attractions between slightly charged atoms on the facing bases. A and T are held by two hydrogen bonds, and C and G by three. So a stretch rich in C and G takes more energy to pull apart. One hydrogen bond is weak, but a DNA molecule has millions of them, so together they hold the strands firmly. Because each one is weak, an enzyme can unzip the strands by breaking only the hydrogen bonds, leaving the strong backbones untouched. Stable, yet easy to open.

### 9. one strand fixes the other
> T, G, C. Then the rest follows: the partner strand reads A, T, G, C, C, T, A, G. Check it: every rung has one large base and one small base, and each pair is A with T or C with G. Notice what just happened. Knowing one strand told us the other exactly, with no guessing. So DNA stores its message twice: if one strand is damaged, the cell can repair it using the other strand as a guide. Watson and Crick noticed this too. If the two strands come apart, each one carries everything needed to rebuild its partner. That's the clue to how DNA is copied.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Nucleotide = phosphate group + deoxyribose sugar + one nitrogenous base (A, T, C or G).
- Phosphate of one nucleotide bonds (covalently) to the sugar of the next: a strong sugar-phosphate backbone. The bases carry the message in their order.
