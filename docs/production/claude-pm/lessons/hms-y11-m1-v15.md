### 2. what health promotion is, and the word control
> The World Health Organization defines **health promotion** as the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health. Control is the key word. A hospital improves health. A campaign that tells people what to do, without changing their circumstances, adds information, not control. So health promotion isn't health care, which treats people who are already unwell, and it isn't just health education. It includes education, but also changes the conditions that decide whether education can be acted on.

### 3. three approaches, by the question each asks
> Australia has used three approaches, and each asks a different first question. The **biomedical approach** asks: what's wrong with this body, and how do we fix it? It's the narrow meaning you met at the start, health as no disease, turned into action. The **behavioural approach** asks: what's this person doing wrong, and how do we get them to stop? The **socio-ecological approach** asks: what conditions are producing this pattern, and who can change them? They aren't ranked. A broken leg needs the biomedical approach, and no amount of town planning will set it. All three still operate today, nested inside each other.

### 4. the first shift: what was killing people changed
> The first shift, from biomedical to behavioural, came from a change in what was killing people. Early last century, infections dominated, and sanitation, vaccination and antibiotics beat them. By the nineteen seventies, the leading killers were heart disease and cancer, which develop over decades and are linked to smoking, diet, alcohol and inactivity. By the time they show up, much of the damage is done. So attention moved upstream, to behaviours.

### 5. the second shift: the gap widened
> The second shift, from behavioural to socio-ecological, came from the inequity data. Education campaigns did work, but unevenly. Rates fell fastest among people who already had the money, time and support to act on the advice. So the average improved while the gap widened, the same pattern we've now seen several times. And the behavioural question produces **victim blaming**, blaming a person for an outcome their conditions produced. If you first ask what someone's doing wrong, the answer to inequity becomes worse choices, and that gets cause and effect backwards.
