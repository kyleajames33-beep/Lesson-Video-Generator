## Decisions

- **Learner question (route placeholder rewritten; open decision 1).** Route: "How can I classify motion quantities by whether direction matters?" Proposed causal form: "Why do some motion quantities need a direction to be complete, and which ones are they?" The answer, built in order: a size alone (a magnitude) doesn't fix where something ends up, so some quantities need a direction to be complete (hook, 2); distance only counts path, displacement is the arrow from start to finish (3 to 5); speed is how fast, velocity is how fast and which way, so equal speeds can be different velocities (6); acceleration says how velocity changes, and its direction decides speeding up or slowing down (7), and a direction change alone is a velocity change (8); time has no direction in space (9); so the test "which way?" sorts the six (10).

### 1. hook (400 metres, and nowhere)
> A car drives one lap of a four hundred metre test **track**, and stops where it started. How far has it travelled? Four hundred **metres**. Now, how far is it from where it started? **Zero**. Both answers are right, because they answer different questions. The first one doesn't care which way the car went. The second one depends on it completely. That difference, whether direction matters, splits the quantities we use to describe motion into two **kinds**.

Visual: cold open, no title card. Plan view of a coded oval test track (two grey lane lines, a chequered start line on the bottom straight, a navy start pin "start"), compass top right; the top-view blue car plate sits on the start line, facing right. ### 2. magnitude: a size alone, or a size and a direction
> Suppose I tell you a drone is five metres from its launch pad. Where is it? It could be anywhere on this **circle**. Five metres is only a size. A size, a number with its unit, is called a **magnitude**. Now say the drone is five metres north of the pad. That pins it to one **point**. A quantity that's just a magnitude is called a **scalar**. A quantity that needs a magnitude and a direction is called a **vector**. We draw a vector as an arrow. Its length shows the magnitude, and its head shows the **direction**.

Visual: board clears to a plan view with a 1 m grid and a compass; a small coded launch pad (navy ring) at centre, the top-view drone plate hovering on it. ### 3. distance and displacement (site we-1)
> The first pair is distance and displacement. A drone flies four metres east, then three metres **north**. Distance is the total length of the path it actually travels. Four plus three, so seven **metres**. Distance ignores direction and just keeps adding up, so it's a scalar. Displacement is the change in position: one straight arrow from where it started to where it **finished**. These two legs make a right angle, so Pythagoras gives five **metres**. But five metres alone isn't the full answer. Displacement is a vector, so it needs a direction too, the way this arrow **points**.

### 5. prediction answer: never more than the distance
> It's the third trip. On the first, the path bends, so the straight arrow is a **shortcut**: five metres instead of seven. On the second, the drone comes back, so the displacement is **zero**, though the distance is six. Only the trip that goes in one straight line, without turning back, gives seven and **seven**. A straight line is the shortest way from start to finish, so the size of the displacement can never be more than the distance. That's the test track too: four hundred metres of distance, and no displacement, because the car came **back**.

### 6. speed and velocity
> The second pair is speed and velocity. Both speedometers read sixty kilometres per hour, exactly the **same**. Speed is how fast something covers distance, so it's a scalar. But one car is heading east, and the other is heading **west**. Velocity is how fast the displacement changes, so it carries a direction: sixty kilometres per hour east, and sixty kilometres per hour **west**. After one hour, one car is sixty kilometres east of where they started, and the other is sixty kilometres **west**. Same speed, different velocities, and very different places to end up.

### 7. acceleration: a direction that decides
> What about acceleration? Acceleration tells you how quickly the velocity changes. This car is moving east and **braking**. Each second, its velocity arrow gets shorter. The change from one arrow to the next points **west**, so the acceleration points west. Now the same car speeds up instead. Each arrow is longer than the last, so the change, and the acceleration, point **east**. The changes are the same size. Only the direction tells you whether the car speeds up or slows down. So acceleration is a **vector**.

Visual: board clears to the side-view road with the blue car moving right; the right column writes "acceleration: how quickly velocity changes". On "braking" the car slows and stops being followed; above the road a strip of four orange velocity arrows stacked at one-second ticks writes in turn, tails aligned: "20", "17", "14", "11 m s⁻¹" (author note c; values on screen only). On "same size" the two rose change arrows (west and east) sit side by side, equal length, ringed. On "vector" the word "vector" writes in the right column under acceleration with a navy arrow icon.

### 8. the roundabout surprise
> Here's something surprising. This car goes round a roundabout at a steady forty kilometres per hour. The speed reading never **moves**. But watch the velocity arrow. It keeps the same length, and it keeps **turning**. A turning arrow is a changing velocity. And a changing velocity means the car is accelerating, even at a constant **speed**.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Magnitude: the size of a quantity (a number with a unit). Scalar: magnitude only. Vector: magnitude and direction, drawn as an arrow (length shows magnitude, head shows direction).
2. Scalars: distance, speed, time. Vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration. Test: does it need an answer to "which way?"
3. Distance: total length of path travelled. Displacement: change in position, a straight arrow from start to finish, with direction. Size of displacement ≤ distance (equal only for a straight line that never turns back).

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a direction completes a vector: a size alone leaves a whole ring of possible positions (2). Why distance and displacement differ: distance adds every metre of path; displacement is only the start-to-finish arrow, so bends and returns shorten it (1, 3, 5). Why the size of the displacement can never exceed the distance: the straight line is the shortest path between start and finish (5). Why equal speeds can be different velocities: velocity also says which way, and that decides where the car ends up after an hour (6). Why acceleration is a vector: the direction of the change decides speeding up or slowing down (7). Why a car at a constant speed can accelerate: its velocity arrow turns (8). Why time is a scalar: it has no direction in space (9). Why units don't decide: m s⁻² carries no direction, but the quantity does (10).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** distance and displacement are the same (3, 5; crossed in 13 and 15); speed and velocity are interchangeable (6; A2); constant speed means no acceleration (8); time has a direction (9); acceleration is a scalar because of its unit (10, crossed); a displacement is complete without its direction (3; key note 1).
- **One idea at a time:** one quantity pair per scene; the prediction compares only path against arrow; the sort comes after every quantity has been seen moving.
- **Video makes it easier:** the student watches the distance counter only ever grow while the start-to-car arrow grows and shrinks (1), sees a ring of positions collapse to a point when a direction is added (2), sees the arrow cut the corner of the path (3), watches the cars separate with equal speed readouts (6), sees the change arrows flip between braking and speeding up (7), and watches a velocity arrow turn while the speed reading holds still (8).
