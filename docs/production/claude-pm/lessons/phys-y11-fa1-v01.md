## Decisions

- **Learner question (route placeholder rewritten; open decision 1).** Route: "How can I classify motion quantities by whether direction matters?" Proposed causal form: "Why do some motion quantities need a direction to be complete, and which ones are they?" The answer, built in order: a size alone (a magnitude) doesn't fix where something ends up, so some quantities need a direction to be complete (hook, 2); distance only counts path, displacement is the arrow from start to finish (3 to 5); speed is how fast, velocity is how fast and which way, so equal speeds can be different velocities (6); acceleration says how velocity changes, and its direction decides speeding up or slowing down (7), and a direction change alone is a velocity change (8); time has no direction in space (9); so the test "which way?" sorts the six (10).

### 1. hook (400 metres, and nowhere)
> A car drives one lap of a four hundred metre test **track**, and stops where it started. How far has it travelled? Four hundred **metres**. Now, how far is it from where it started? **Zero**. Both answers are right, because they answer different questions. The first one doesn't care which way the car went. The second one depends on it completely. That difference, whether direction matters, splits the quantities we use to describe motion into two **kinds**.

### 2. magnitude: a size alone, or a size and a direction
> Suppose I tell you a drone is five metres from its launch pad. Where is it? It could be anywhere on this **circle**. Five metres is only a size. A size, a number with its unit, is called a **magnitude**. Now say the drone is five metres north of the pad. That pins it to one **point**. A quantity that's just a magnitude is called a **scalar**. A quantity that needs a magnitude and a direction is called a **vector**. We draw a vector as an arrow. Its length shows the magnitude, and its head shows the **direction**.

### 3. distance and displacement (site we-1)
> The first pair is distance and displacement. A drone flies four metres east, then three metres **north**. Distance is the total length of the path it actually travels. Four plus three, so seven **metres**. Distance ignores direction and just keeps adding up, so it's a scalar. Displacement is the change in position: one straight arrow from where it started to where it **finished**. These two legs make a right angle, so Pythagoras gives five **metres**. But five metres alone isn't the full answer. Displacement is a vector, so it needs a direction too, the way this arrow **points**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a direction completes a vector: a size alone leaves a whole ring of possible positions (2). Why distance and displacement differ: distance adds every metre of path; displacement is only the start-to-finish arrow, so bends and returns shorten it (1, 3, 5). Why the size of the displacement can never exceed the distance: the straight line is the shortest path between start and finish (5). Why equal speeds can be different velocities: velocity also says which way, and that decides where the car ends up after an hour (6). Why acceleration is a vector: the direction of the change decides speeding up or slowing down (7). Why a car at a constant speed can accelerate: its velocity arrow turns (8). Why time is a scalar: it has no direction in space (9). Why units don't decide: m s⁻² carries no direction, but the quantity does (10).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** distance and displacement are the same (3, 5; crossed in 13 and 15); speed and velocity are interchangeable (6; A2); constant speed means no acceleration (8); time has a direction (9); acceleration is a scalar because of its unit (10, crossed); a displacement is complete without its direction (3; key note 3).
- **One idea at a time:** one quantity pair per scene; the prediction compares only path against arrow; the sort comes after every quantity has been seen moving.
- **Video makes it easier:** the student watches the distance counter only ever grow while the start-to-car arrow grows and shrinks (1), sees a ring of positions collapse to a point when a direction is added (2), sees the arrow cut the corner of the path (3), watches the cars separate with equal speed readouts (6), sees the change arrows flip between braking and speeding up (7), and watches a velocity arrow turn while the speed reading holds still (8).
