### 8. the test, and a partial revision
> Scientists compared beech DNA to estimate when the lineages split. Some New Zealand beeches seem to have split from Australian ones long after the sea separated the two. ## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Camera survey (2 to 5).** Illustrative: 30 × 40 ÷ 12 = 100; 30 × 40 ÷ 14 ≈ 85.7 (shown "≈ 86"). Camera trapping with individual identification by coat pattern is an established method for feral cats with distinctive markings (spatial capture-recapture); tabby cats are hard to tell apart, which is the source of matching errors. Correct as a teaching model.
- **Southern beech (7, 8).** *Nothofagus* occurs in southern South America, south-eastern Australia including Tasmania, New Zealand, New Guinea and New Caledonia (New Caledonia not named on screen). Classic vicariance explanation (Gondwana breakup). Knapp and colleagues 2005 (PLoS Biology 3: e14) used molecular dating to argue that some trans-Tasman lineages diverged well after the Tasman Sea opened (about 80 to 85 million years ago), implying long-distance dispersal; later studies support a mix of vicariance and dispersal, and dating estimates vary. Science check to confirm the wording "Some New Zealand beeches seem to have split from Australian ones long after the sea separated the two".
- **Peppered moth (11c).** Industrial melanism near polluted cities; criticism of Kettlewell-era methods (moths released onto trunks in unnatural positions and densities); Michael Majerus's seven-year field study (2001 to 2007, published by Cook and colleagues 2012, Biology Letters 8: 609 to 612) found differential bird predation on naturally resting moths. The stem names no scientist. Correct.
- **Quolls (11a).** Spotted-tailed quolls are identified individually by spot patterns in camera-trap studies. Correct (illustrative numbers).
