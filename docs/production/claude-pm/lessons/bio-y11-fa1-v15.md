### 2. three kinds of need
> Think about what a cell is doing every second. It's pumping ions, moving vesicles and building new proteins to replace old ones, and all of that takes energy. It's also making new parts, and it needs a place where its chemical reactions can happen at all. So the six materials meet three kinds of need: energy, building materials, and the right conditions for reactions. Let's sort them, one at a **time**.

### 3. glucose: the fuel (a quick prediction)
> Glucose is the cell's main fuel. Energy is stored in the chemical bonds that hold it together. In respiration, mostly inside mitochondria, the cell breaks glucose down and transfers that energy to ATP, the energy currency that powers the pumps and vesicles. A leaf cell makes its own glucose by photosynthesis. Quick prediction: so does a leaf cell still need **oxygen**? Yes. Plant cells respire all the time, day and night, just like animal cells, so they need oxygen and glucose **too**.

### 4. oxygen: breaking glucose down completely
> So what does oxygen actually do? It lets the cell break glucose down completely, all the way to carbon dioxide and water. Without oxygen, the breakdown stops part-way, and the cell gets only a small fraction of the energy. With oxygen, each glucose releases far more. That's why a cell that runs short of oxygen soon runs short of ATP, and the first jobs to fail are the ones that need ATP, like **pumping**.

Visual: the oxygen chip leaves the strip. A coded inset above the animal cell: a glucose hexagon on a short horizontal track of six steps. On "breaks glucose down completely" the hexagon splits step by step all the way to grey carbon dioxide and blue water icons at the end (label "with oxygen: all the way"), and a tall gold bar of ATP badges rises; on "stops part-way" a second track below halts after the first two steps (label "without oxygen: part-way") with a short gold bar (grey tag "how much less: in the next video"). On "runs short of ATP" the animal cell's pump from scene 2 stops cycling and its badge greys (terracotta tag "low oxygen → low ATP → pumps stop first"; grey tag "from Why does a cell spend energy to move ions uphill?"). The oxygen chip lands in "Energy" beside glucose. Bright at the end: the two bars and the stopped pump. Hold about 1 second.

### 5. carbon dioxide: waste or raw material
> Carbon dioxide is the special case. In every respiring cell, it's a waste. If it built up, it would make the cytoplasm more acidic, and enzymes, the proteins that speed up the cell's reactions, would stop working properly. So it diffuses out. But in a leaf cell in the light, carbon dioxide is also a raw material. Photosynthesis uses its carbon atoms to build glucose. So the same molecule is an output of one process and an input to another. The reaction decides its role, not the **molecule**.

### 6. amino acids: the building blocks
> Now building materials. Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. Ribosomes join them into long chains, and those chains fold into the proteins that do most of the cell's work: enzymes, the carrier and channel proteins in the membrane, and proteins that hold the cell's shape. Proteins wear out and get replaced, so the cell needs a steady supply. Animal cells get amino acids from the food the animal eats. Plant cells make their own, using sugar from photosynthesis and nitrogen from the nitrate ions their roots **collect**.

### 7. water: where reactions happen
> Then there's water, and it's easy to overlook. Water is the solvent: the cell's chemicals dissolve in it, and two molecules can only react if they can move around and meet. Water is also a reactant itself. Photosynthesis uses it, and so do reactions that break big molecules into smaller ones. Respiration produces it. And in plant cells, water pressing outward against the cell wall keeps the cell **firm**.

### 8. ions: small, but essential
> Ions are charged particles from dissolved minerals, and each kind has its own jobs. Magnesium sits at the centre of chlorophyll, so a leaf can't capture light without it. Iron is part of haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in red blood cells. Sodium and potassium keep the water balance right and carry signals in nerve and muscle cells. Many enzymes only work with the right ion attached. So here's a line to remember: water and ions are not the packaging. They're half the **recipe**.

### 9. all six, and which way they go
> Now all six together. Glucose is the fuel. Oxygen lets glucose be broken down completely. Amino acids build proteins. Water is the solvent and a reactant. Ions help enzymes, keep water balance and form key molecules. And carbon dioxide depends on the reaction. Now look at the arrows. A respiring animal cell takes in glucose and oxygen, and sends out carbon dioxide and water. A leaf cell in bright light takes in carbon dioxide and water, and sends out oxygen. Whether a material goes in or out depends on which reactions the cell is **running**.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Glucose: the main fuel. Respiration breaks it down and transfers the energy stored in its bonds to ATP.
2. Oxygen: lets glucose be broken down completely in aerobic respiration, releasing far more energy. Plant cells respire too, so they also need oxygen.
3. Carbon dioxide: a waste of respiration (removed because it makes the cytoplasm acidic and upsets enzymes) and a raw material for photosynthesis (its carbon builds glucose).
4. Amino acids: building blocks of proteins (enzymes, carrier and channel proteins, structural proteins). Plants make them using nitrogen from nitrate ions.
5. Water: the solvent for reactions, a reactant and a product, and it keeps plant cells firm. Ions: enzyme activity, water balance, parts of key molecules (magnesium in chlorophyll, iron in haemoglobin). Whether a material is an input or an output depends on the cell's reactions.

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Plants respire continuously (scene 3, A2).** Every living plant cell respires day and night; in bright light the photosynthesis rate in leaf cells exceeds the respiration rate. Correct.
- **Complete versus partial breakdown (scene 4).** Aerobic respiration oxidises glucose fully to carbon dioxide and water (about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose); anaerobic pathways stop part-way (2 ATP), so "only a small fraction of the energy" holds (about one fifteenth). No figure is spoken (v16 owns the numbers).
- **Low oxygen and pumps (scene 4).** Falling ATP stops ATP-driven pumps first among the cell's jobs, as in the site's b2 walk-through. Codex to confirm "the first jobs to fail are the ones that need ATP, like pumping" is fair at Year 11 level (protein synthesis also uses ATP and slows; the line names pumping as one example).
- **Carbon dioxide acidity (scene 5).** Dissolved carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid and lowers pH; enzyme activity depends on pH. Correct; "stop working properly" is used rather than "denature".
- **Amino acids (scene 6).** Plants synthesise amino acids from carbon skeletons made from sugars and nitrogen taken up mainly as nitrate (and ammonium) ions. Correct.
- **Water (scene 7).** Solvent; reactant in photosynthesis and hydrolysis; product of aerobic respiration; turgor in plant cells. Correct.
- **Ions (scene 8).** Magnesium is the central atom of chlorophyll; iron (as Fe²⁺) is at the centre of the haem groups in haemoglobin; sodium and potassium in osmotic balance and nerve and muscle signalling; many enzymes require metal ion cofactors (for example magnesium with ATP-using enzymes). Correct. Screen symbols "Mg²⁺" and "Fe²⁺" are ion forms; chlorophyll's magnesium and haem's iron are bound, which the screen labels "Mg" and "contains iron" respect.
- **Table (scene 9).** In bright light a leaf cell has net oxygen release and net carbon dioxide and water uptake; an animal cell's net water output from respiration is small and its overall water balance is set mostly by osmosis, so the "out (net, made by respiration)" cell is a simplification. Codex to check; if judged misleading, change that cell to "made by respiration" without a direction.
- **A1 data (illustrative).** Bean seedlings in nitrate-free hydroponic solution grow far less than in complete solution, a standard result; the masses are illustrative and labelled.
