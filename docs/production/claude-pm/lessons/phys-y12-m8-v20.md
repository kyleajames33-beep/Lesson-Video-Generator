- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "What evidence shows that protons and neutrons have smaller constituents?" The answer: three independent lines. Scattering: high-energy electrons bounce off protons at large angles far more often than a smooth proton allows, as if hitting small hard points inside (3 to 5); patterns: hundreds of related hadrons, as expected from combinations of a few parts (6); change: a neutron turns into a proton, so it has something inside that can change (7); and the quark model built on them predicts both nucleon charges (8). Second half of the dot point: tracks and missing energy revealed particles beyond protons, neutrons and electrons (10 to 12).

### 1. hook (does a proton have parts?)
> We've treated protons and neutrons as solid little balls. But are they fundamental, or made of something smaller? How could you even tell, for something this small? Make your **prediction**. Fire particles at it, just as alpha particles were fired at gold to find the nucleus. So what evidence shows that protons and neutrons have smaller **constituents**?

Visual: opens on v19's final frame (see "Opening frame"); the helium cluster fades, the enlarged proton with "?" stays at the centre. On "solid little balls" the proton outline thickens. On "prediction" a 3 s `Countdown` beside chips "a better microscope", "fire particles at it", "weigh it carefully". On "Fire particles at it" the second chip ticks and the v08 thumbnail pulses. On "constituents" the chip becomes the heading: "What evidence shows that protons and neutrons have smaller constituents?".

### 2. what would count as evidence?
> First, what would fundamental mean? A fundamental particle has no parts, and no size at all, as far as we can tell. So evidence against it would show either parts inside, or a size and a shape. Keep that test in mind, because every piece of evidence today is checked against **it**.

Visual: board change: the evidence test card at the centre: "Fundamental? → no parts, no size". On "no parts, and no size at all" two tick boxes "parts inside?" and "size and shape?". On "evidence against it" the boxes turn into question marks. On "it" the card shrinks to the top right, where it stays to scene 9.

### 3. a probe small enough
> To see inside something, your probe needs a wavelength smaller than it. Remember, wavelength is h over p, so more momentum means a shorter wavelength. In the late nineteen sixties, a three kilometre linear accelerator in California fired electrons at protons with twenty billion electron volts of energy. Their wavelength was about thirty times smaller than a **proton**. Electrons were a good choice, because they don't feel the strong force, and they behave like points, so any structure they reveal belongs to the **target**.

Visual: board change: the painted linear accelerator tunnel `y12p-linac-tunnel-v1.png` (scaled uniformly) across the top third; caption "SLAC linear accelerator, California (3 km): illustration". On "wavelength is h over p" chip "λ = h/p (v12): more momentum → shorter wavelength". On "twenty billion electron volts" an energy readout "E = 20 GeV" and a navy electron dot racing down the tunnel. On "proton" the wavelength comparison: a proton disc (diameter "≈ 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m") with a wave whose wavelength shrinks as the readout rises, ending at "λ ≈ 6 × 10⁻¹⁷ m (about 30 × smaller)", small grey tag "at this energy p ≈ E/c". On "target" chip "electron: no strong force, point-like → a clean probe".

### 4. what they saw
> Here's what they saw. Most electrons were deflected only a little. But far more of them bounced off at large angles than a smooth proton could ever **produce**. It was the same surprise as alpha particles bouncing back from gold, one level **deeper**.

Visual: board change to two panels. Left, the observation panel (pale grey card, "schematic, not data"): a bar chart, x "scattering angle", y "number scattered". On "Most electrons were deflected only a little" tall navy bars at small angles. On "produce" grey outline bars "if the proton's charge were smooth" fall quickly to almost nothing at large angles, while navy "measured" bars stay well above them; tag "many more large-angle scatters". On "deeper" the v08 thumbnail slides in beside the chart with its rare backward path, tag "v08: atom → nucleus"; the right panel stays empty, headed "what it means?".

### 5. what it means
> Now what it means. A smooth, spread-out proton can only nudge an electron gently. Hard, large-angle bounces mean the electron hit something small and concentrated inside the proton. The pattern fitted a proton containing a few tiny, point-like parts. So the proton has parts, and the first box on our test is **ticked**.

Visual: the right panel, headed "model", draws: a dashed proton boundary. On "A smooth, spread-out proton can only nudge" a pale uniform fill with a navy path bending gently. On "something small and concentrated inside" the fill fades and small dots appear inside, a navy path bouncing sharply off one. On "a few tiny, point-like parts" three small dots, tag "model: small hard parts (not a photograph)". On "ticked" the test card's "parts inside?" box ticks "✓ scattering".

### 6. too many particles
> The second clue came from numbers. By the nineteen sixties, experiments had found hundreds of particles that feel the strong force, called hadrons. Hundreds is far too many to all be fundamental. And they fell into neat families, with patterns in their charges and masses. Remember the periodic table. Its patterns came from atoms being built from a few parts. These patterns pointed the same **way**.

Visual: board change to the hadron pattern grid: dots appear in bursts with year labels "1950", "1955", "1960", "1965", a counter climbing "→ hundreds". On "called hadrons" chip "hadron: feels the strong force". On "neat families" the dots slide into regular hexagonal groupings (positions schematic), tag "families with patterns in charge and mass". On "Remember the periodic table" a small periodic-table outline beside it, tag "patterns → built from a few parts". On "way" the test card's "parts inside?" box adds "✓ patterns".

### 7. a neutron that changes
> The third clue is change. A free neutron decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, as you saw in beta decay. If a neutron had no parts, there'd be nothing inside to rearrange. Something inside it changes. In the quark model, one of its quarks changes **type**.

Visual: board change: a grey neutron disc. On "decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino" the identity change: equation "n → p + e⁻ + ν̄" builds; a navy e⁻ dot and a grey "ν̄" dot leave; the disc turns rose and relabels "p". On "nothing inside to rearrange" a crossed chip "no parts → no change possible". On "type" the disc opens (dashed, "model") to show "u d d", one "d" flipping to "u", now "u u d"; tag "d → u (more in the next video)". The test card adds "✓ decay".

### 8. the quark model
> In nineteen sixty-four, Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig proposed that hadrons are made of quarks. The up quark has a charge of plus two thirds, and the down quark has minus one third. A proton is up, up, down. Two thirds plus two thirds minus one third is plus **one**. A neutron is up, down, down, which gives zero. One pair of values fits both nucleons, so the model isn't just fitted to one **result**. Quarks have never been found alone. Pull them apart, and the energy makes new quarks **instead**.

Visual: board change to `m8-particles` charge sums: two dashed boundaries "proton" and "neutron", each tagged "model". On "The up quark has a charge of plus two thirds" chip "u: +⅔e"; on "minus one third" chip "d: −⅓e". On "A proton is up, up, down" three dots "u u d"; on "one" the sum "+⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1" ticks green against "proton: +1 (measured)". On "which gives zero" "u d d: +⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0" ticks against "neutron: 0 (measured)". On "result" tag "same two values fit both: a constraint, not a fit". On "instead" a small animation: two quark dots pulled apart, a new pair appearing between them, tag "confinement: quarks are never seen alone".

### 9. the same test on an electron
> Here's an important contrast. Fire the same kind of probe at an electron, and it shows no structure at all, as far as anyone has measured. It still behaves like a point. So the evidence doesn't say everything has parts. It separates protons and neutrons, which do, from electrons, which **don't**.

Visual: board change: the test card enlarges to two columns, "proton" (three ticks: scattering, patterns, decay) and "electron". On "shows no structure at all" the electron column's boxes stay empty, tag "point-like below 10⁻¹⁸ m". On "don't" the columns label "composite" and "fundamental (as far as we know)"; the card shrinks away.

### 10. a track that bent the wrong way
> Now the second half of the question. What evidence showed other particles exist?
