## Decisions

- **Anchor: three shapes at once (d08's frame).** Screen time (tail right), sleep (tail left) and apples (no tail) are on screen from the first frame, so the words land on pictures students already know.
- **Year 12 z01's words, word for word.** Symmetric: "the left side is a mirror image of the right". Skewed: "one tail longer than the other". Tail: "the thin end on each side". z01 recalls "Year 11: symmetric, skewed" and "Year 11: positively skewed".
- **Skew is named by the tail, not the peak (the key slip).** Positively skewed: the long tail points towards the bigger numbers, to the right. Negatively skewed: the tail points to the left. The peak sits on the opposite side, which is why "the peak is on the right, so it's positive" is the classic slip (A1).
- **A reason in context every time.** Apples: most near a typical size, as many a bit lighter as a bit heavier. Screen time: it can't go below zero, but a few students use their phones for hours. Sleep: most students sleep close to their usual amount, but one bad night can be very short.
- **Peaks and flatness.** Unimodal (one peak); bimodal (two peaks with a dip between: a food truck's lunch and dinner rushes); multimodal (more than two: a café's breakfast, lunch and after-school rushes); uniform (flat: every value about equally common: a fair die rolled 600 times). A bimodal shape often means two groups mixed together (previews d18's clusters).
- **Plain meanings at first use.** Distribution ("how the values are spread along the number line: where they bunch up and where they thin out"); peak ("a value or class that is more common than its neighbours"); unimodal, bimodal, multimodal ("one, two, more than two peaks"); uniform ("flat: every value about equally common").
- **Simulations (standard item 3).**
  1. **Mirror fold** (scene 2): the apple histogram folds along a line at 175 g; the halves match; a fold on the screen-time histogram does not.
  2. **Shape slider** (scene 4): a smooth outline over a 30-bar histogram; dragging a tail handle right or left stretches the tail and moves the peak the other way; a live label switches between "symmetric", "positively skewed" and "negatively skewed", and a tail pointer follows the long side.

> Three datasets, three different shapes. The screen times pile up on the left and trail away to the right. Our sleep data bunch up on the right, with one lonely value far to the left. ### 2. symmetric: a mirror image
> Start with the apples. Most weigh close to a hundred and seventy-five grams, and there are about as many a bit lighter as a bit heavier. Fold the histogram down the middle, at a hundred and seventy-five, and the two halves land on each other. That's a symmetric distribution. The left side is a mirror image of the right. Each side thins out into a tail, the thin end on each side, and here the two tails match. That makes sense for apples. They grow towards a typical size, and small differences either way are equally **likely**. Lots of things made or grown to one size look like this, like the amount of drink a machine pours into each bottle.

### 3. skewed: one tail longer than the other
> Now screen time. Here the two sides don't match. Most students sit between two and four hours, and then the bars thin out slowly to the right, all the way to seven and a half hours. Most students slept six and a half to eight and a half hours, and one slept just three. That tail points left, towards the smaller numbers, so sleep is negatively skewed. Both shapes have just one peak. It's only the tails that differ. Notice the sleep peak is on the right. People often name the skew by the peak, and get it backwards. Name it by the **tail**.

### 4. the shape slider
> Here's why the peak and the tail sit on opposite sides. This histogram starts symmetric. Drag its tail out to the right, and the bulk of the data has to shift left to make room, so the peak moves left. Now it's positively skewed. Drag the tail out to the left instead, and the peak shifts right. Negatively skewed. Wherever the tail goes, the peak moves the other way. The tail is where the few unusual values are. The bulk is where most of the data sit. So always look for the long, thin side first, and name the shape after **it**.

### 5. peaks, and a flat shape
> Shape is also about peaks. A peak is a value, or a class, that's more common than its neighbours. Screen time, sleep and apples each have one peak, so they're unimodal. Now a food truck's customers through the day. There's a rush at lunch, a dip in the afternoon, and another rush at dinner. Two peaks with a dip between, so it's bimodal. Two peaks often mean two groups mixed together, here lunch customers and dinner customers. If you see two peaks, ask what the two groups might be. A café that's busy at breakfast, lunch and after school has three peaks. More than two is multimodal. And sometimes there's no peak at all. Roll a fair die six hundred times, and each face comes up about a hundred times. A flat shape like that, where every value is about equally common, is **uniform**.
