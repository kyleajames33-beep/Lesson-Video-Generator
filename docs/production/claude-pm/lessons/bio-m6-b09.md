### 2. using a process: yeast and bread
> Start with the bread. Yeast is a single-celled fungus. Mixed into dough, its cells break down sugar for energy, releasing carbon dioxide, whose bubbles make the dough **rise**, and a little ethanol. People were baking and brewing this way for thousands of years, long before Louis Pasteur showed, in the eighteen-fifties and sixties, that living yeast does the work. Making bread doesn't change the yeast's DNA. We just **use** what its cells already do.

### 3. what biotechnology means
> That's the heart of it. **Biotechnology** is using living things, their cells or their processes, on purpose, to make a product or solve a problem. Bacteria turning milk into yoghurt count. ### 4. choosing the parents: selective breeding
> Now the wool. For generations, breeders kept the sheep with the finest wool and bred them together. That's **selective breeding**. No one touched a sheep's DNA. Each lamb still got a normal mix of its parents' alleles. But by choosing the parents, breeders changed **which alleles** were passed on, so alleles for finer wool became more common in the flock. It's slow, and it's limited to variation that already exists in the flock.

### 5. changing the DNA: insulin from bacteria

Scientists had put DNA coding for **human insulin** into the bacteria, which read it like any of their own genes, and made human insulin. Here the organism's DNA really was changed. Joining DNA from different sources like this is called **recombinant DNA**.

### 6. the real question: what exactly is changed?
> So the useful question isn't whether a technique is old or new. It's **what exactly is being changed**. Some technologies just use an organism's process, like fermentation. Some choose **which parents** reproduce: selective breeding, and today, artificial insemination of livestock and artificial pollination of crops. Some **copy** existing DNA, growing identical plants from tissue samples, or making many copies of one gene. And some **insert or edit** DNA, like the insulin bacteria, and cotton carrying a gene from a soil bacterium.

### 7. what each kind is good for
> Each kind has its own advantage. Choosing parents works for traits controlled by **many genes**, like wool quality, because breeders judge the whole animal and don't need to know which genes are involved. But it uses only alleles the species already has, and it takes generations. Copying keeps a valuable combination of alleles **together**, with no reshuffling by meiosis. And inserting DNA can add a trait the species **never had**, in a single generation, like cotton plants that make their own protein against caterpillars, from a gene taken from a soil bacterium.

### 8. past, present and future
> Put it on a timeline. The old methods didn't disappear when the new ones arrived. Yeast still raises bread, and breeders still select, now helped by DNA tests that show which animals carry useful alleles. Sometimes one product spans the whole history. Cheesemakers traditionally used rennet, an enzyme taken from calves' stomachs. Today, much cheese is made with the same enzyme, **chymosin**, produced by microbes carrying the gene for it. Biotechnology works across **agriculture**, **medicine** and **industry**. And the newest tools, like gene editing, aim to change one chosen stretch of DNA, which is where future uses are heading.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Biotechnology: deliberately using living organisms, cells or biological processes to make a product or solve a problem, in agriculture, medicine and industry. It includes old methods (fermentation, selective breeding) and new ones.
- Fermentation uses an organism's process: yeast turns sugar into carbon dioxide (which raises bread) and ethanol. The yeast's DNA is not changed.
- Selective breeding and reproductive technologies (artificial insemination, artificial pollination) choose the parents. They change which alleles are passed on and how common they become, but make no new alleles.
- Cloning copies existing DNA (a whole organism or one gene). Recombinant DNA technology inserts DNA, which can come from another species (bacteria making human insulin, since 1982; Bt cotton). Gene editing changes a chosen stretch of DNA.
- Advantages: choosing parents suits traits controlled by many genes; copying keeps a valuable allele combination together; inserting DNA can add a new trait in one generation.

Memory: Biotechnology is older than GMOs. Reproductive technologies choose the parents; they don't change genes.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (20 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; OpenStax Biology 2e, 17.1 Biotechnology and 7.5 Metabolism without oxygen) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Yeast fermentation.** *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* is a single-celled fungus; in dough it ferments sugars to carbon dioxide and ethanol (alcoholic fermentation; with some aerobic respiration early in proofing), and the trapped CO₂ forms the gas cells that make bread rise (OpenStax 7.5). "Breaks down sugar for energy, releasing carbon dioxide ... and a little ethanol": correct. The ethanol mostly evaporates in baking (author note).
- **"For thousands of years".** Brewing and leavened bread are documented in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia several thousand years ago; the 14,400-year Shubayqa remains are not claimed (site correction 2). Correct as stated.
- **Pasteur.** Pasteur's studies (lactic fermentation 1857; alcoholic fermentation memoir 1860; "Études sur la bière" 1876) established that living microorganisms carry out fermentation (Institut Pasteur). "In the eighteen-fifties and sixties": correct.
- **Definition.** Matches the site's and common textbook definitions (use of living organisms, cells or biological processes for products or solutions). Correct.
- **Yoghurt and enzymes.** Lactic acid bacteria (*Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus*, *Streptococcus thermophilus*) ferment milk to yoghurt; microbial enzymes (proteases, lipases, amylases) are used in laundry detergents. Correct.
- **Merino selective breeding.** Merino flocks have been selected for fine wool over centuries in Spain and, from the late eighteenth century, in Australia; fibre diameter is a moderately heritable, many-gene trait. "For generations, breeders kept the sheep with the finest wool and bred them together": correct. "Alleles for finer wool became more common in the flock": correct at this level (author note: a polygenic trait; many alleles of small effect).
- **Insulin dates.** Insulin was first used to treat diabetes in January 1922 (Toronto), extracted from cattle and pig pancreases. Recombinant human insulin (Humulin, made in *E. coli*) was approved in the US in October 1982 (first recombinant DNA drug approved). The first bacterial expression (Goeddel et al. 1979, PNAS) used synthetic DNA coding for the A and B chains (site correction 1). "From the nineteen-twenties ... in the early nineteen-eighties, human insulin made by bacteria went on sale ... Scientists had put DNA coding for human insulin into the bacteria": correct. "People with type one diabetes need insulin every day": correct (no person shown). Today human insulin and analogues are made in *E. coli* or yeast (author note; the narration makes no "most" claim).
- **Reproductive technologies.** Artificial insemination and artificial (hand) pollination control which gametes combine; they do not normally change DNA sequence (site L13; bank insight). Correct.
- **Copying.** Plant tissue culture (micropropagation) and runners or suckers produce genetically matching plants by mitosis; gene cloning makes many copies of one DNA sequence. Correct. Clones' phenotypes can still differ (environment); not spoken (b16).
- **Bt cotton.** Carries *cry* genes from *Bacillus thuringiensis* coding for proteins toxic to certain caterpillars (for example *Helicoverpa* species) when they eat the plant (Australian Bollgard varieties). "Cotton plants that make their own protein against caterpillars, from a gene taken from a soil bacterium": correct (B. thuringiensis is a soil bacterium).
- **Advantages.** Selective breeding suits traits controlled by many genes and uses existing variation (site L08 Compare model); cloning preserves a genotype without meiotic reshuffling (site L13 card 3); recombinant DNA introduces a trait not present in the species, in one generation of transformation (site L08 and L13). Correct as stated, with "can".
- **Rennet and chymosin (scene 8).** Traditional rennet is extracted from the fourth stomach (abomasum) of unweaned calves; its main enzyme is chymosin. Fermentation-produced chymosin, made by microbes (for example *Aspergillus niger*, *Kluyveromyces lactis* or *E. coli*) carrying the calf chymosin gene, was approved in the US in 1990 and is now used for a large share of cheese (estimates around 80 to 90 per cent in the US). "Much cheese is made with the same enzyme, chymosin, produced by microbes carrying the gene for it": correct, with "much" rather than a figure.
- **DNA tests in breeding.** Marker-assisted and genomic selection are used in Australian sheep and cattle breeding (genomic breeding values). "Breeders still select, now helped by DNA tests that show which animals carry useful alleles": correct as a general statement.
- **Gene editing.** Gene editing tools (such as CRISPR-Cas9) target a chosen DNA sequence. "Aim to change one chosen stretch of DNA": correct.
- **Attempt facts.** Yoghurt bacteria (fermentation); AI with one bull's semen across many cows (common in dairy); bananas propagated from suckers (Cavendish bananas are sterile triploids, propagated vegetatively); Bt cotton (transgenic). Correct.
