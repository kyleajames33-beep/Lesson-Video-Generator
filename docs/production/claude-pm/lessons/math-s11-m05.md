### 1. hook: the windscreen price
> Here's the red hatchback from the car yard, fourteen thousand nine hundred and ninety dollars on the windscreen. But that isn't what it costs to drive it away. So what does it really cost to buy a car, and get it on the **road**?

### 2. stamp duty: every hundred dollars, or part
> First, stamp duty. It's a tax the state government charges when a car changes owners. In New South Wales, for a car up to forty-five thousand dollars, it's three dollars for every hundred dollars of the price, or part of a hundred. So count the hundreds. Fourteen thousand nine hundred and ninety is a hundred and forty-nine full hundreds, with ninety dollars left **over**. That ninety is part of a hundred, so it counts as a whole hundred. That's a hundred and fifty lots of a hundred dollars, and a hundred and fifty times three dollars is four hundred and fifty dollars of stamp **duty**. Multiplying a hundred and forty-nine point nine by three gives four hundred and forty-nine seventy. Close, but **wrong**, because the rule charges every part of a hundred in **full**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): the car shrinks to the top left (420 px). Card at the top right (36 px): "NSW stamp duty (passenger car): up to $45 000: $3 per $100 or part". Centre: the block counter, blue $100 blocks filling in rows of ten, counter climbing "1 … 149"; on "over" a smaller block "$90" sits at the end. On "counts as a whole hundred" the part block snaps to full size, outlined amber, label "or part = 1 more", hand tag "a part counts as 100"; the counter reads "150". On "duty" a violet "$3" coin lands on each block (fast cascade) and they sum: row "150 × $3 = $450" (52 px), framed green; the checklist's "tax" chip fills "$450". On "wrong" a red slip "149.9 × 3 = $449.70" writes and is crossed (caption "a part counts as 100"). On "full" the amber part block pulses.

### 3. above forty-five thousand
> Dearer cars pay more, but only on the dollars above the limit. Above forty-five thousand dollars, the first forty-five thousand costs a fixed thirteen hundred and fifty, and every hundred, or part, above that costs five dollars. Take a forty-eight thousand dollar ute. The part above the limit is three thousand dollars, which is thirty lots of a hundred. Thirty times five is a hundred and **fifty**. So the duty is thirteen hundred and fifty plus a hundred and fifty, fifteen hundred **dollars**. Charging the whole price at five dollars a hundred would almost double it, and that's not the **rule**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 2): the ute `ms12-ute-v1.png` (about 600 px) at the top left with "$48 000". Card (36 px): "above $45 000: $1350 + $5 per $100 or part above $45 000". ### 4. registration and the green slip
> Back to the hatchback. Next comes registration, the yearly fee that lets a car use public roads. Here it's an example three hundred and eighty-five dollars, and it depends on the car's **weight**. But you can't register a car without a CTP green slip. That's compulsory third-party insurance, and it covers people who are injured in a crash. An example green slip here costs seven hundred and eighty dollars, and young drivers usually pay **more**. Then there's a small fee to transfer the car into your name, forty **dollars**. All three are compulsory, so they all go into the cost of buying the **car**.

### 5. which insurance pays for what?
> The green slip covers injuries to people. It doesn't fix cars. Say you crash, and there's three thousand dollars of damage to your car, and two and a half thousand to the other driver's. With only a green slip, you pay all fifty-five hundred **yourself**. Third-party property insurance is optional, and covers damage you do to other people's cars and property. With it, the other car is paid for, less your excess, the part of a claim you pay yourself. But your own three thousand is still **yours**. Comprehensive insurance is also optional, and it covers your own car as well. Here it's an example sixteen hundred and forty dollars a year, with an eight hundred and fifty dollar excess. After the same crash, you'd pay just the eight hundred and fifty **dollars**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why 150 lots: a part of $100 is charged in full (2). Why $1,350 first: only the dollars above $45,000 move to $5 (3). Why the green slip is in the on-road cost: no green slip, no registration (4). Who pays after a crash: each policy covers different things (5). Why the drive-away cost is higher: every compulsory cost is added (6). Why a loan costs more: 48 repayments add to more than the price (7).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** "or part" ignored (2, crossed); the whole price at $5 (3, crossed; A1 crossed); the green slip covering your car (5; A2 crossed); compulsory costs left out (6); a loan judged by its repayment (7).
- **Visual standard, item by item:** 1 the hatchback from the first frame; 2 a new stage every 40 to 60 s; 3 the block counter with its snapping part block, the threshold bar, the who-pays table sliding bills, the 48-block loan strip; 4 fixed roles; 5 large type; 6 hand tags; 7 g01 order with framed answers and crossed slips; 8 framed card and five bullets; 9 the $90 block snapping to a full $100.
- **One idea at a time:** duty (2), threshold (3), compulsory fees (4), insurance (5), drive-away (6), loan (7).
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen tasks with every demand visible; 10 s each; feedback about 25 s and 25 s.
- **Avoided double teaching:** totals and percentages from m04 and m01 in chips; loan repayments supplied (Year 12 teaches the method).
- **Syllabus coverage:** `ci5b329382` (2 to 7, A1, A2; key notes 1 to 5): purchase price (6), loan repayments (7), registration (4), compulsory (4) and non-compulsory third-party insurance (5), comprehensive insurance (5), stamp duty at current rates (2, 3).
