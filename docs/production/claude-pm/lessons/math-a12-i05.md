### 2. turn it upside down: −cos x
> Check another point. At three pi over two, sine x is minus one, but cos x is climbing there, with gradient **one**. Everywhere, the gradient of cos x is minus sine x, the exact opposite of what we need. So turn cos x upside **down**. Flipping a curve upside down reverses every slope, at the same x. Now minus cos x climbs from zero to pi, exactly where sine x is positive, and falls from pi to two pi, where sine x is negative. Watch the gradient of minus cos x as a point moves along. It traces sine x **exactly**. Differentiate back: minus cos x has derivative minus, minus sine x, which is sine x. So the integral of sine x is minus cos x, plus **C**. Slide the curve up or down, and its gradients don't change.

### 3. cos x and radians
> Cos x is the easier one. From the derivative rules, the gradient of sine x is cos x. At zero, sine x climbs with gradient **one**, and cos of zero is one. At pi over two it's flat, and cos of pi over two is **zero**. So the integral of cos x is sine x, plus C, with no minus sign. Both rules come from derivatives that only work with x in radians, so these primitives need radians too.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why cos x fails: its gradients are −sin x, opposite to the heights of sin x, shown at π/2 and 3π/2 (1, 2).
