## Practice questions and model answers

1. (Q1, 5 marks.) A two-year bowel screening kit campaign; Region A 38 to 47 per cent, comparison Region B 38 to 40 per cent (card in scene 2a). Evaluate its effectiveness as a disease-prevention method, using the data.
   Model: How it works: the kit detects hidden blood; a positive result leads to colonoscopy, where polyps can be removed before becoming cancer and cancers found early, so more kits returned should mean fewer deaths over time (secondary prevention) (1 mark). Evidence: kit return in Region A rose by 9 percentage points (38 to 47 per cent), against 2 points (38 to 40 per cent) in the comparison region, so about 7 points can be attributed to the campaign (1 mark). Limitations: kit return is an impact measure, and two years is too short to show fewer bowel cancer deaths (the outcome) (1 mark); the regions were similar but not randomly assigned, so other differences may contribute, and 53 per cent of Region A still did not return a kit (1 mark). Judgement with an improvement: the campaign was effective at increasing screening, which is likely to help prevent deaths, but its effect on the disease is not yet shown; follow early-stage diagnoses and deaths for ten years or more, and target and report separately the groups least likely to return kits (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed: "the campaign raised returns by 9 points". Other wrong chains named in the review: "47 per cent of cancers were prevented"; "the campaign worked because Region A is higher than Region B" (both started at 38, so the change is what matters); "screening is primary prevention".
2. (Q2, 2 + 4 marks.) RPE65 gene therapy trial; 13 of 20 treated and 0 of 10 untreated navigate the dimmest course after a year (card in scene 3a). (a) Explain how it could improve vision. (b) Evaluate it as a method of preventing vision loss.
   Model: (a) A harmless virus acts as a vector, delivering working copies of the RPE65 gene into retinal cells (1 mark); the cells transcribe and translate the gene to make the missing protein, so the retina can regenerate its light-detecting pigment and respond to light again (1 mark). (b) Evidence: 65 per cent of treated participants could navigate in the dimmest light against 0 per cent of the control group, and the control group strengthens the evidence that the treatment caused the improvement (judging chance would need a statistical test) (1 mark). What it aims to prevent: further loss of vision in the treated person (the phenotype); because only body (somatic) cells are changed, the faulty alleles are still passed to children (1 mark). Limitations: a small trial with one year of follow-up; it helps only people with this single-gene cause, a small share of inherited retinal disease; it works only while retinal cells survive; it is very expensive (1 mark). Judgement: it improved functional vision (dim-light navigation) over one year for this specific gene fault, but the trial does not establish that degeneration has stopped, and it is not a way to prevent inherited eye disease across a population (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed: "it stops the disease being inherited". Other wrong chains named in the review: "the virus repairs the faulty gene in every cell"; "65 per cent of patients were cured"; "the untreated group was unnecessary".
3. (Q3, 2 + 5 marks.) A cochlear implant clinic's report and speech scores (card in scene 4a). (a) Why implants fitted is not a valid measure; a more meaningful outcome measure. (b) Evaluate the implants with the data and one access consideration.
   Model: (a) The number fitted is a process measure: it counts what the clinic did, not whether the patients' hearing or communication improved (1 mark); a more meaningful outcome measure is the speech test score, because the effect of the disorder is difficulty understanding speech (1 mark). (b) How it works: the implant bypasses damaged hair cells and stimulates the auditory nerve, so it can help where hearing aids no longer can (1 mark). Evidence: average words correct in quiet rose from 15 to 70 per cent, and in noise from 5 to 35 per cent, compared with each person's own best hearing aids; 28 of 30 use the implant daily (1 mark). Limitations: in noise most words are still missed; the results are averages from one clinic with no comparison group, so some individuals may have gained much less (1 mark). Access: a nine-month average wait, plus surgery, programming and months of rehabilitation, which may be hard to reach for some people (1 mark). Judgement: cochlear implants were highly effective at restoring access to speech in quiet and partly effective in noise for these adults; they assist hearing rather than restore normal hearing (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed: "every patient improved to 70 per cent". Other wrong chains named in the review: "more implants fitted means the program is more effective"; "70 per cent means normal hearing"; "the daily-use figure shows the implants cured their hearing loss".
