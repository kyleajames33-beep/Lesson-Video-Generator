### 2c. what it gains, and the risk of the same reach
> What does it gain? Calves from a proven bull, one whose merit for milk is shown by its daughters' milk records, on many farms, and faster improvement in traits like milk yield than leaving mating to chance. But the same reach is the risk. If a few bulls father most of the calves, the herd's gene pool, its variety of alleles, narrows. The cows then share more of the same alleles. If the herd is closely related, the cows share a weakness to a disease, so one disease can spread through the whole herd. And a harmful allele carried by a popular bull can be passed to many calves.

### 2d. the fertility trade-off, and the cost
> Studies of Holstein cows in several countries found that as milk yield rose through selection, the chance of becoming pregnant after each insemination fell, and part of the fall was genetic. Insemination makes it easy to use the top milk bulls everywhere, so selection for one trait runs fast, and so does its side effect. A cow that struggles to get pregnant can have a harder life on the farm. Semen and a trained worker cost money, but usually less than buying and keeping a top bull on every farm.

### 5. what "evaluate" asks for
> Evaluate means make a judgement using criteria, which are the things you judge it by. Four make a good start. Gain: what does it improve, shown by evidence such as a comparison with similar farms that do not use it? Variety: does it narrow the gene pool? Cost: money, time and skill. And for animals, welfare, which is how well their lives go. A good answer gives a judgement, reasons from each criterion, a weighing, and when it would change.

### 6c. a model Evaluate answer: weighing and the verdict
> Finally, weigh them. Here the gain can be large and the money cost is usually lower than keeping top bulls on every farm, so the answer turns on variety and welfare, and both depend on how the bulls are chosen. The verdict: the benefit is real, and it lasts if many unrelated bulls are used, each on only a share of the cows, and health traits count in choosing them. Frozen semen, which can be stored for decades, from older, different bulls, heritage breeds, which are older breeds still bred, and outcrossing, which means breeding with unrelated animals, can also protect variety.

### 7. attempt

### 10. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Artificial insemination: semen from a chosen male is collected, divided into many doses, usually frozen, and placed in a female's reproductive tract during heat, before ovulation. It spreads one male's alleles widely, usually at a lower cost than buying and keeping a male on every farm.
- Artificial pollination: the breeder removes the anthers of the seed parent, puts pollen from the chosen parent on its stigma and covers the flowers. Over several generations she keeps the plants with the wanted traits.
- Copying the winner (cloning, for example potato tubers) gives identical plants quickly. They share the same weaknesses, as the Cavendish banana shows.
- Using a few parents on many farms narrows the gene pool, the variety of alleles in a population, so one disease or harmful allele can spread. Frozen semen from other bulls, heritage breeds and outcrossing (breeding with unrelated individuals) help protect variety.
- Evaluate means make a judgement using criteria, the things you judge it by.
