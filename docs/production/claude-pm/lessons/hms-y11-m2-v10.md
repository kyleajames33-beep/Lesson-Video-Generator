### 2. the third column
> The third system is the **aerobic system**. It finishes the job glycolysis starts. ### 3. why it's slow: delivery

Marathon runners plan their fuel, fluids and pace. Sprinters don't need to.

### 4. fat or carbohydrate?
> So which fuel does it burn? Fat holds more than twice the energy per gram, and even a lean athlete stores enough for many hours. Carbohydrate is faster, and it's the only food fuel the body can use without oxygen. So intensity decides the mix. At rest and at low intensity, fat leads. As the pace rises, the body shifts toward carbohydrate, because fat can't be supplied fast enough. Fat is the bigger tank, but the narrower pipe.

### 6. the gap at the start
> The aerobic system's slow start causes a problem at both ends of a session. When you start running, demand jumps at once, but heart rate, stroke volume and breathing take a minute or two to catch up. That gap is the **oxygen deficit**, and the anaerobic systems cover it. It's why the first minutes of a run feel harder, and why a warm-up helps: it raises supply before the real work starts. Once supply meets demand, you're in a **steady state**, and you can keep going. Push above what oxygen can cover, and there's no steady state. The effort now has a time limit.

### 7. the oxygen after you stop
> At the other end, you stop, but your oxygen use stays high. That's **excess post-exercise oxygen consumption**, or EPOC. The extra oxygen pays for recovery. It rebuilds phosphocreatine and ATP, clears the lactate, and reloads oxygen onto haemoglobin and onto myoglobin, the muscle's own oxygen store. So recovery isn't doing nothing. It costs energy. That's why a light cool-down clears by-products faster than sitting down.

### 8. all three at once
> Now the part of the dot point most answers leave out: **interplay**. All three systems run all the time. A long passage of hard defence pushes glycolysis up. But most of seventy minutes is walking and jogging, so across the whole match, the aerobic system supplies most of the energy.

### 9. why a netballer trains aerobically
> So why should a netballer train aerobically, when no single effort lasts more than a few seconds? Because the aerobic system is what recovers them between efforts. In every walk and jog, it rebuilds phosphocreatine and clears by-products. A fitter player rebuilds faster, so they reach the next sprint with more fast fuel. That doesn't show up as a faster single sprint. It shows up in the last quarter, as sprints that stay sharp. Aerobic fitness in team sport is about the recovery, not the running.

## Memory aid check

- **"Fat: bigger tank, narrower pipe."** Assumption: fat's maximal rate of supply is lower than carbohydrate's and its store is larger. True for every athlete; the pipe's width improves with endurance training (more fat used at a given pace), which is an adaptation for Year 12, not a change in the comparison. Author note.
