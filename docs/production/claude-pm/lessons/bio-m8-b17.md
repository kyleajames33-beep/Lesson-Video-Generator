### 3. kidney failure
> When many nephrons are damaged, less blood is filtered. Diabetes is the most common cause in Australia, because high glucose damages the tiny filtering blood vessels. High blood pressure and inflamed filters can too. The remaining nephrons work harder, so people often feel well until most function is gone. Then wastes build up. Urea rises, extra water causes swelling and high blood pressure, and potassium climbs, which can upset the heart's rhythm. Untreated, kidney failure is usually fatal.

### 4. haemodialysis
> **Haemodialysis** does the filtering outside the body. Blood flows from the arm to a **dialyser**, a cylinder packed with thousands of thin hollow fibres. Blood runs inside the fibres, and a fluid called **dialysate** flows around them. The fibre walls are partially permeable: urea can cross, but blood cells and proteins are too big. The dialysate has no urea, so urea diffuses out of the blood, down its concentration gradient. But it has normal glucose levels, so glucose has no gradient and stays. It's like clean blood without the waste.

### 5. counter-current, water and potassium
> Two details make it work better. The dialysate flows the opposite way to the blood. So all along the fibre, blood meets fluid with even less urea, and the gradient stays steep from one end to the other. That's **counter-current flow**. A slight pressure difference also pushes out extra water, which failed kidneys can't get rid of. And the fluid's potassium is set low, so extra potassium diffuses out too.

Visual: the `MembraneZoom` widens into a long horizontal fibre section. On "the opposite way" the dialysate arrows reverse to run against the blood arrows (label "opposite directions"). On "all along the fibre" `ConcBars` draw at five points along the fibre: blood urea falls left to right, dialysate urea also falls in the opposite direction, and a coded gap bracket stays the same size at every point (label "gradient stays steep"). ### 7. peritoneal dialysis

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Kidney jobs: remove urea (waste from protein), balance water, salts (potassium) and acidity, make a hormone for red blood cell production, activate vitamin D. Kidney failure (most often from diabetes in Australia): urea, water and potassium build up.
- Haemodialysis: blood flows through hollow fibres; urea diffuses into urea-free dialysate down its gradient; cells and proteins stay; glucose stays (same level as blood). Counter-current flow keeps the gradient steep; pressure removes extra water.
- Dialysis must be repeated (in-centre, often about 4 to 5 hours, 3 times a week) because urea keeps being made. It can't make hormones; fluid and potassium are limited. It assists, it doesn't replace.
- Peritoneal dialysis: the peritoneum is the membrane; glucose in the fluid draws water by osmosis; at home; risk of peritonitis.
- Transplant: replaces all the kidney's jobs, continuously. Needs a tissue match and lifelong immunosuppressants (infection and some cancer risk); donor shortage; can be rejected or fail.

Memory: dialysate is clean blood without the waste; transplants need a match and immunosuppressants.
