### 2. why it's a health issue
> In Year 11 you met health literacy: finding, understanding, judging and using health information. A **critical health consumer** does the judging part before acting. Why does it matter? Four reasons, and each one is a health outcome. Money, spent on things that don't work, which hurts people with least the most. Delay, when believing a claim pushes back treatment that works. Direct harm, from interactions or unsafe doses. And lost trust. People who hear endless contradictions decide nobody knows anything, and then reject the reliable advice too. It's harder now, because feeds rank posts by engagement, not accuracy, so confident claims travel furthest. But here's the bigger point. If every person has to check every claim alone, the system fails the people with the least time, education and money. Regulation and reliable public information are there so that checking is a safeguard, not the only defence.

### 3. whom to believe: four tests
> So how do you know whom to believe? Not by memorising a list of good sources. It goes out of date, and it won't help with one you've never seen. Use four tests, in order. First, who benefits if I believe this? A seller isn't automatically wrong, but needs stronger evidence. That's a **conflict of interest**. Second, is the expertise in the right field? A specialist outside their specialty is a classic source of confident mistakes. Third, does it fit the wider evidence? One study against everything else is more likely an outlier than a breakthrough. Fourth, does the source correct itself? Good sources state uncertainty and change their minds. So **credibility** comes from how a source works, not how sure it sounds. Certainty is the cheapest thing to produce.

### 4. what you need to know: six questions
> Next, what do you need to know to decide? Six questions, whether it's surgery, a supplement or an app. What exactly is claimed, and for whom? How big is the benefit? Insist on the absolute change. If a risk falls from two in a thousand to one in a thousand, that's **relative risk** halved, but the **absolute risk** fell by one person in a thousand. What are the harms and costs? What are the alternatives, including doing nothing? How certain is it? And does it fit me, my conditions, my medicines, what I value? Asking these out loud is what a shared decision looks like.

### 5. eight ways a true statement misleads
> Then check accuracy. **Accuracy** is whether this claim is true, which is a separate question from whether the source is credible. And most misleading claims aren't lies. They're true facts arranged to suggest something they don't show. Eight patterns. **Correlation** sold as cause. Green tea drinkers may also exercise more, smoke less and earn more. That third factor is **confounding**, and it's the question that decides the green tea claim. Relative risk with no absolute risk. A blood test number moving, instead of the disease itself. The wrong population, like cells, animals or young volunteers. An anecdote. Cherry-picking one study. Empty authority words, like clinically proven, natural or chemical-free. And pressure to decide now. For a service, ask three more. Is the practitioner registered? What can you do if it goes wrong? And will they refer you on when something is beyond them?

### 6. the sixty-second method
> Now put it together into one claim checker, short enough to use in a shop. Every gate is something you've just used. Take the tub. One, who benefits if I believe this? The seller. Two, what's the actual claim? Strip the adjectives. Boost immunity, in whom, by how much? Nothing testable is left. Three, where would it be if it were true? In clinical guidance, not only on a label. Four, how big, in absolute terms? It won't say, and that's the answer. Five, what am I giving up? Money, and maybe a vaccine. Then match the evidence you demand to the stakes. Something cheap and harmless can be tried on weak evidence. Anything risky, or replacing treatment, needs strong evidence. Being critical isn't refusing to believe things. It's believing in proportion to the evidence.
