- **Site errors found (this read).** Root to add to `../site-corrections-bio-y11.md`.
  1. L06 hook: "For 250 years after cells were discovered, no one knew mitochondria had internal folds or that membranes existed". Two problems. Cells were named in 1665 and cristae were first described from electron micrographs in the early 1950s (Palade; Sjöstrand), nearly 290 years. And the cell membrane's existence was inferred well before electron microscopy (from osmosis and permeability experiments in the late 1800s, and a lipid bilayer was proposed in 1925); what no one had seen was its structure. Proposed: "For nearly 300 years after cells were named, no one saw the folds inside mitochondria or the layered structure of a membrane, because no microscope could resolve them."
  2. L06 card 2 and SA1 model answer: "It revealed the cell's ultrastructure, the cell membrane, ribosomes ...". The membrane was not discovered by electron microscopy (see 1); electron microscopy revealed its layered structure. Proposed: "the structure of the cell membrane". The same card repeats "confirmed prokaryotes lack internal membrane-bound organelles", already logged for L01 (some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, have internal membranes); this design says "bacteria such as *E. coli*".
  3. L06 why it matters: "viruses (~20–300 nm) are far too small for any light microscope to resolve". The largest viruses in that range are near the light limit, some giant viruses (over 400 nm) can be seen with a light microscope, and super-resolution fluorescence microscopes now resolve below 200 nm. Proposed: "most viruses (about 20 to 300 nm) are too small for an ordinary light microscope to show in any detail".
  4. L06 card 2 and vocabulary: "resolves to about 0.2 nm" stated without qualification. The 0.2 nm figure is the best instrument resolution on ideal specimens; stained thin sections of cells usually give about 1 to 2 nm. The corrections log already declines to replace one unqualified limit with another; this design keeps "about 0.2 nanometres in the best instruments" (the route figure) and adds a screen tag "cell samples: usually 1 to 2 nm". ### 5. why light stops at about 200 nanometres
> Light has a limit because it travels as waves, about four hundred to seven hundred nanometres long. A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre. Waves can't pick out details much smaller than about half their length, so a light microscope can't separate points closer than about two hundred nanometres. A cheek cell, about fifty micrometres across, is easy to see. A mitochondrion, about a micrometre wide, is a fuzzy speck. But ribosomes and membranes are far smaller than two hundred nanometres, so they stay invisible.

### 15. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Magnification: how many times bigger the image is than the object. Resolution: the smallest gap between two points still seen as two. Resolution, not magnification, limits detail (more magnification past the limit is empty magnification).
- Light microscope (1600s onwards): about 200 nm, limited by the wavelength of light. Revealed cells (Hooke, cork), living single-celled organisms (van Leeuwenhoek), nuclei and chloroplasts: the evidence for cell theory. Cannot show ribosomes, membranes or cristae.
- Electron microscope (1930s): about 0.2 nm in the best instruments (cells about 1 to 2 nm), about 1000 times finer. TEM: thin slices, the inside. SEM: surfaces in 3D. - **Magnification "more than a thousand times" (scene 1).** School and research compound light microscopes reach ×1000 to ×1500 useful magnification (×100 oil objective with ×10 or ×15 eyepiece). Correct.
- **"an animal cell holds millions of ribosomes" (scene 1).** A growing mammalian cell holds about 10 million ribosomes; a typical animal cell holds millions. Correct as "millions".

Van Leeuwenhoek, from 1674, single-lens microscopes, described living single-celled organisms in pond and rain water; he described *Volvox* in 1700 (the screen tag). Correct.
- **Cell theory (scene 2).** Schleiden and Schwann (1838 to 1839), with "all cells come from cells" added by Remak and Virchow (1850s). The two-claim statement is standard. "Over the next two centuries" (1660s to 1850s): correct.
- **Light wavelength and limit (scene 5).** Visible light about 400 to 700 nm; the resolution limit is about half the wavelength (Abbe), about 200 nm for the best light microscopes. Correct.
- **Sizes (scene 5).** Cheek cell about 50 to 60 µm (v06: about 56 µm). Mitochondria about 0.5 to 1 µm wide, visible as specks or rods in light microscopes. Eukaryotic ribosome about 25 to 30 nm. Membrane about 7 to 10 nm thick. Correct ("about twenty-five", "under ten").
- **Electron microscope (scene 6).** First built 1931 (Knoll and Ruska), exceeded light resolution by 1933, commercial from 1939. Electron wavelength at 100 kV about 0.004 nm (over a hundred thousand times shorter than visible light; "thousands of times shorter" is a safe understatement). Best resolution about 0.05 to 0.2 nm; thin sections of cells usually about 1 to 2 nm (screen tag). "roughly a thousand times finer" (200 ÷ 0.2 = 1000). Correct with the tag.
- **TEM and SEM (scene 6).** TEM passes electrons through thin sections (internal structure); SEM scans the surface with a focused beam (surface in 3D). Correct.
- **Discoveries (scene 7).** Cristae and the double mitochondrial membrane (Palade, 1952 to 1953); the endoplasmic reticulum (Porter, Claude and Fullam, 1945; named 1953), ribosomes (Palade, 1955); the Golgi body (seen in 1898 with silver staining, its reality debated until electron micrographs of the 1950s); the double nuclear envelope with pores (1950s). Bacteria such as *E. coli* lacking a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (electron microscopy, 1950s to 1960s). "In the 1940s and 1950s" covers the ER's first image (1945, Porter, Claude and Fullam, cultured cells thin enough to image without sectioning) and the 1950s thin-section work; the science check confirmed this wording (Revision 2).
- **Cristae and surface area (scene 8).** The inner mitochondrial membrane's folding increases its area for the respiration enzymes of the electron transport chain. Spoken "a large area to hold the enzymes of respiration". Correct at this level.
- **Vacuum and preparation (scene 9).** Electrons are scattered by gas molecules, so the column is evacuated. Conventional TEM preparation fixes (chemically preserves and kills), dehydrates, embeds, sections thinly and stains with heavy metals; SEM uses prepared (fixed, dried, coated) surfaces, not thin sections; cryo-EM uses frozen-hydrated samples (silent tag, scene 11). Spoken "dehydrated" stands for dehydration. Neither standard method films living cells. Images are greyscale; colour is added afterwards (false colour). Correct.
- **Mesosome (scene 10).** Described in the 1950s and 1960s (named about 1960) as membrane infoldings in chemically fixed bacteria; shown in the 1970s and 1980s to be artefacts of chemical fixation, absent in rapidly frozen (cryofixed) cells. "In the 1960s" is when they were widely described. Correct.
- **Newer tools (scene 11, silent tags).** Fluorescence microscopy labels chosen molecules in living cells; cryo-electron microscopy freezes samples rapidly in vitreous ice, preserving near-native structure (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2017). Correct.
