Main question: *How do blood components let one fluid transport, defend and clot?*
Entry knowledge: see above. Students have watched FA2 v01 to v19.
Stops after: blood is a tissue: a liquid, plasma (about 55 per cent), carrying red cells (about 45 per cent) and a thin layer of white cells and platelets; plasma is mostly water, so it dissolves and carries glucose, amino acids, salts, hormones, carbon dioxide, urea, proteins and heat; red cells carry oxygen because they are packed with haemoglobin, an iron-containing protein that loads oxygen in the lungs and releases it in tissues (red colour comes from it; less haemoglobin means less oxygen); a red cell is biconcave, so it has a large surface for its volume and a short diffusion distance; it has no nucleus (and no mitochondria), so it has more room for haemoglobin and does not use the oxygen it carries; it is flexible, so it bends through capillaries narrower than itself; the cost is that it has little ability to replace worn proteins and lasts about four months; white cells keep a nucleus, so they can make proteins such as enzymes and antibodies, and change shape, so they squeeze out of capillaries and engulf microbes; platelets are small fragments of a large bone marrow cell, not complete cells, so huge numbers gather fast at damage, stick to form a plug and trigger reactions that turn a plasma protein into fibrin threads that trap cells in a clot.
Handoff: blood is one fluid with these parts, but it doesn't stay the same: why does it leave each organ changed? (route entry bio-y11-fa2-v21)
