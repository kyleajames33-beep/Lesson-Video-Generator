### 2. a fraction of the adult dose
> All three work the same way. They multiply the adult dose by a fraction less than one, so a child gets part of the adult dose. What changes is what the fraction uses. Young's formula uses the child's age in years. Clark's uses the child's weight in kilograms. Fried's uses the age in months, and it's meant for babies. The HSC formula sheet gives you all **three**.

### 3. worked example: Young's formula
> Here's Young's formula. The dose equals the child's age in years, times the adult dose, over the age plus twelve. For an eight-year-old, that's eight times five hundred, over eight plus twelve. Work out the bottom first, in brackets. Eight plus twelve is twenty, and four thousand over twenty is two hundred **milligrams**. That's eight twentieths, or forty per cent, of the adult **dose**. As the age goes up, the fraction grows, but it never reaches one. Typing it in without brackets is the slip. Eight times five hundred, divided by eight, plus twelve, gives five hundred and twelve, more than an adult **dose**.

### 4. Clark's formula: by weight
> Clark's formula uses weight. The dose equals the child's weight in kilograms, times the adult dose, over seventy. Seventy kilograms stands for an average adult. A child who weighs twenty-five kilograms gets twenty-five times five hundred, over seventy, which is about one hundred and seventy-eight point six **milligrams**. That's about thirty-six per cent of the adult dose, because twenty-five is about thirty-six per cent of **seventy**.

### 5. Fried's formula: babies, by months
> Fried's formula is for babies, so it uses age in months. The dose equals the age in months, times the adult dose, over one hundred and fifty. For a nine-month-old, that's nine times five hundred, over a hundred and fifty, which is thirty **milligrams**. Now, here's why the unit matters. Put nine months into Young's formula as if it were nine years, and you'd get about two hundred and fourteen milligrams, more than seven times too **much**. So always check which unit a formula **wants**.

### 6. which formula, and why they differ
> So which formula? Look at what you're given. Months, use Fried's. Years, use Young's. Kilograms, use Clark's. And if you work out the same child two ways, the answers won't match. Our eight-year-old weighs twenty-five kilograms. Young's gives two hundred milligrams, and Clark's gives about one seventy-nine. Age and weight are different ways of standing in for body size, so the estimates **differ**. That's one more reason a doctor or pharmacist **decides**.
