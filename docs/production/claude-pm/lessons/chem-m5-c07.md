### 2. what gives the most ammonia in the settled mixture
> First, what gives the most ammonia in the settled mixture? Chemists call how much of the nitrogen and hydrogen has become ammonia, once settled, the **equilibrium yield**. For one starting gas and pressure, more ammonia in the settled gas means a higher yield. The forward reaction gives out heat, 92 kilojoules for each mole of nitrogen, so heat goes on the right. **Cooling** takes heat away, and the shift goes toward it, so it raises the yield. **Squeezing** shifts toward fewer gas particles, two against four, so it does too. Pulling the ammonia out leaves the settled mixture the same, but lets the rest of the gas make more, so the total made rises. The **catalyst** changes only the speed.

### 5. pressure helps both, so what stops it?
> Pressure helps **both**. Squeezing crowds the particles, so collisions are more frequent and both rates rise, and it shifts the balance toward the side with two gas particles. So why not a thousand atmospheres? Each extra bit of pressure buys **less** extra ammonia, but the thicker steel, stronger pumps and extra energy cost **more** each time, and a leak is dangerous. The plant settles where the extra ammonia stops paying for the extra cost, at about two hundred atmospheres.

### 6. take the ammonia out and send the rest round again
> Even at this compromise, only part of the gas reacts on each pass: about a **fifth** of the gas leaving the reactor is ammonia, a little under the settled value, since the gas does not stay to settle fully. So the gas is cooled until the ammonia turns to a liquid, called **condensing**, and is drawn off, while the nitrogen and hydrogen stay gases. The unused gas is pumped back into the reactor, which is **recycling**. It holds less ammonia than a settled mixture would, so the shift runs **forward** again. Over many trips, more than **ninety-five percent** of the gas fed in ends up as ammonia.

### 12. key notes (quiet, hold about 90 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Equilibrium yield is how much of the reactants has become product once the mixture has settled. The rate decides how soon the mixture gets there. A plant needs both, because it makes product in a limited time.
- If the forward reaction gives out heat, cooling raises the equilibrium yield but lowers both rates, because fewer collisions have enough energy. Industry uses a moderate temperature and a catalyst. Haber: about 400 to 450 degrees Celsius with iron.
- A catalyst makes the mixture settle sooner but does not change the settled mixture. By making a moderate temperature fast enough, it lets the plant avoid a much hotter reactor that would lower the yield.
- Higher pressure raises the yield when there are fewer gas particles on the product side, and it raises the rate by crowding the particles. Cost and safety limit it (Haber: about 150 to 250 atmospheres, not 1000). Condensing out the product and recycling the unused reactants raises the overall conversion (Haber: above 95 percent).
- To justify a condition, give the effect on the yield with its reason, the effect on the rate with its reason, and what limits it.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Haber is a compromise: yield asks for cold, speed asks for hot.* Second line: *Justify with three things: yield, rate, limit.* Condition tag: "forward reaction gives out heat and is too slow when cold. If it takes in heat, heat helps both."

## Memory aid check
