## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "How do a point and derivative determine tangent and normal lines?" The answer, built in order: the derivative gives a gradient, and a gradient alone describes a whole family of parallel lines (hook); the point on the curve, from f, picks out one of them, so the tangent is y − f(a) = f′(a)(x − a) (2, 3); turning the tangent's slope triangle a quarter turn swaps its run and rise and reverses one of them, so the normal's gradient is −1/m when m ≠ 0 (4, 5); when m = 0 the flat triangle turns upright, so the normal is the vertical line x = a (6); the same two steps work for any function in the course, and only the differentiating changes (7).

### 1. hook (a gradient is not yet a line)
> Last video ended here. This curve is y equals cos two x, and by the chain rule, its derivative is minus two sin two x. At x equals pi over six, that gives a gradient of minus root **three**. So is that enough to draw the tangent? Not quite. Here's a line with gradient minus root three. Here's **another**, and another. They're all parallel, and only one of them passes through the point on the **curve**. A gradient tells you the direction of a line, but not where it sits. So how do a point and a derivative pin down the tangent, and the line through the same point at right angles to it, called the **normal**?

### 2. two ingredients
> A line needs two things: one point it passes through, and its gradient. You met the formula in Year 11. y minus y one equals m, times x minus x one. For a tangent, both come from the curve, at the same x. The point comes from f itself. Substitute x into the original function to get the height. The gradient comes from f prime, at that same x. The tangent is the line through that point with that **gradient**. It doesn't have to touch the curve only once. This one meets the curve again, further **along**, and that's fine.

### 3. worked example: the tangent to cos two x
> Let's write it out. Find the equation of the tangent to y equals cos two x, at the point where x equals pi over six. First the point. cos of two times pi over six is cos of pi over three, which is a **half**. So the point is pi over six, a half. Next the gradient. dy by dx is minus two sin two x. At pi over six, that's minus two times sin pi over three. That's minus two times root three over two, so minus root **three**. Now substitute both into the formula. y minus a half equals minus root three, times x minus pi over **six**. If the question asks, expand it to y equals minus root three x, plus pi root three over six, plus a **half**.

### 4. why the normal's gradient flips
> The normal goes through the same point, at right angles to the tangent. You know its gradient from Year 11: perpendicular gradients multiply to minus one. Here's why, in one picture. Draw a small triangle on the tangent, with a run along, and a rise. Now turn the whole triangle a quarter turn, about the **point**. The run and the rise swap places, and one of them now points the opposite **way**. So the new gradient is the old one flipped upside down, with its sign changed. That's minus one over m. And that needs m to be non-zero, because one over zero doesn't **exist**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a gradient is not enough: many parallel lines share it (1). Why the point comes from f: the point is on the curve, and f gives its height; f′ gives a gradient, not a height (2, 10). Why a tangent can meet the curve again: it is defined by the point and the gradient there, not by touching once (2). Why the normal's gradient is −1/m: a quarter turn swaps the run and rise and reverses one of them (4). Why m ≠ 0 is needed: a flat triangle turns into an upright segment, and an upright line has no gradient (4, 6). Why the horizontal tangent never meets the x-axis: both lines are horizontal, at different heights (6). Why the method works for every function: the two ingredients are the same; only finding f′ changes (7).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** "a gradient is enough" (1, refuted by the family); "use f′ for the y-value" (2, said positively; 10, crossed); "a tangent touches the curve at only one point" (2, refuted on the graph); "the normal's gradient is −1/0" (6, crossed); "vertical normal x = height" (12, crossed); "cos 1 in degrees" (7, chip).
- **One idea at a time:** the hook moves one line; scene 2 adds one ingredient per sentence; each worked scene writes one row per spoken step with the point and gradient kept in a small results box; the quarter turn is one motion.
- **Video makes it easier:** the student sees parallel lines with the right gradient miss the point (1), sees the tangent meet the curve again (2), watches the triangle turn so the run and rise visibly swap (4), watches the flat run leg stand up into x = 1 (6), and sees the tangent and the x-axis run side by side without meeting (6). A `NormalLineLayer` draws it inside `FunctionGraph` through `graphTransform`, clipped to the plot box, with an optional right-angle mark at the point (drawn only when the plot has equal units).
2. **`QuarterTurnNormal`** (SVG layer inside `FunctionGraph`): props `graph` (the same `GraphSpec`), `point {x, y}`, `slope` (finite), `run` (mathematical units, default 1), `progress` 0 to 1, optional `direction` ('anticlockwise' default), `runLabel`, `riseLabel`, `turnedRunLabel`, `turnedRiseLabel`, `showLine` (draws the full line through the point, rotating with the triangle, ending on the normal or on the vertical line x = x₀ when slope = 0). The triangle's legs keep their colours through the turn (run leg teal, rise leg amber, as `TangentTriangle` and `ReflectionLayer`), and the labels switch only at progress 1. The rotation is about the point in screen space. Equal units are required: the part throws, or the pilot check fails, if |sx| and |sy| per mathematical unit differ by more than 0.5 per cent, because only then does a screen quarter turn give a mathematical right angle. For slope 0 the rise leg has zero length and is not drawn; the run leg turns upright. Checks: the final direction of (run, run × m) rotated is (−run × m, run), gradient −1/m; product of gradients −1 for m in {−√3, 1/cos 1, 2e, 2, −0.5}; slope 0 gives a vertical final line at x = x₀; the leg endpoints stay attached to the point for 101 progress states.
