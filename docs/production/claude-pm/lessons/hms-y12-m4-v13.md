### 2. strategy and tactic, one level down
> Earlier you met the two words: the strategy is the plan for the whole contest, and tactics are the actions that serve it. Here they carry everything, so be exact. If you'd do it once in a game, it's probably a tactic. If it describes the whole contest, it's a strategy.

### 3. four families of factors
> Many factors shape how a plan is applied, and listing them earns less than grouping them. There are four families. First, you: your fitness, skills, experience, injuries, and for a team, **cohesion**, how well the group works together, and **role clarity**, everyone knowing their job. Those decide which strategies are even possible. A pressing game needs the fitness to sustain it. Second, the opponent: their strengths, weaknesses and patterns, found through **opponent analysis**. Third, the **conditions**: weather, wind, surface, venue, crowd, and the rules and how officials interpret them. Fourth, the state of the contest: the score, time left, fatigue, momentum, and what's at stake. Notice that you and the opponent are mostly known in advance. Conditions and the state of the contest aren't. That's why good plans carry contingencies.

### 4. one factor, two consequences
> The strongest answers take one factor and show it working differently in the two settings. Take changing the plan. For the tennis player, one person decides and acts, so it costs seconds. For a team, the change has to be decided, communicated, understood the same way by everyone, and then executed together. Until then, some players run the old plan and some the new one. That isn't worse tactics. It's the cost of eleven decision-makers. Teams manage it with **structured flexibility**: a few rehearsed alternatives, each with a name and a trigger, so a change means calling something the squad already owns. And teams have advantages too. They can run several tactics at once, and change players mid-game. So individuals adapt faster, but teams can run more at once.

### 6. discussing means weighing
> The verb is discuss, so put the factors against each other. They often conflict. Exploit the opponent's weakness, or play to your own strength? Attack the weakness only if you can do it with a strength. Otherwise your strength usually wins, because a plan you can't carry out isn't a plan. Stick with the plan, or change it? Ask whether it's failing, or just not working yet. Change too early and you waste a good plan, too late and there's no time to recover. So set the trigger before the contest. And take a risk, or protect a lead? That depends on what's at stake and the time left. The same score late in a dead match and in an elimination final calls for different risks. Name the conflict, then say what resolves it.
