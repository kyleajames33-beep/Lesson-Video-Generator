### 1. hook (fire and ash)
> Light a log in a fire and within the hour it's ash, smoke and a warm room. Now try the reverse. Put the ash, smoke and warm air together and you will never get the **log** back. Yet a tree makes sugar and wood out of carbon dioxide and water, all day long. So why does a fire run all the way one way, while a tree can build the other way? To find out, we need two ideas from Year 11: **enthalpy**, which is about heat, and **entropy**, which is about spreading out.

### 2. non-equilibrium (what stays away from balance)
> Last time, a sealed flask settled into a mixture where the forward and reverse rates matched and the amounts stopped changing. A fire is different. Its change keeps going one way until the fuel or the oxygen runs out, and nothing **balances** it. A system where the net change is still going, with no steady balance, is called a **non-equilibrium system**. Combustion is one, and photosynthesis is another. They stay away from equilibrium for opposite reasons.

### 3a. recall: heat and spreading
> First, heat. The enthalpy change, written delta H, is the heat a reaction gives out or takes in. A negative delta H means heat flows out into the **surroundings**, like a fire. Giving out heat spreads energy into the surroundings, and that is why it helps a reaction. Second, spreading. Entropy, written S, measures how spread out the particles and the energy are, in other words how many ways they can be arranged. The change in entropy is written delta S. A tightly packed crystal has low entropy. When it breaks into separate molecules that **fly** about, the particles are more spread out, so delta S is positive. Keep the two apart: heat leaving is the delta H term, and the particles spreading out in the reacting mixture is the entropy term.

Visual: coded energy-level diagram draws live: a high level "reactants", a lower level "products", a terracotta arrow dropping between them labelled "delta H negative: heat out", heat arrows radiating into a blue-grey "surroundings" box on "surroundings". Then the diagram ghosts and a block of 20 tightly packed indigo particles in a grid breaks and scatters across the frame on "fly" (particles only, no heat arrows), labelled "entropy up, delta S positive". On the last sentence two tags sit side by side: terracotta "delta H term: heat leaving" and indigo "entropy term: particles in the mixture".

### 3b. recall: delta G
> A reaction is helped along by giving out heat and by spreading out. You met the combination in Year 11, as one number called the free energy change, delta G, which equals delta H minus **T delta S**, where T is the temperature in kelvin, which is always positive, so T delta S has the same sign as delta S. If delta G is negative, the reaction can run on its own. The word for that is **spontaneous**.

Visual: the equation delta G = delta H - T delta S writes on large and is held alone on screen, with the two terms underlined (heat terracotta, spreading indigo) and "T in kelvin" in a small chip. ### 4. worked example: burning glucose
> Glucose plus oxygen makes carbon dioxide and water. Burning one mole of glucose gives a delta H of **minus two thousand eight hundred and three** kilojoules, and at room temperature T delta S is **plus seventy-eight** kilojoules. First, heat. Delta H is negative, so heat flows out, and that helps. Second, spreading. A solid sugar crystal and six oxygen molecules become six carbon dioxide gas molecules and six liquid water molecules. The crystal is gone and seven particles become **twelve**, so there are more ways to arrange them. Entropy goes up, and T delta S is positive. Now, delta G is delta H minus T delta S, which is minus two thousand eight hundred and three, minus seventy-eight, giving minus **two thousand eight hundred and eighty-one** kilojoules per mole. Both terms push the same way, but the heat term does almost all the work, and spreading adds a little more.

### 5a. how strong
> How big is big? Minus two thousand eight hundred and eighty-one kilojoules is a very strong pull towards products. The reverse reaction is so slight that the leftover reactants can't even be **measured**, so we treat the reaction as running to completion. As a rough guide, a delta G close to zero, within a few tens of kilojoules, leaves a real mixture of reactants and products, like the flask last time. A delta G hundreds of kilojoules below zero leaves essentially all products. That is why ash never becomes a log again.

### 6b. photosynthesis: what holds a leaf away from equilibrium
> So how does a leaf do it? A leaf is an **open system**. Sunlight keeps supplying energy, carbon dioxide keeps arriving and oxygen leaves. Cut off the light and photosynthesis **stops**, because nothing supplies the energy any more. The leaf's sugar can still be broken down by respiration, but that is a separate process, and it runs in the light and in the dark. So it is the constant light input that keeps photosynthesis going, away from equilibrium.

### 11. key notes (quiet)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Enthalpy change (delta H) is the heat a reaction gives out (negative) or takes in (positive). Entropy (S) measures how spread out the particles and energy are.
- Delta G = delta H - T delta S, with T in kelvin (always positive). A negative delta G means the reaction is spontaneous (it can run on its own). A positive delta G means it needs energy fed in.
- A non-equilibrium system has a net change still going, with no steady balance.
- Burning glucose: delta H is negative and entropy increases, so delta G is very negative and the reaction runs essentially to completion. In any combustion, work out both delta H and T delta S, because entropy can fall (hydrogen burning) and the heat term can still win.
- Photosynthesis (the glucose case) is that combustion reversed: delta H is positive and entropy decreases, so delta G is large and positive. It runs only while light keeps supplying energy to an open system.
- Delta G shows where a change is heading, not how fast it gets there.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen, shown again here): *Heat out and spread out: it can run on its own. Heat in and packed in: it needs energy fed in all the time.* Condition tag: "when both terms agree".

Assumptions: delta H negative with entropy increasing gives negative delta G at every temperature, and delta H positive with entropy decreasing gives positive delta G at every temperature, because delta G = delta H - T delta S has both terms pushing the same way. True for temperatures above 0 K (T positive) with delta H and delta S treated as constant. "Can run on its own" means spontaneous (negative delta G) and does **not** mean fast; the wording avoids "push" (used earlier only for the driving force) and avoids "runs", which could be heard as speed. Scene 7 says out loud that the aid applies only when the terms agree and that disagreeing signs need delta G worked out. It does not apply to mixed-sign cases: ammonium nitrate dissolving (heat in, spread out) and the hydrogen burning attempt (heat out, packed in) need the calculation. "Spread out" means the spreading of the reacting particles, the same meaning as the entropy term in the attempt, not the heat spreading into the surroundings. The gas-moles shortcut would give no answer for glucose (6 gas moles to 6) and must not be added.

The bank's "Enthalpy wins cold, entropy wins hot" is **not used**: it solves same-sign cases through temperature, and no scene here has a same-sign example to tie it to. It also belongs more naturally with the entropy-driven examples (ammonium nitrate, calcium carbonate) that this video leaves out. The bank's "Gas moles are the biggest entropy clue" is used implicitly (attempt condition "three gas molecules become a liquid"), not as a named aid. It is deliberately not added to scene 3: for glucose the gas moles stay at six, so the shortcut would not help.
