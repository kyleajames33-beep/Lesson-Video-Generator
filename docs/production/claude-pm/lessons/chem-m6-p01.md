- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Colour chart (constructed, typical of school results; red cabbage extract varies with variety, extraction and dilution): pH 2 red, 4 pink, 6 violet, 7 purple, 8 blue, 10 blue-green, 12 green; above about 13 yellow-green to yellow. These are the hues the coded swatches use (hex values chosen at build to match the painted purple and green plates). The narration says "a typical result" and "your batch may differ a little". Well C (0.10 mol/L NaOH, pH about 13) is green at first and can drift to yellow-green within minutes (published charts: pH 12 green to greenish-yellow, pH 14 yellow). Read it straight away (scene 6 tag); the cycle avoids this by adding base only until green (about pH 11.5). (b) Chemistry of the colour: red cabbage contains a mixture of acylated cyanidin glycosides (anthocyanins). At low pH the red flavylium cation dominates; as the pH rises it loses protons to the violet neutral quinoidal base and the blue anionic quinoidal base; hydration to colourless forms and ring-opening to yellow chalcones also occur, especially at high pH and over time. Several proton losses and the mixture of related pigments give the series of colours. The learner copy says each pigment "behaves like the indicator HIn", "can lose more than one proton" and that the extract is "a mix of several of them": a fair simplification of the proton-transfer steps and the mixture; the hydration and ring-opening steps are not learner copy except as "change shape and start to break down" in scene 9. The green seen at about pH 10 to 12 is usually a blend of blue anionic quinoidal forms with some yellow chalcone, not one deprotonated form. The screen's single green token and the line "red forms ⇌ H⁺ + green forms" are learner simplifications of several proton-transfer steps; narration keeps "forms" plural ("the forms that have lost protons"). (c) Reversibility and its limit: within roughly pH 1 to 11 over a few minutes, the proton-transfer steps reverse quickly, so the same colours return. In strong base (pH above about 12) over minutes to hours, ring-opening and oxidative degradation progressively destroy the pigment, and the colour turns yellow and may not return on adding acid. The narration says "can turn yellow" and "acid may not bring the red back"; no time or pH threshold is stated to learners. This is the site's "alternative explanation for a colour change that is not an acid-base equilibrium". (d) Drop counts (a drop taken as 0.05 mL; 0.10 mol/L HCl and NaOH; the extract's own buffering ignored): 2.0 mL extract; +10 drops HCl (0.50 mL, 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol) gives [H₃O⁺] about 0.020 mol/L, pH about 1.7 (red); +12 drops NaOH (6.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol) leaves 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol excess OH⁻ in 3.1 mL, pH about 11.5 (green); +12 drops HCl leaves 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol excess H₃O⁺ in 3.7 mL, pH about 1.9 (red). Each later cycle adds 24 drops (1.2 mL). After the first acid step and three full cycles the sample is about 6.1 mL, holding 2.0 mL of extract: about a third of the starting pigment concentration, so visibly paler, with the same hues. The screen drop counter shows "+10 drops", "+12 drops"; the volumes and pH values are not learner copy. (e) Why heat: hot water denatures membrane proteins and disrupts the membranes of the cabbage cells, so anthocyanins (stored in the cell vacuoles) leak out faster; anthocyanins are water-soluble glycosides. Long boiling can degrade them, so "hot water for ten minutes" (site) rather than boiling. Cooling: safer handling, and colour comparisons made at room temperature (anthocyanin colour shifts slightly with temperature). Extract keeps for a day or two in a refrigerator. (f) Equal drops: the eye judges hue more reliably when the pigment concentration is similar across wells; very dilute purple can look blue-grey, very concentrated green can look dark. Three drops into about 20 drops (1 mL) of each reference solution. (g) Reference solutions: commercial buffers at pH 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10 and 12, or solutions checked with a calibrated meter (P03). The extract is added in small amounts so it barely changes the buffers' pH. (h) A2: 0.10 mol/L NaHCO₃ has pH about 8.3 (½(pKa₁ + pKa₂) = ½(6.35 + 10.33) = 8.34). The feedback tag shows a meter reading "8.34" as the constructed check value. A household baking soda solution of unknown concentration gives a similar value (the amphiprotic estimate does not depend strongly on concentration). (i) Household samples (scene 10): white vinegar about pH 2.4 to 3 (pink-red; "between two and four" from the chart); an ammonia-based cleaner about pH 11 to 11.5 (green; "about eleven or twelve"). The narration says "about"; the meter readouts are constructed ("2.5" and "11.4"; 2.5 sits inside the measured 2.4 to 2.6 for 4 to 5 % white vinegar, science check M4). (j) Significant figures and precision: pH from the colour chart is reported as a range or "about", never with decimal places; the meter displays 0.01 (P03), and a calibrated meter is accurate to within a few hundredths (display resolution is not accuracy). Volumes: 2.0 mL, 20 g, 100 mL are method values. ### 2. where the colour comes from
> The purple comes from pigments called **anthocyanins**, stored inside the cabbage's cells, and they dissolve in water. ### 7. the reversibility cycle
> Now the key test. Put two millilitres of extract in a small beaker and add acid until it's **red**. Then add sodium hydroxide, drop by drop, until it just turns **green**. Then acid again, until it's red. Base removes hydronium, so the pigment's equilibrium shifts to the forms that have lost protons, and acid shifts it **back**. Run the cycle **three times**, in the same beaker.

### 8. why the same sample, and why three cycles
> Why the same sample? Fresh wells only show each colour separately. One sample going back and forth shows the reaction **runs both ways**. Three cycles check that you didn't misread a single return. The colour gets **paler** each time, because the sample keeps getting diluted, but the **same two colours** keep returning. That's evidence the pigment isn't being used up.

### 11. variables, validity, reliability and accuracy
> What are the variables? You change the **pH** of the solution, and you observe the **colour**. Keep the extract batch, the number of drops, the volume of solution and the lighting the same. **Validity**: one batch of extract and the water well mean the colour change comes from the pH alone. **Reliability**: repeat wells, and repeat cycles, give the same colours. **Accuracy**: a colour estimate is good to about a pH unit, and a calibrated meter does much better.

### 19. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Red cabbage's colour comes from a mixture of anthocyanins, which behave like weak-acid indicators (HIn) that can lose more than one proton, so the extract shows several colours that change gradually.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the extract shows several gradual colours: several proton losses and a mixture of related pigments whose colours blend, both now said in narration (2). Why hot water: heat damages the membranes in the cabbage cells, so the pigment leaks out, and it dissolves in water (3). Why filter: you judge the solution's colour, not leaf pieces (3). Why cool: safer handling and fair colour comparisons (3, screen tag). Why calibrate: a colour means nothing until it is matched to a known pH (4). Why equal drops: colour depth depends on pigment amount (4). Why the chart's precision is limited: its spacing (5). Why the water well: dilution alone pales the colour, so the acid or base must cause a change of colour (6). Why the colour reverses: base removes hydronium and acid replaces it, shifting the pigment's equilibrium (7, C13 callback). Why one sample: only it can show a change in both directions (8). Why three cycles: a single return could be misread (8). Why paler each cycle: dilution (8). Why a colour may not return: the pigment molecules change shape and break down in strong base over time, so well C is read straight away (6, 9). Why a poor titration indicator: gradual, stepwise changes across the scale, not one sharp change inside the jump (10).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** the extract has one transition range (5); the extract "has a pH" (6, tag); a paler colour means a pH change (6, 8); fresh wells show reversibility (8, A1, crossed); every colour change is an acid-base change (9); a colour match gives an exact pH (A2, crossed); dilute 0.10 mol/L acid and base are corrosive, or both equally harmless (12, said precisely: HCl low hazard, NaOH just under the irritant limit).

- Household values (author note i): white vinegar (about 4 to 5 percent ethanoic acid, about 0.7 to 0.8 mol/L; with Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, pH = ½(4.74 − log 0.75) = ½(4.74 + 0.12) = 2.43) reads about 2.4 to 2.6, inside "between 2 and 4" on the chart; the constructed meter value is 2.5 (Revision 2; 2.6 was at the top of the realistic range). ## Safety facts check (self-check, 21 Oct; science check to confirm)

- **Sodium hydroxide, 0.10 mol/L:** 4.0 g/L, about 0.40 percent by mass. EU CLP Annex VI specific concentration limits for NaOH: Skin Corr. 1A at 5 percent or more, Skin Corr. 1B from 2 to under 5 percent, Skin Irrit. 2 and Eye Irrit. 2 from 0.5 to under 2 percent. At 0.40 percent it falls just below the irritant limits (0.5 percent). Supplier SDSs for 0.1 M NaOH vary: ALS not classified; Agilent Australia (0.1 N) H290 only; American Elements H314 (see the m6p04 science check). CLEAPSS HazCard 91 classes 0.05 to 0.5 mol/L as IRRITANT and enforces eye protection. Learner copy (narration, matching P04): "sits just under the irritant limit, so treat a splash as harmful"; screen: "just below the EU irritant limit (0.5 %); supplier sheets vary, so check yours". - **Hydrochloric acid, 0.10 mol/L:** 3.65 g/L, about 0.36 percent. CLP limits for HCl: Skin Corr. 1B at 25 percent or more; Skin Irrit. 2 and Eye Irrit. 2 apply from 10 to under 25 percent. STOT SE 3 applies at 10 percent and above. At 0.36 percent it is not classified for health hazards and carries H290 at most on supplier SDSs (P02 recorded the same for 1 mol/L). Learner copy: "low hazard"; safety glasses still worn.
- **Hot water:** near-boiling water scalds; the teacher prepares the extract and cools it (site safety box; plan row).
