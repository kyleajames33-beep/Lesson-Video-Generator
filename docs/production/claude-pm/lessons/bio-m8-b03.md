### 2. three words, and a range
> First, three words that sound alike. **Glucose** is the sugar dissolved in your blood, the fuel your cells use in respiration. **Glycogen** is a store of glucose: long, branched chains of it packed inside liver and muscle cells. **Glucagon** is a hormone. So glycogen is the store, and glucagon is the messenger. Blood glucose is measured in millimoles per litre. When you haven't eaten for a while, it usually sits around four to six. After a meal it rises for a while, often to about seven or eight in a healthy person, then comes back down.

### 7. two hormones, one balance
> Why two hormones? Think of driving at a steady speed over hills. Going downhill you speed up, so you press the brake. Going uphill you slow down, so you press the accelerator. Insulin works like the brake on rising glucose, and glucagon like the accelerator when it falls. With only one pedal, you could ease off it, but you couldn't actively push the other way. Where the analogy breaks: both hormones are released at some level all the time, and it's the balance between them that shifts.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Glucose: the sugar in blood, fuel for respiration. Glycogen: stored glucose in liver and muscle. Glucagon: a hormone. Usual fasting range about 4 to 6 mmol/L.
- Pancreatic islets are receptor and control centre: beta cells detect a rise and release insulin; alpha cells detect a fall and release glucagon.
- Glucose rises → insulin → muscle and fat cells take up more glucose (more transporters in the membrane), liver and muscle build glycogen (glycogenesis) → blood glucose falls, and insulin falls back to a low level.
- Glucose falls → glucagon → liver splits glycogen into glucose (glycogenolysis) and releases it → blood glucose rises.
- Both loops are negative feedback. The pancreas signals; the liver, muscle and fat cells are the effectors. On a graph, insulin rises after glucose.

Memory: Insulin lets glucose in. Glucagon acts when glucose has gone.
