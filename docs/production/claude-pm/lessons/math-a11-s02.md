### 1. hook: one cone, two sets
> Here's an ice-cream shop's cabinet, and one cone it sold today, with a scoop of chocolate and a scoop of vanilla. Most cones are easy to sort, but this one isn't. ### 2. in both, or in either
> Let's start with numbers. Take A, the set one, two, three, four, and B, the set three, four, five. The elements in A and in B are three and four. That set is called the intersection, it's written with this cap shape, and we read it A intersect **B**. Now collect everything that's in A, or in B. That's one, two, three, four and five. It's called the union, written with a cup shape, and we read it A union **B**. In maths, "or" includes both, so three and four are in the union too. But each element is listed once. So the union has five elements, not four plus three, which is seven. That would count three and four **twice**.

Visual: clear change: two braces, 52 px, "A = {1, 2, 3, 4}" (blue) and "B = {3, 4, 5}" (teal). The 3 and 4 in both braces ring violet. On "B" (first) the row "A ∩ B = {3, 4}" (violet, 52 px) builds with "∩" drawn large; hand tag "∩: in both". Row "|A ∪ B| = 5", tag "listed once". On "twice" a red slip "|A ∪ B| = 4 + 3 = 7" writes and is crossed, caption "3 and 4 counted twice".

### 3. the Venn diagram
> A Venn diagram draws all of this at once. The rectangle is the universal set U, everything we're talking about. Each set is a circle, and the circles overlap where elements belong to both. That makes four regions. A only holds one and two. Both holds three and four. B only holds five. And outside both circles is anything in U that's in neither set. If U is the numbers one to seven, that's six and **seven**. Every element of U lands in exactly one region. Now we can shade. The overlap is A intersect B. Both circles together, overlap included, is A union **B**. So the intersection always sits inside the union. And everything outside A is A complement, from last **video**.

### 4. worked example: fifty cones

Vanilla only is twenty minus eight, **twelve**. That's forty-two cones with at least one of the two flavours, which is C union V. So neither is fifty minus forty-two, which is **eight**. That's V complement, everything outside the vanilla circle, twenty-two plus eight, which is **thirty**. Writing thirty in the chocolate only region is the **slip**. That counts the eight twice.

### 5. disjoint sets
> Some sets share nothing at all. Let P be the prime numbers under ten, that's two, three, five and seven. Let E be the even numbers under ten, leaving out two, so four, six and eight. Is any number in both? **No**. So P intersect E is the empty set, and we call P and E disjoint. On a Venn diagram, the circles pull apart and the overlap **disappears**. Back at the shop, the cones with two chocolate scoops and the cones with two vanilla scoops are disjoint too, since no cone is both. But they aren't complements, because a strawberry cone is in neither. Disjoint only means nothing in **common**. In probability, disjoint events get another name, mutually exclusive.

Visual: stage change: braces "P = {2, 3, 5, 7}" (blue) and "E = {4, 6, 8}" (teal), 52 px; tokens drop into a two-circle `VennDiagram` with U = {1, ..., 9} (ink). On "disappears" the circles slide apart (`VennDiagram` disjoint mode), the violet overlap shrinks to nothing, "∅" writes in the gap; tag "disjoint: nothing in common". ## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why sets can overlap: one cone is in both (1). Why the union is listed once: an element belongs to the union whether it is in one set or two (2). Why regions add to |U|: each element is in exactly one region (3). Why the overlap goes first: each circle's total includes it (4). Why disjoint is not complement: an element can be in neither (5).
