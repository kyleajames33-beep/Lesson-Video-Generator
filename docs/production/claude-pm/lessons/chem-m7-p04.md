## Decisions

- **Main question:** *Warm an alcohol with acidified dichromate and it turns green. That shows something was oxidised, but what did you actually make? How do you make, collect and test the products of primary and secondary alcohols so the evidence points to one answer, and do it safely?* Heading on screen: "Orange to green: so what did you make?"
- **Site sources used:** L12 card 3: the oxidation summary table (primary: distillation, aldehyde; primary, excess, reflux: acid; secondary: ketone; tertiary: stays orange; scenes 3 and 5 to 7); its Must Do "The orange→green colour change confirms oxidation occurred, it does NOT identify the specific product. To determine the product, you need: (1) the conditions (distillation vs. reflux); AND (2) a secondary test on the product (Tollens' for aldehyde vs. litmus for acid)" (the whole video's spine; scenes 1, 8 and 9); its Safety box (chromium(VI) toxic, corrosive, carcinogenic; gloves, goggles; spent chromium as heavy-metal waste, not the sink; "Schools normally run dichromate oxidations as a small-scale teacher demonstration with full PPE and ventilation"; scene 11 and the risk table, with its concentrated-acid wording corrected, see site corrections); Worked Example 2's design (three alcohols, dichromate, distil, Tollens', litmus), rebuilt with new alcohols (C₃ isomers plus a C₄ tertiary alcohol, not the site's C₄H₁₀O set). L13 card 3: Tollens' procedure ("freshly prepared ... warm gently in a water bath (~50°C) for 2–3 minutes"), Fehling's or Benedict's (blue to brick-red), the Complete Test Results Summary (scene 8's evidence table), the Tollens' safety Must Do (scene 11, with its compound corrected). Site order kept: oxidise, then test.
- **Why these alcohols:** propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol share the formula C₃H₈O, so the only difference is the position of the OH: the fairest comparison a class can make. No tertiary alcohol has three carbons, so 2-methylpropan-2-ol (C₄) is the tertiary comparison; the narration says so in one clause (a validity point, not hidden). Propanal (bp 48 °C) and propanone (56 °C) boil well below their alcohols (97 °C and 82 °C), so both distil off cleanly; ethanal (21 °C) would be harder to collect. These alcohols and products differ from c08a's worked examples (ethanol to ethanal and ethanoic acid; propan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol for the class rule), except that propan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol reappear here as real reagents, which is the point of a practical companion.
- **Control and known samples (Revision 2):** a tube of acidified dichromate with water instead of alcohol, warmed with the others (shows that warming alone does not turn it green); a glucose solution as a known aldehyde for Tollens' and Benedict's only (glucose's open-chain form has an aldehyde group, it is safe to handle, and a positive result shows those aldehyde-test reagents work); a known dilute acid for litmus; water as the no-change control in every test. A negative test on a distillate is evidence against an aldehyde, not proof of absence under every condition. Author note (c).
- **Main teaching move:** the colour change answers one question (was it oxidised?), the conditions decide the product (distil or reflux; primary or secondary), and the test on the separated product answers the other (what is it?). Validity depends on testing the distillate: the flask still holds sulfuric acid (turns litmus red whatever the product) and green or orange chromium ions (would hide a Benedict's colour change). The distillate holds the product with some water, separated from those non-volatile reagents; it is not a pure product.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) green shows oxidation, not the product (1); (2) the aim and a fair set of alcohols, plus a control (2); (3) part A sorts the alcohols: primary and secondary green, tertiary and water orange (3); (4) one variable changed, the rest kept the same (4); (5) distil as it forms to catch the aldehyde, because it boils far below the alcohol and leaves before further oxidation; the distilling temperature supports the identification (5); (6) reflux with excess oxidant reaches the acid because the condenser returns the aldehyde to the oxidant (6); (7) the secondary alcohol gives a ketone that dichromate doesn't oxidise further (7); (8) each product has its own test pattern (8); (9) test the distillate, not the flask, and run known samples: validity (9); (10) repeats agree: reliability; matching known samples and boiling temperatures supports the identification: accuracy; a faint acid result in the aldehyde sample is an investigation prompt on screen, not a spoken diagnosis (10); (11) the hazards and their controls (11); memory aid (12); attempts and brief feedback (13 to 16); decision rule and handoff (17); key notes (18).
- **Entry knowledge:** chem-m7-c08a (class rule, [O], acidified potassium dichromate orange to green, distil for the aldehyde, reflux with excess for the acid, secondary to ketone, tertiary stays orange, the green colour shows oxidation not the product, chromium(VI) hazard stated once). chem-m7-c08b (aldehyde and ketone structures and names; Tollens' silver mirror, Fehling's or Benedict's brick-red, both only with the aldehyde; dichromate test; the flow "oxidise, distil, test the product"; Tollens' made fresh). skills-s07 and chem-m7-p03 (validity, reliability, accuracy; word, feature, fix; hazard, harm, control). chem-m7-p01 (the organic waste container; GHS codes on a risk table). chem-m6 (litmus: blue turns red in acid). Year 11: distillation and reflux apparatus from c06 and c08a plates.

No new science terms.
- **Attempt systems (unseen, every condition visible, HSC wording, original):** (A1) "In a teacher demonstration, propan-1-ol was heated with excess acidified potassium dichromate under reflux for 20 minutes. The flask was cooled and a strip of blue litmus paper was dipped into the reaction mixture. The litmus turned red, and a student concluded that propanoic acid had formed. Explain why this conclusion is not valid, and describe how the method should be changed to give a valid conclusion." Tests the validity move (the flask contains sulfuric acid). Answer: the acidified dichromate contains sulfuric acid, which turns litmus red whether or not propanoic acid formed, so the test does not measure the product; distil off the product and test the distillate (and/or test a blank of acidified dichromate treated the same way). Crossed mistake: "repeat the litmus test several times" (repeats check reliability, not validity; s07's word, feature, fix). (A2) "Pentan-2-ol and 2-methylbutan-2-ol were each warmed with acidified potassium dichromate solution. Each mixture was then distilled, and the distillate was warmed with freshly prepared Tollens' reagent. - **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Volumes and concentrations (screen only, small grey, for teachers; not spoken): 2 mL of acidified potassium dichromate solution (about 0.1 mol L⁻¹ K₂Cr₂O₇ in about 1 mol L⁻¹ H₂SO₄, teacher-made), 5 drops of each alcohol, water bath about 60 °C for about 5 minutes; reflux about 20 minutes; Tollens' warmed in a water bath at about 50 to 60 °C for 2 to 3 minutes (site L13). Science check to confirm these are typical school values. (b) Revision 2: every dichromate step (parts A and B) is a teacher demonstration in a fume cupboard. The science check could not verify the current CSIS Appendix D use category for potassium dichromate (staff login) and found no basis for "many schools let only the teacher handle the solid", so the design takes the safest teaching choice and does not assert any student permission. This is not a claim that dilute dichromate is banned; the banned item is concentrated chromic-acid cleaning mixture. Root-pending (Open decisions). (c) Glucose as a reference aldehyde: open-chain glucose has an aldehyde group; it gives a positive Benedict's and Tollens' test. It is a sugar, not a product of this practical, so it is labelled "known aldehyde" and never presented as an oxidation product. (d) Propanal distillation: the aldehyde is distilled off as it forms, with the oxidant added to the alcohol slowly (or the alcohol to warm oxidant), so the aldehyde meets little excess oxidant; the screen says "add slowly; distil at once". (e) Acid by reflux, then distil a sample: propanoic acid (bp 141 °C) co-distils with water from the aqueous mixture; the distillate is a dilute acid, enough to turn blue litmus red. (f) Accuracy of identification: in an aqueous mixture the still-head temperature is only a guide (water and azeotropes shift it); the narration says "supports" and "near", not "proves". (g) A faint red on litmus from the aldehyde distillate has several possible causes (acid carried over by slow distillation or entrainment, oxidation in air during storage, dirty apparatus, method errors), so it is shown only as an on-screen investigation prompt, not a spoken single diagnosis (science S4). (h) 2-methylpropan-2-ol melts at about 25 °C, so in a cool laboratory it may be solid; the risk row says the teacher warms the bottle in the water bath, never over a flame. (i) Pronunciation: "propan-one-ol", "propan-two-ol", "two-methylpropan-two-ol", "pentan-two-ol", "two-methylbutan-two-ol", "pentan-two-one"; "Tollens'" as "tollen's"; "Benedict's" as written; "chromium six" for chromium(VI).

### 1. hook (green, but what?)
> Warm three alcohols with acidified dichromate, and two of them turn it **green**. That tells you something was oxidised. It doesn't tell you **what** formed: an aldehyde, a ketone and an acid can all come from the same orange solution. Here you'll make each product, collect it and test it, so the evidence points to **one** answer.

### 2. the aim, and a fair set of alcohols
