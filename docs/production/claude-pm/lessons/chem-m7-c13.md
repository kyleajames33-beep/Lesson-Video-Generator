Syllabus dotpoint (as printed):

"draft and construct flow charts to show reaction pathways for chemical synthesis, including those that involve more than one step"

This video covers planning a multi-step synthesis from a target and its allowed starting materials, and drawing it as a flow chart with every reagent and condition. Chem-m7-c10 introduced the flow chart with one two-step route; chem-m7-g03 practises harder targets.

**Boundary:** this video starts by choosing a target functional group and the starting materials, and stops at a justified multi-step pathway with conditions. It teaches: the reactions from c04a to c12 sit on one reaction map, each box a functional group family and each arrow a known reaction with its own reagents and conditions; the alcohol is the hub, so many routes pass through it; a four-move method: (1) name the functional groups in the target and the starting materials and count carbons (these reactions change functional groups and keep the chain length; only ester and amide formation join two molecules, so their carbons add); (2) work backwards from the target, asking which reaction makes its group (an ester comes from an acid and an alcohol, cut at the single-bonded oxygen; an acid from a primary alcohol by oxidation; an alcohol from an alkene and steam, or from a haloalkane), until you reach a starting material; (3) choose between possible routes: when the position of a group matters, start from a haloalkane that already has its halogen on the right carbon, because hydroxide takes the halogen's place on the same carbon, while water or HBr on propene can go on either carbon and goes mainly to the middle one, so it gives mainly the wrong product (Revision 2); (4) write the route forwards as a flow chart, in the order you'd carry it out, a compound in each box with its name, reagents and conditions on each arrow, distillation for an aldehyde and reflux with excess oxidant for an acid, an equilibrium arrow for hydration and esterification; then check every arrow: no single step turns an alkane into an alcohol (halogen and UV light first, giving a mixture); a ketone has no hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon, so acidified dichromate doesn't oxidise it further (a dead end), and a tertiary alcohol doesn't oxidise at all; each step loses some product, so a shorter route usually gives a higher overall yield; worked example: propyl ethanoate from ethene and 1-bromopropane, two arms (ethene → ethanol → ethanoic acid; 1-bromopropane → propan-1-ol) joining at esterification. **Not here:** reaction mechanisms and the words nucleophile and elimination as mechanisms (author note b); alcoholic NaOH elimination (not shown in this video from Revision 2; author note b); Markovnikov's rule by name (c04a author note b; author note c; the narration says only that propene's product is mainly the middle-carbon one); reduction reactions and reagents such as LiAlH₄ (not Module 7; author note d); the five-component "top-band" answer template and conditions-table timing drills (site L20; author note e); isolation and purification after each step (c10 and p05; one grey chip only); yield calculations (author note f); industrial synthesis such as aspirin (site hook; site correction 2); the practice set (chem-m7-g03).

- **Does it explain causes?** Why count carbons: these reactions change groups but keep the chain, so a mismatch means a joining step or a wrong start (3). Why work backwards: the target's group tells you the last reaction, and each precursor's group tells you the one before (4). Why a haloalkane instead of propene: hydroxide takes the halogen's place on the same carbon, while water or HBr on propene goes mainly to the middle carbon, so the haloalkane must already have its halogen on the right carbon (5). Why write forwards: that is the order you'd carry the route out (6). Why reflux, not distillation, for the acid: the aldehyde can't escape before it's oxidised again (6, one-sentence c08a recall). Why the double arrows: hydration and esterification are equilibria (6, c06 and c10 recall). Why alkane to alcohol fails: no reaction we've met does it in one step; UV substitution first, which gives a mixture (7). Why the ketone is a dead end: its carbonyl carbon has no hydrogen to remove, so the acid needs the primary alcohol (7, c08a and c10 recall). Why keep routes short: every step loses product, so overall yield falls (7).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** arrows that don't exist (alkane to alcohol; ketone to acid; 7, crossed); distillation gives the acid (6, said positively); single arrow on equilibria (6); routing an alkene through HX to fix the OH's position (5, crossed chip); choosing an alkene start when the position matters (feedback 1, crossed); the wrong alcohol is oxidised for a named ester (feedback 2, crossed); the shortest route is automatically best (7, "usually", softened as the site's route quality note).
