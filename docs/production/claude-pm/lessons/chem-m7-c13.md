Syllabus dotpoint (as printed):

"draft and construct flow charts to show reaction pathways for chemical synthesis, including those that involve more than one step"

This video covers planning a multi-step synthesis from a target and its allowed starting materials, and drawing it as a flow chart with every reagent and condition. Chem-m7-c10 introduced the flow chart with one two-step route; chem-m7-g03 practises harder targets.

**Boundary:** this video starts by choosing a target functional group and the starting materials, and stops at a justified multi-step pathway with conditions. It teaches: the reactions from c04a to c12 sit on one reaction map, each box a functional group family and each arrow a known reaction with its own reagents and conditions; the alcohol is the hub, so many routes pass through it; a four-move method: (1) name the functional groups in the target and the starting materials and count carbons (these reactions change functional groups and keep the chain length; only ester and amide formation join two molecules, so their carbons add); (2) work backwards from the target, asking which reaction makes its group (an ester comes from an acid and an alcohol, cut at the single-bonded oxygen; an acid from a primary alcohol by oxidation; an alcohol from an alkene and steam, or from a haloalkane), until you reach a starting material; (3) choose between possible routes: when the position of a group matters, a haloalkane with the halogen on the right carbon fixes it, because hydroxide takes the halogen's place on the same carbon, while adding water to an unsymmetrical alkene can put the OH on either carbon and gives a mixture; (4) write the route forwards as a flow chart, a compound in each box with its name, reagents and conditions on each arrow, distillation for an aldehyde and reflux with excess oxidant for an acid, an equilibrium arrow for hydration and esterification; then check every arrow: no single step turns an alkane into an alcohol (halogen and UV light first, giving a mixture); a ketone has no hydrogen on its carbonyl carbon, so acidified dichromate doesn't oxidise it further (a dead end), and a tertiary alcohol doesn't oxidise at all; each step loses some product, so a shorter route usually gives a higher overall yield; worked example: propyl ethanoate from ethene and 1-bromopropane, two arms (ethene → ethanol → ethanoic acid; 1-bromopropane → propan-1-ol) joining at esterification. **Not here:** reaction mechanisms and the words nucleophile and elimination as mechanisms (author note b); alcoholic NaOH elimination (screen chip only, as in c06); Markovnikov's rule and which product of an unsymmetrical alkene is major (c04a author note b; author note c); reduction reactions and reagents such as LiAlH₄ (not Module 7; author note d); the five-component "top-band" answer template and conditions-table timing drills (site L20; author note e); isolation and purification after each step (c10 and p05; one grey chip only); yield calculations (author note f); industrial synthesis such as aspirin (site hook; site correction 2); the practice set (chem-m7-g03).

### 2. the reaction map
> Good news: you've already met every reaction you need. Put them all on one **map**. Each box is a family of compounds, and each arrow is a reaction from an earlier video, with its own reagents and conditions. Alkenes add water to make alcohols. Haloalkanes swap their halogen for O H. Alcohols oxidise to aldehydes, ketones or acids. Acids join alcohols to make **esters**, or amines to make amides. And alcohols can lose water, back to an alkene. Alkanes need a halogen and U V light before they can join in at all. The alcohol sits in the **middle**, so many routes pass through it.

Visual: the board clears. Task chip "Map". On "map" the reaction map build runs: the nine family boxes place one at a time. ### 3. move 1: name the groups and count carbons

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why count carbons: these reactions change groups but keep the chain, so a mismatch means a joining step or a wrong start (3). Why work backwards: the target's group tells you the last reaction, and each precursor's group tells you the one before (4). Why a haloalkane instead of propene: hydroxide takes the halogen's place on the same carbon, while water on an unsymmetrical alkene can go on either carbon and gives a mixture (5). Why reflux, not distillation, for the acid: reflux returns the aldehyde to the flask so it meets more oxidant, while distillation lets it escape as ethanal (6). Why the double arrows: hydration and esterification are equilibria (6, c06 and c10 recall). Why alkane to alcohol fails: no reaction we've met does it in one step; UV substitution first, which gives a mixture (7). Why the ketone is a dead end: its carbonyl carbon has no hydrogen to remove (7, c08a recall). Why keep routes short: every step loses product, so overall yield falls (7).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** arrows that don't exist (alkane to alcohol; ketone to acid; 7, crossed); distillation gives the acid (6, said positively); single arrow on equilibria (6); the OH moves to another carbon in substitution (5; feedback 1, crossed); the wrong alcohol is oxidised for a named ester (feedback 2, crossed); the shortest route is automatically best (7, "usually", softened as the site's route quality note).
- **One idea at a time:** one model per scene (the target and starts; the map; one tally; one backward chain; one choice; one flow chart; one flawed chart); the map shrinks to a grey card and only the current path lights.
