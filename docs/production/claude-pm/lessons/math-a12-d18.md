### 2. variables, constraint and one variable
> Start by naming what can change. Let x metres be each side out from the wall, and y metres the side along it. The fence covers x, y and x, so two x plus y equals **forty**. That's the constraint, a fixed total that links the variables. The area is x times y. But y changes whenever x does, so we can't differentiate yet. Use the constraint to replace y. y equals forty minus two x, so A equals x, times forty minus two x, which is forty x minus two x **squared**. Now the area depends on x alone.

### 3. the physical domain
> Which values of x make a real pen? x has to be positive, or there's no width. And y has to be positive too. Forty minus two x is positive only when x is less than **twenty**. So the domain is x between zero and twenty, with neither end included. At each end the pen **collapses**. Near zero it's a thin strip along the wall, and near twenty it closes into a narrow strip sticking out from the wall. The area gets close to zero at both ends, but those aren't pens, so zero is a limit, not a value the area **reaches**. Stating the domain matters, because it decides which stationary points count, and what we compare at the ends.

### 4. optimise and conclude
> Now the calculus. A prime of x is forty minus four x, which is zero at x equals **ten**, inside the domain. Earlier in the course, we found a global maximum by comparing every candidate. Here the ends aren't candidates, because they aren't in the domain. So compare ten with the limits at the ends. A of ten is two hundred, bigger than both, and there's only one stationary point, so the area rises to two hundred and then falls. That makes two hundred the global **maximum**. Now answer the question. The pen is ten metres out from the wall and twenty metres along it, with an area of two hundred square **metres**. And here's why the square loses. Each metre along the wall costs one metre of fence, but each metre out from the wall costs two, because there are two of those sides. So the best pen is longer along the **wall**.

### 5. worked example: the open box (model)

For the domain, x must be positive to cut anything, and twenty-four minus two x must be positive to leave a base, so x is less than **twelve**.

### 6. worked example: the open box (solve and answer)
> Expand first. V is four x cubed, minus ninety-six x squared, plus five hundred and seventy-six x. So V prime is twelve x squared, minus a hundred and ninety-two x, plus five hundred and seventy-six. Take out twelve and factorise, to get twelve, times x minus four, times x minus **twelve**. Twelve isn't in the domain, and it would leave no base, so reject **it**. At x equals four, V is four times sixteen squared, which is one thousand and twenty-**four**. Near both ends the volume shrinks towards zero, so that's the greatest volume. Cut four-centimetre squares, and the box holds one thousand and twenty-four cubic **centimetres**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the area changes with a fixed fence: widening the pen shortens it (1, the pen reshaping). Why eliminate y: y changes whenever x does, so A must be in x alone (2, the nudge). Why x < 20: y must be positive (3). Why the ends are not answers: the pen collapses and the area only approaches zero (3). Why 200 is the greatest: it is the only stationary value and is larger than both end limits, so the area rises then falls (4). Why the square loses: each metre out from the wall costs two metres of fence and each metre along it costs one, so the best pen is longer along the wall, with y = 2x (4). Why x = 12 is rejected: it is outside the domain and leaves no base (6). Why the shorter side sets the domain in A2 (feedback 2).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** "the square is best" (1); "differentiate xy with y still in it" (2); "the ends are values to evaluate" (3, 4; key note 3); "keep the root outside the domain" (6; feedback 2, crossed); "answer with x" (4, 6; feedback 1, crossed; key note 4); "every optimisation has closed ends" (A1's unbounded domain, feedback 1).
- **One idea at a time:** one modelling step per scene in 2 to 4; one modelling step per sentence in 5; one row per cue in the tables.
- **Video makes it easier:** the student watches one fence reshape into four pens with live areas (1), sees y shrink when x grows (2), watches the pen collapse at both ends while the graph's point slides towards each open circle (3), watches the pen follow the graph's point over the top (4), and watches the card fold into the box at its best cut (5, 6). Each motion is the evidence for the next sentence.
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen HSC-style tasks with every condition and unit visible, 10 seconds each with the invitation to pause, nothing answer-bearing; A1 asks for a minimum on an open unbounded domain, A2 for a maximum with a root the domain rejects, so neither repeats a worked example's structure. One crossed mistake each; feedback about 32 s and 30 s (scenes 9 and 11).
- **Avoided double teaching:** d10's candidate comparison is recalled in one sentence ("we found a global maximum by comparing every candidate"); global maximum is not redefined (the inequality appears as a grey line); the second derivative test is not used; factorising is done on screen without explanation.
- **Syllabus coverage:** `ci443204dc` (2, 3, 5; attempts); `ci4a528415` (4, 6; ends checked as limits, no discontinuities of f′ in these models, said in 4); `ci5668f984` (4, 6, 9, 11: conclusions with units after rejecting values outside the domain); `cif819815e` (4, applied as A(x) ≤ 200 on the domain); `ci7e542996` (3, 4, 6: excluded ends examined as limits).
- **Out of scope by design:** whole-number domains; trigonometric and exponential objectives; three-dimensional problems with two constraints; the second derivative test as the global check.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 8; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A rectangular garden has an area of 50 m². It is fenced on three sides, and a straight wall forms the fourth side. Find the dimensions of the garden that use the least length of fencing, and state that least length."
Model answer: Let x m be each side perpendicular to the wall and y m the side along it. xy = 50, so y = 50/x. Fencing L = 2x + y = 2x + 50/x, for x > 0. L′(x) = 2 − 50/x² = 0 gives x² = 25, x = 5 (x > 0). As x → 0⁺ and as x → ∞, L → ∞, and x = 5 is the only stationary point, so it gives the least fencing. y = 10. The garden is 5 m by 10 m (10 m along the wall), using 20 m of fencing. Classic mistake (crossed): "least fencing: x = 5".

**Attempt 2 (scene 10; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "An open box is made from a rectangular sheet of card 16 cm by 10 cm by cutting equal squares of side x cm from each corner and folding up the sides. Find the value of x that gives the greatest volume, and state the greatest volume."
Model answer: V = x(16 − 2x)(10 − 2x) = 4x³ − 52x² + 160x, with 0 < x < 5 (10 − 2x > 0). V′ = 12x² − 104x + 160 = 4(3x − 20)(x − 2) = 0 gives x = 2 or x = 20/3; 20/3 > 5, so it is rejected. V(2) = 2 × 12 × 6 = 144; V → 0 as x → 0⁺ and x → 5⁻. Greatest volume 144 cm³ when x = 2 cm. Classic mistake (crossed): "x = 20/3".
