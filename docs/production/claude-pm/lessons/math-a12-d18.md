## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "How do a constraint and physical domain determine an optimum?" The answer, built in order: with a fixed fence the shape trades width against length, so the area changes (hook); the constraint links the variables and lets the area be written in one variable (2); the real pen exists only for 0 < x < 20, and at both ends it collapses, so the ends are limits, not pens (3); calculus finds the one stationary point, and comparing it with the end limits shows it is the greatest value the area reaches (4); the same steps, with a rejected root, give the box (5, 6).
- **The hook surprise (site `we-l20-1`, values re-ordered as a contrast; original framing).** Opens on d17's final frame. A `FencePen` plan view reshapes as x changes with the fence length fixed: 5 m out gives 5 × 30 = 150 m²; 15 m out gives 15 × 10 = 150 m²; a square pen, 40/3 m each way, gives 1600/9 ≈ 177.8 m²; 10 m out gives 10 × 20 = 200 m². - **Constraint and objective (scene 2; site five-step card, explained).** "Name what can change": x metres out from the wall, y metres along it. Constraint 2x + y = 40 (only three sides are fenced: site `we-l20-1` step 1). Objective A = xy. The reason to eliminate is given in plain words: y changes whenever x does, so A must be written in terms of x alone before differentiating (site Trap 04 and drill 5, explained rather than listed). - **Physical domain and limits (scene 3; d10's deferred "attained versus limit").** x > 0 for a width; y > 0, so 40 − 2x > 0, x < 20. Domain 0 < x < 20, both ends excluded (site's form). Answer in context: 10 m out from the wall, 20 m along it, area 200 m². ### 2. variables, constraint and one variable
> Start by naming what can change. Let x metres be each side out from the wall, and y metres the side along it. The fence covers x, y and x, so two x plus y equals **forty**. That's the constraint, a fixed total that links the variables. The area is x times y. But y changes whenever x does, so we can't differentiate yet. Use the constraint to replace y. y equals forty minus two x, so A equals x, times forty minus two x, which is forty x minus two x **squared**. Now the area depends on x alone.

### 3. the physical domain
> Which values of x make a real pen? x has to be positive, or there's no width. And y has to be positive too. Forty minus two x is positive only when x is less than **twenty**. So the domain is x between zero and twenty, with neither end included. At each end the pen **collapses**. Near zero it's a thin strip along the wall, and near twenty it's squashed flat against it. The area gets close to zero at both ends, but those aren't pens, so zero is a limit, not a value the area **reaches**. Stating the domain matters, because it decides which stationary points count, and what we compare at the ends.

### 4. optimise and conclude
> Now the calculus. A prime of x is forty minus four x, which is zero at x equals **ten**, inside the domain. Earlier in the course, we found a global maximum by comparing every candidate. Here the ends aren't candidates, because they aren't in the domain. So compare ten with the limits at the ends. A of ten is two hundred, bigger than both, and there's only one stationary point, so the area rises to two hundred and then falls. That makes two hundred the global **maximum**. Now answer the question. The pen is ten metres out from the wall and twenty metres along it, with an area of two hundred square **metres**. And here's why the square loses. Each metre along the wall costs one metre of fence, but each metre out from the wall costs two, because there are two of those sides. So the best pen is longer along the **wall**.

### 5. worked example: the open box (model)
