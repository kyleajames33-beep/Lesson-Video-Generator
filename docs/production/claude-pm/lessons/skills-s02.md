## Decisions

- **Main question:** *Identify, describe and explain look alike. What does each one ask you to write, and how do you know you've actually explained?* Heading on screen: "Identify, describe, explain".
- **Frequency data used (`research/exam-skills/verb-frequency.md`, Section II written responses in the 10 NESA Chemistry and Biology papers, 2021 to 2025):**
  - **Explain:** 57 units (Chemistry 28, Biology 29). It is the most frequent key word, and more than twice the next (calculate, 24).
  - **Explain by mark value:** 2 marks 13, 3 marks 20, 4 marks 16, 5 marks 5, 7 marks 3. So most are 2 to 4 marks (49 of 57), 3 marks is the commonest, and the range is 2 to 7.
  - **Describe:** 18 (Biology 14). **Outline:** 18 (14 of them at 2 marks). **Identify:** 16 (1 mark 6, 2 marks 7).
  - **Spoken in the hook:** 57; more than twice the next; usually two to four marks. **On screen in scene 10:** the 2 to 7 range. The key notes give 57 and "usually 2 to 4 marks". The counting caveat is a grey tag: "NESA glossary key words only; Section II, 2021 to 2025, counted by question part".
- **Glossary wording:** the pack (`glossary.md`) paraphrases and holds no verbatim copy. So the screen labels each definition "NESA glossary, paraphrased" and uses no quotation marks:
  - identify: recognise and name;
  - describe: give features or characteristics;
  - explain: show a cause, effect or relationship, and how or why it happens;
  - outline: give the main features in general terms.
  - The pack's note that NESA says to read words in the context of the question is spoken in scene 2.
  - The pack's note that questions may use plain prompts such as "why" is used in scene 6 ("it asks why").
- **What markers reward (`what-markers-reward.md`, Explain row):** "Name the relevant feature, then show how it causes the stated outcome." In one three-mark question (Biology 2024 Q24(b)), describing the features reached two marks, and linking them to the outcome reached three. Spoken in scene 10 as "in one recent three-mark explain question". The question is not described, so nothing of the NESA question is reproduced. The guideline reference is in the research findings table only.
- **Faults (`common-faults.md`):**
  - "State an outcome without the causal link": 12/12 reports. Spoken in the hook as "every year, NESA's marking feedback mentions answers that say what happens, but not why".
  - "Describe when asked to explain" is inside the 12/12 nearby-question row. It is shown in the weak answer (scene 8) and in both crossed habits.
- **Why a three-part ladder on one stimulus (Biology, fly reciprocal cross):** HSC papers often set lettered parts on one stimulus with rising verbs. Using one stimulus for identify (1), describe (2) and explain (3) holds the content still. So the only thing that changes between the answers is what the verb demands. That is the skill.

### 2. three-verbs
> Here's what NESA's glossary says, in short. **Identify** means recognise and name. **Describe** means give the features or characteristics. In plain words: what it's like, or what happened. **Explain** means show the cause and effect: how or why it happens. A fourth verb, **outline**, asks for the main features in general terms, a lighter describe. NESA also says to read each verb **in its question**, so let's use one.

### 7. explain-build

Red is dominant, so every daughter has red eyes. Point three. Each point says what causes what.

### 10. because-test

Explain questions run from two marks to seven. More marks means a longer **chain**, not a longer list.

### 12. feedback-1
> Removing the cap lets carbon dioxide gas **escape**, so there's less above the drink. Fewer gas molecules dissolve, so the dissolving rate **falls**, while leaving goes on at the same rate, at first. Leaving is now faster, so carbon dioxide comes **out of solution** as bubbles. ### 15. decision-rule
> So, when you see the verb: **identify**, give the name. **Describe**, give the features, with numbers if there's data. **Outline**, the main features, briefly. **Explain**, show how each cause leads to its effect, step by step, and check every sentence says what causes what. **Next**: compare and contrast, where the trick is writing in **pairs**.

### 16. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, hold about 50 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Identify: recognise and name. Describe: give the features or characteristics. Outline: the main features in general terms (NESA glossary, paraphrased). Tip: identify needs a name per mark, no reasons; describe uses values from any data.
2. Explain: show the cause and the effect, how or why it happens (NESA glossary, paraphrased). Show how each cause leads to its effect; a "because" alone is not enough.
3. Explain is the most common key word in recent HSC Chemistry and Biology written sections (57 question parts, 2021 to 2025), usually for 2 to 4 marks. More marks need a longer chain of links, not a longer list.
4. Test each sentence: does it say what causes what? If it only says what happened, it is a description.

## Questions used and model answers

All questions are written for HSCScience; none is copied from or adapted from a specific NESA question. Mark tallies follow the HSCScience illustrative guide.

**Worked question (Biology, three parts on one stimulus).** Fruit fly eye colour gene on the X chromosome. A white-eyed female x a red-eyed male. Offspring: females 52 red, 0 white; males 0 red, 48 white.
- **(a) Identify which eye-colour allele is dominant. (1 mark)** Model: the red allele.
- **(b) Describe the results of this cross. (2 marks)** Model:
  1. All 52 female offspring had red eyes.
  2. All 48 male offspring had white eyes. Daughters matched their father's phenotype and sons their mother's.
- **(c) Explain why all the male offspring have white eyes but all the female offspring have red eyes. (3 marks)** Model:
  1. The eye-colour gene is on the X chromosome, and the Y chromosome carries no allele for it.
  2. Sons receive their only X from their mother, who is X^r X^r, so every son is X^r Y. With no second allele to mask it, the recessive white allele is expressed.
  3. Daughters receive their father's X, which carries the dominant red allele, so every daughter is X^R X^r and has red eyes.
- **Weak (c) shown:** "All the males had white eyes and all the females had red eyes, because the trait is sex-linked and males have only one X." Illustrative 1 of 3 (for "males have only one X"). It loses 2: it repeats (b), and never says where each X comes from, for sons or daughters.

**Drill 1 (Chemistry, explain, 3 marks).** A sealed bottle of soda water contains CO2(g) ⇌ CO2(aq). When the cap is removed, bubbles form. Explain this observation.
- Model:
  1. Removing the cap lets CO2 gas escape, so the concentration (pressure) of CO2 gas above the liquid decreases.
  2. Fewer gas molecules collide with the surface and dissolve, so the rate of dissolving decreases, while the rate at which dissolved CO2 leaves is unchanged at first.
  3. The reverse reaction is now faster, so CO2 comes out of solution as gas bubbles. (Equivalent: by Le Chatelier's principle, the system shifts left to partially counteract the loss of CO2(g).)
- Crossed habit: "Bubbles form because gas comes out of the drink." (describes the observation).

**Drill 2 (Biology, explain, 2 marks).** DNA replication gives two identical molecules. Assuming no copying errors, explain why.
- Model:
  1. The two strands of the original molecule separate, and each acts as a template for a new strand (semi-conservative replication).
  2. Free nucleotides join by complementary base pairing (A with T, C with G), so each new strand is complementary to its template. Both daughter molecules therefore have the same base sequence as the original, and so as each other.
- Crossed habit: "They are identical because each has one old strand and one new strand." (describes the result; no cause of the matching sequence).
