### 1. hook (weak acid, less heat?)
> Here's a prediction to make. Mix one mole per litre hydrochloric acid with the same amount of sodium hydroxide, and you get about **minus fifty seven** kilojoules per mole of water. ### 3. the weak acid still reacts completely
> So what happens when hydroxide arrives? It grabs the few hydronium ions that are there. ### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Every neutralisation in dilute solution can be counted as ending with H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l), about −57 kJ/mol.
2. A weak acid still reacts completely: OH⁻ removes H₃O⁺, the equilibrium shifts right, and more molecules ionise until all have reacted.
3. By Hess's law, ΔH(neutralisation) = ΔH(ionisation step) + (−57 kJ/mol), so ΔH(ionisation step) = ΔH(measured) − (−57 kJ/mol). The ionisation step = energy in (a bond breaks) + energy out (water surrounds the new ions), so it can be positive, close to zero or negative.

Use Hess's law and a particle model to explain ethanoic acid, hydrofluoric acid and ammonia, then see why neutralisation heat cannot tell you how strong an acid is.
