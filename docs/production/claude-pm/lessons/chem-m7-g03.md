### 2b. q1-choose (which carbon, which alkene)
> Count first. Both alkenes have six carbons, and so does the target, so the chain keeps its length. The target's C double bond O is on carbon **three**. Oxidation turns an O H into a C double bond O on the same carbon, so you need hexan-3-ol. Now look at the double bonds. Hex-3-ene is **symmetrical**: whichever carbon the O H joins, it's carbon three. Hex-1-ene isn't. Water adds to it mainly on carbon **two**, so it gives you mainly the wrong alcohol.

### 2c. q1-route (two steps, two equations)
> So use hex-3-ene. Step one: water with a dilute acid catalyst, heated, gives **hexan-3-ol**. That's an equilibrium, so it gets a double arrow. Step two: acidified potassium dichromate, heated under **reflux**, oxidises that secondary alcohol to hexan-3-one, and the orange turns green. A ketone isn't oxidised any further by dichromate, so the product stays put. For each equation, write the oxidant as O in square brackets, and check that the atoms **balance**.

### 2d. q1-mistake (the classic mistake)
> The classic slip is picking hex-1-ene anyway. Its alcohol is mainly hexan-2-ol, and oxidation leaves the C double bond O where the O H was. So you'd make **hexan-2-one**: the same formula, but a different compound. It's an isomer of the target.

### 3b. q2-backwards (cut the ester, find the alcohol)
> Work backwards. Cut the ester at the single-bonded oxygen. The ethanoate part needs ethanoic acid, and the ethyl part needs **ethanol**. Ethanoic acid comes from oxidising ethanol too, so both halves come from one alcohol. And where does that ethanol come from? No reaction we've met turns ethane straight into an alcohol, so go through a **haloalkane**: chloroethane first.

### 6. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Find the carbon the target's group sits on first. Oxidation and substitution keep the group on the same carbon, so the start must put the OH (or the halogen) there.
2. A symmetrical alkene (hex-3-ene, but-2-ene) gives one alcohol. An unsymmetrical one (hex-1-ene, but-1-ene) gives mainly the OH on carbon 2. For an OH on carbon 1, start from a 1-haloalkane: NaOH(aq), heat under reflux.
3. Example: hex-3-ene → (H₂O, dilute acid catalyst, heat, ⇌) → hexan-3-ol → (K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺, reflux) → hexan-3-one. C₆H₁₄O + [O] → C₆H₁₂O + H₂O.
4. No direct alkane → alcohol: alkane → (Cl₂, UV light; a mixture) → haloalkane → (NaOH(aq), reflux) → alcohol. A starting material used twice is split. Low overall yield: mixtures, an equilibrium, losses at several stages.
5. Check every box as well as every arrow: a wrong alcohol gives an isomer or a ketone dead end; distil gives the aldehyde, excess oxidant with reflux gives the acid.

1. (Q1.) Hexan-3-one, CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₂CH₃, from hex-1-ene or hex-3-ene. (a) Which starting material? (b) Two-step flow chart with reagents, conditions and a balanced equation for each step.
   Model: (a) Hex-3-ene. Hexan-3-one's C=O is on C3, and oxidation forms the C=O on the carbon that held the OH, so hexan-3-ol is needed. Hex-1-ene is unsymmetrical; water adds mainly to C2, giving mainly hexan-2-ol (which would oxidise to hexan-2-one, an isomer of the target).
