### 2. where the water goes
> Start with where the water goes. Water travels up from the roots in **xylem**, the plant's water-carrying tubes. Inside a leaf, water evaporates from the wet walls of the cells into air spaces, so the air in there is very moist. That vapour diffuses out through tiny pores called **stomata**, mostly on the underside of the leaf. This loss of water vapour is **transpiration**. On a hot, dry, windy day the outside air is drier, and wind sweeps away the moist air at the leaf surface, so water vapour leaves faster.

### 3. the trade-off

### 4. guard cells open the pore
> Each stoma sits between two **guard cells**. Their walls are thicker on the side facing the pore. To open it, guard cells take in potassium ions, so the solution inside them gets more concentrated, and water follows by osmosis. The cells swell and become **turgid**, firm with water. Because the thick inner walls stretch less, the swollen cells bow apart, like two long balloons with tape along their inner edges, and the pore opens.

### 5. ABA closes it under stress
> Now the stress response. When the soil dries and the plant loses water faster than it takes it up, cells in the roots and leaves make more of a hormone called abscisic acid, or **ABA**. ABA reaches the guard cells and makes potassium ions leave them. Water follows the ions out by osmosis, the guard cells go limp and straighten, and the pore closes, often within minutes. So guard cells don't pump water. They move ions, and water follows.

### 6. the same loop, in a plant
> That's negative feedback, with the five parts you know. The stimulus is the leaf's water content falling. The receptors are cells in the roots and leaves that detect the shortage. There's no brain, so those same cells act as the control, releasing ABA as the signal. The effectors are the guard cells. The response: stomata close, water loss falls, and the water content recovers. The response opposes the fall. And when water is plentiful again, ABA levels drop and the stomata can reopen.

### 7. the cost of closing
> But closing has a cost. With the stomata shut, much less carbon dioxide gets in, so photosynthesis slows and the plant grows less. In a dry spell, it puts survival ahead of growth. And if the soil stays dry, closing may not be enough. Water still escapes slowly, uptake keeps falling, the cells lose their firmness, and the leaves droop. That's **wilting**.

### 8. xerophytes: built-in savings
> Some plants live where dry is normal. **Xerophytes**, plants adapted to dry habitats, have savings built into their leaves. A thick, waxy **cuticle** is a waterproof coat, so less water evaporates straight through the leaf surface. Their stomata often sit sunken in pits, lined with hairs. ### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Transpiration: water evaporates from cell walls inside the leaf and diffuses out as vapour, mostly through stomata. Hot, dry, windy air speeds it up. The same stomata let in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.
- Guard cells: they move potassium ions and water follows by osmosis. In: turgid, they bow apart (thick inner walls), the stoma opens. Out: flaccid, the stoma closes.
- Water stress: root and leaf cells make ABA → K⁺ leaves guard cells → water follows → stomata close → less water lost (negative feedback). Cost: less CO₂, slower photosynthesis and growth.
- Xerophytes (built in, always working): thick waxy cuticle (waterproof), sunken stomata with hairs and rolled leaves (trap humid air), small leaves (less surface), hanging leaves (cooler). Some store water (succulents) or reach more of it (deep roots).

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (30 Oct) against standard references. The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Pathway of water (scene 2).** Water moves up in xylem, evaporates from mesophyll cell walls into intercellular air spaces and diffuses out as vapour, mainly through stomata; some is lost through the cuticle. Standard plant physiology (Taiz and Zeiger, *Plant Physiology and Development*). Correct.
- **Stomata mostly on the underside (scene 2).** True for most broad-leaved dicots; many grasses and vertical-leaved plants have stomata on both surfaces. Spoken "mostly on the underside of the leaf", matching the site. Codex to confirm the hedge is enough.
- **Hot, dry, windy air (scene 2).** Higher leaf temperature raises the vapour concentration inside; dry air lowers it outside; wind thins the boundary layer. Correct; p02 owns the factors as variables.
- **Guard cell mechanism (scene 4).** Light-activated H⁺-ATPases drive K⁺ (with Cl⁻ and malate) into guard cells; water follows by osmosis; radially arranged cellulose microfibrils and the thicker inner (ventral) wall make the cells bow apart. Year 12 texts say "thicker inner walls"; the site says the same. Spoken "Their walls are thicker on the side facing the pore ... the thick inner walls stretch less". Codex to confirm this is acceptable at Year 12 (the fuller account is microfibril orientation). Grey tag "ions moved in, using energy" is correct for opening.
- **Balloon model (scene 4).** Two long balloons with tape along the inner edges curve apart when inflated, a standard classroom model of guard cell bowing. Where it breaks: guard cells swell by osmosis, not by being blown up, and the stiffness comes from wall thickness and fibre orientation. The narration uses it for shape only. Correct as limited.
- **ABA (scene 5).** ABA rises in roots and leaves under water deficit (leaf vascular tissue is a major site in many species); ABA activates anion and K⁺ efflux channels in guard cells; water leaves by osmosis; closure within minutes. Correct at this level. Spoken "make potassium ions leave them" (not "pump"), so it is accurate for closure.
- **Reopening (scene 6).** ABA is broken down or conjugated after rewatering and stomata reopen, sometimes after a lag. Spoken "ABA levels drop and the stomata can reopen". Correct as hedged.
- **Cost of closing (scene 7).** Closure greatly reduces CO₂ uptake and photosynthesis; cuticular water loss continues at a low rate. Spoken "much less carbon dioxide gets in" and "Water still escapes slowly". Correct.
- **Xerophyte features (scenes 8, 9).** Thick cuticle reduces cuticular transpiration; sunken stomata and trichomes increase boundary layer resistance; *Triodia* (spinifex) leaves are narrow and rolled, with stomata sheltered in protected longitudinal grooves, which the specialist Triodia key places on both upper and lower surfaces (Revision 2, science S2; the narration no longer says "inside"); vertical leaf orientation in many eucalypts reduces midday radiation load and leaf temperature. Spinifex wording settled in Revision 2; Codex to recheck the eucalypt statement wording ("many eucalypts hang their leaves vertically"; adult leaves are typically pendulous).
- **"Many of these plants grow slowly" (scene 10).** A general trend (stress-tolerant plants often have low growth rates); hedged with "one reason" and "many". Codex to flag if misleading.
- **Attempt 1 data.** Invented teaching data: leaf ABA rising about 14-fold over six days without water, transpiration falling from 12 to 2 g per hour. ABA rises of 10-fold or more under drought are widely reported; 12 g per hour is plausible for a pot of wheat plants rather than one plant at midday. Codex to judge whether "a pot of wheat plants" reads more honestly on the card, and whether g per hour is the best unit.
- **Time-sensitive facts spoken.** None.

On a hot, dry, windy afternoon a plant can lose water faster than its roots take it up, and it has no brain, nerves or blood. Learn how water leaves a leaf by transpiration, why the same stomata must let carbon dioxide in, how guard cells use potassium ions and osmosis to open and close each pore, and how the hormone ABA closes stomata when water runs short. ## Review notes for Claude (author, not learner copy)

- **Teaching checks.** Causes: why water leaves a leaf (evaporation inside, diffusion out down a concentration difference; 2); why hot, dry, windy air speeds it (drier air, humid layer removed; 2); why stomata cannot simply stay shut (shared pore; 3); why guard cells open (ions in, water follows, thick inner walls make them bow; 4); why ABA closes them (ions out, water follows; 5); why the response is negative feedback (it opposes the fall; 6); why closing costs growth (less CO₂; 7); why each xerophyte feature saves water (barrier, smaller vapour difference, less surface, cooler leaf; 8, 9). Attention: a plant with no brain still responds within minutes (1); a deal with a price (3). Visual medium: one leaf window that the camera moves through, a matched stoma pair that opens and closes on the spoken cause, a leaf that turns into a xerophyte leaf, and a plant that wilts. Apply: interpret data (11a), explain and compare an unseen plant (11c). Remember: "ions move, water follows" and "waterproof, shrink, keep cool, trap humid air".
