## Decisions

- **Main question:** *In soft water, soap and a detergent both froth. In hard water, soap turns cloudy and barely froths, while the detergent keeps going. How do you set up a fair comparison, measure what you see, and use the results to explain how soaps and detergents work?* Heading on screen: "Soap or detergent: which survives hard water?"
- **Site sources used (site lesson 17):** card 4 (hard water's Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺; scum as calcium or magnesium carboxylate; "soap consumed in scum formation is no longer available for cleaning, more soap required"; anionic detergent heads; the Must Do to name Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, the precipitate and the head difference; the Common Error "Detergents are stronger than soaps"; scenes 6, 8 and A2); card 3 (emulsification, "an emulsion, not a solution"; the Common Error "Soap dissolves the grease"; scene 8); Worked Example 07 (a household in a hard water area with bar soap and sodium lauryl sulfate; the evaluation that soap is comparable in soft water; scene 6 and the detergent choice), with its solubility wording softened as c12's site correction 3 requires. The site has no hands-on procedure; the procedure here is built from the route start and stop and from common school practice (author note a).
- **Why calcium chloride in distilled water (Revision 2):** tap water hardness varies from place to place and contains several ions. A known amount of calcium chloride in distilled water gives a hard-water sample of known composition. It adds chloride as well as calcium (twice as much chloride, and a higher ionic strength), so the narration does not claim calcium is the only difference: calcium is the proposed cause from its chemistry (it forms the scum), and a soap tube in sodium chloride solution with the same chloride is offered as an optional check. Concentration 0.001 mol L⁻¹ (about 100 mg L⁻¹ as CaCO₃, moderately hard; author note b), chosen so soap is fully scummed while the screening estimate keeps calcium dodecyl sulfate below its Ksp. Magnesium sulfate works too and is named in the key notes.
- **Why sodium dodecyl sulfate as the detergent:** a single, known anionic detergent (the site's sodium lauryl sulfate) lets the comparison be about the head group. Many dishwashing and laundry products also contain other ingredients, and some laundry detergents contain water softeners (builders), which would hide the head's effect; scene 10 and A2 use this as a validity point. The narration says "a pure detergent, like sodium dodecyl sulfate". The soap solution is a pure soap (soap flakes or a plain potassium soap, made up by the teacher).
- **Two measures of one action:** froth height is quick and easy to read but only indirectly about cleaning; the oil test (time until a clear oil layer reappears) measures how long the mixture keeps oil dispersed (emulsion stability in a tube), which is closer to the emulsifying action c12 taught but is not a measure of oil removed from fabric. When both point the same way, the conclusion is stronger. Scene 8 says so.
- **Example results (screen, labelled "example data"; author note c):** froth height after 30 s, mean of three: soap, soft 6.0 cm (5.8, 6.2, 6.0); soap, hard 0.6 cm (0.5, 0.8, 0.5) with cloudiness and white flecks; detergent, soft 6.4 cm (6.3, 6.6, 6.3); detergent, hard 6.1 cm (6.0, 6.3, 6.0), clear; water only, soft and hard, under 0.2 cm. Oil test, time until a clear oil layer reappears: water only about 40 s; soap in hard water about 2 minutes; soap in soft water and detergent in both more than 10 minutes (the timing stops at 10 minutes). Revision 2: these values are placeholders for the revised recipe until a pilot sets them; they appear on screen only and are never spoken (the narration describes the pattern qualitatively).
- **Main teaching move:** one cause, two signs. Hard water's calcium ions lock soap into scum, so less soap is left to make froth or coat oil; the detergent's calcium salt is much more soluble, so it keeps working. The investigation's job is to make sure the method does not explain the difference: same volumes, same drops, same shaking, same timing, controls, and a pure detergent.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) the observation to explain (1); (2) the aim; hard water made with a known amount of CaCl₂ (chloride checked by an optional NaCl tube); six tubes including controls (2); (3) part A method: same volume, drops, shakes, time; the controls show water alone barely froths (3); (4) the variables (4); (5) results: soap fails in hard water, detergent barely changes (5); (6) cause: scum locks up soap; detergent's calcium salt stays dissolved (recall, 6); (7) part B: oil emulsion timing (7); (8) the emulsion is soap-coated oil droplets; less free soap in hard water, so the droplets join sooner; two tests agree (8); (9) reliability (9); (10) validity and accuracy (10); (11) hazards and controls (11); memory aid (12); attempts and brief feedback (13 to 16); decision rule and handoff (17); key notes (18).

Include a net ionic equation for one hardness ion (state which ion you use), using sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COONa, as the soap." (3 marks.) Answer: sample Y is hard water: it contains Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ions; the stearate ions react with them to form an insoluble solid, the white scum: 2C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻(aq) + Ca²⁺(aq) → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Ca(s), or 2C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻(aq) + Mg²⁺(aq) → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Mg(s) (either earns the mark); the first drops (about 15 more than distilled water) are used up making scum, so lasting froth forms only once enough hardness ions have been removed for free soap to remain. > Shake a little soap solution in pure water, and you get a tall **froth**. Shake the same soap in hard water, and it turns cloudy, and the froth barely forms. A detergent froths in **both**. Let's show that fairly, measure it, and explain it.

### 2. the aim, and the tubes

### 3. part A: the froth test
> Part one is a froth test. Put ten millilitres of each water into matching test tubes, and add five drops of soap or detergent solution. Stopper it, shake it ten times the same way, and stand it in the rack. After thirty seconds, measure the froth **height** with a ruler, from the liquid's surface to the top of the froth. The controls show how much froth water alone makes: almost **none**.

Visual: method card in the right zone, one step per cue, earlier steps dimming: "1 10 mL water in each tube (same size tubes)"; "2 5 drops of solution (soap or detergent)" (coded dropper outlines, no hands); "3 stopper; shake 10 times, same way" (the c12 shake runs on all six tubes together); "4 stand 30 s; measure froth height". On "height" the froth ruler slides beside the soap-soft plate, zero at the liquid surface (tag "froth height: liquid surface to top of froth"). On "none" the two control slots show a thin coded line of bubbles only, chips "under 0.2 cm". Bright at the end: the ruler, step 4 and the control chips. Hold about 1 second.

### 4. the variables
> What changes, and what stays the same? Two independent variables: the cleaning agent, soap or detergent, and the water, soft or hard. The dependent variable is the froth height, plus whether a solid **forms**. Keep the water volume, the drops, the solution strengths, the shaking and the waiting time the **same**. Then any difference belongs to the agent or the water.
