**Boundary:** this video starts by identifying the OH group and classifying the carbon that holds it, and stops at explaining the boiling point and solubility trends of alcohols. It teaches: an alcohol has an OH (hydroxyl) group on a carbon with only single bonds; the OH is covalently bonded, not a hydroxide ion (spoken), so alcohols do not make water alkaline (screen line only); intramolecular bonding: the O–H bond (and the C–O bond) is polar because oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly, so O is δ− and H is δ+, and the oxygen keeps two lone pairs; naming alcohols by IUPAC rules (longest chain containing the C–OH carbon; drop the alkane's final "e" and add "ol"; number from the end nearer the OH; locant before "ol"; methanol and ethanol need no locant; branches as in c01; the OH wins the numbering over a branch); classification by counting the carbons bonded to the C–OH carbon (1 primary, 2 secondary, 3 tertiary; methanol counted as primary by convention; branching elsewhere does not change the class); hydrogen bonding between alcohol molecules (δ+ H of one OH to a lone pair on a neighbour's O; H donor, O acceptor), alongside dispersion forces from the chain; between series: an alcohol boils far higher than an alkane with the same number of electrons (propane and ethanol, 26 electrons each), and boiling overcomes the intermolecular forces without breaking the O–H bond; within the series: one OH each, so similar hydrogen bonding, and boiling point rises with chain length (methanol to butan-1-ol); isomers of C₄H₁₀O: boiling point usually falls from primary to secondary to tertiary because the more compact shape gives less contact and weaker dispersion forces, while all three hydrogen bond; solubility: the OH hydrogen bonds with water, replacing the bonds it breaks, so methanol to propan-1-ol mix in any proportion; the non-polar chain breaks water's hydrogen bonds without replacing them, so solubility falls gradually with chain length (butan-1-ol partly, hexan-1-ol barely, as a layer); long-chain alcohols mix with non-polar solvents. **Not here:** alcohol reactions (combustion chem-m7-c07; dehydration, substitution with HX and oxidation chem-m7-c08; the class is used there, so one forward clause only); producing alcohols (chem-m7-c06, the next video); enthalpy of combustion (chem-m7-c07, p03, g02; the site's L11 figures are not used); methanol's toxicity (site hook; it is metabolic, not an intermolecular-force property, see Decisions); diols and alcohols with a C=C (not needed up to this route stop; author note e); branching and water solubility (2-methylpropan-2-ol; author note d; key notes leave it out); comparison with carboxylic acids, aldehydes and ketones (chem-m7-c08b, c09); ### 2. the hydroxyl group and its polar bond
> On paper, take ethane and swap one hydrogen for an O H group. That gives ethanol, an **alcohol**. This O H is a hydroxyl group, on a carbon with only single bonds. It's covalently bonded, so it isn't a hydroxide ion. Inside the molecule, oxygen pulls the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does. So the O H bond is **polar**: the oxygen is slightly negative, the hydrogen slightly positive. And the oxygen keeps two **lone pairs**, electrons not used in bonding.

### 3. naming alcohols
> Naming uses the rules you already know, with one change. Find the longest chain that holds the carbon with the O H. Take the alkane's name, drop its final e, and add **ol**. Number from the end nearer the O H, and put that number before ol. So C H three, C H two, C H two O H is **propan-one-ol**. Move the O H to the middle carbon, and it's **propan-two-ol**. Methanol and ethanol need no number, because there's only one place the O H can go.

### 7. hydrogen bonds between alcohol molecules

### 9. along the series: the chain sets the trend

Each extra C H two adds electrons and contact, so the dispersion forces grow, and the boiling point **climbs** steadily.

### 19. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Alcohol: an OH (hydroxyl) group on a carbon with only single bonds; covalent, not OH⁻. The O–H bond is polar (O δ−, H δ+), and O has two lone pairs. Name: longest chain holding the C–OH carbon; alkane name minus "e" + "ol"; number from the end nearer the OH (the OH beats branches): propan-2-ol, 4-methylpentan-2-ol.

3. Boiling point: hydrogen bonds (δ+ H to a lone pair on a neighbour's O) plus dispersion forces. Same electrons: propane −42 °C, ethanol 78 °C. 4. Solubility in water: the OH hydrogen bonds with water, so C1 to C3 alcohols mix in any proportion. Longer non-polar chains break water's hydrogen bonds without replacing them, so solubility falls gradually (butan-1-ol partly, hexan-1-ol barely). Long chains mix with non-polar solvents.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the O–H bond is polar: oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly (2). Why the OH is not a base: it is covalently bonded, not a hydroxide ion (2). Why the OH wins the numbering: it is named in the ending (4). Why the class needs the C–OH carbon, not the look of the molecule (5, 6). Why alcohols hydrogen bond: δ+ H and a lone pair on a neighbour's O (7). Why ethanol boils far above propane: equal electrons give similar dispersion forces, so the difference is hydrogen bonding (8). Why boiling does not break the O–H bond: boiling separates molecules (8). Why boiling point rises along the series: constant OH, longer chain, more dispersion (9). Why tertiary isomers usually boil lowest: compact shape, less contact (10). Why small alcohols mix with water: the OH replaces the hydrogen bonds it breaks (11). Why longer chains dissolve less: the non-polar chain breaks bonds it cannot replace (12). Why long chains mix with non-polar solvents: dispersion forces on both sides (12, silent screen chip and key note 4; c03 callback).
