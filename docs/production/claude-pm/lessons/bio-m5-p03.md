## Decisions

- **Site sources used:** lesson 16 frequency card ("60 of 100 sampled individuals ... 60%"; describes a group, not every individual; cautious language "more common", "in this sample", never "proves" or "always"); the earlobe characteristic (attached and free); the "Sample quality and limitations" card (large sample, clear recording method, consistent definitions; limitations: small sample size, single location, observer bias or classification errors, one-generation snapshot); the trap "a difference in a data table does not automatically mean a biologically meaningful difference for the whole species"; the data interpretation sequence (state the trend, compare with values, state a limitation, keep the conclusion proportional); the "Read the Table" task (group A 18 attached and 42 free; group B 30 and 30), used unchanged as the attempt; the Analyse walkthrough's "frequency data is strong evidence about distribution and weak evidence about cause".
- **Site items not used (recorded here only):** (1) The Tuskegee hook. It carries two site errors flagged in the extract (Belmont Report origin; "autonomy" is not a Belmont principle), and a medical-ethics case is out of proportion to a classroom tally. The ethical point is kept as practice: consent, anonymity, the right to say no (scene 3). (2) The site's population A and B earlobe percentages (24 and 76; 41 and 59) are unsourced; not used. (3) The site's phrasing implies earlobe attachment is a simple inherited trait; it is not (see author note a), so the video counts the characteristic and makes no allele claim.
- **Characteristic choice:** earlobe shape, the site's example; tongue rolling named as an alternative on screen. Both are non-identifying, visible, quick to classify and not sensitive. Neither is a simple one-gene trait (author note a), so the video says so in one sentence and keeps allele frequencies with B11's roan cattle.
- **No faces or ears drawn.** The definition card is a **coded line diagram**: two simple outline curves of a lower ear edge meeting a vertical line ("side of the head"), one with a rounded lobe hanging below the join (free) and one meeting the line directly (attached), plus an in-between curve. This is an abstract diagram (owner rules: avoid drawing people; code only abstract diagrams), not a painted ear. Doubt flagged for the owner (doubt 1).
- **Main teaching move:** one class collection, done well, then scaled up. Choose a safe characteristic; get agreement and keep it anonymous; define categories (with "unsure") before counting; self-classify on unnamed slips; two counters; record a tally table and frequencies with a stated denominator; pool four classes; present a column graph; show with a bead bag why a bigger sample is a better estimate; analyse trend, pattern and relationship; name limitations, validity and reliability; then the site's two-group task.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) one class's count is not yet a population frequency: method, sample size and classification decide what it means (1); (2) the characteristic must be visible, quick, non-sensitive, and is not a one-gene trait, so it is counted, not turned into alleles (2); (3) data about people need agreement and anonymity, so no result can be traced and no one feels pressured (3); (4) definitions before counting, with an "unsure" box, because different judgements make the numbers mean different things (4); (5) unnamed slips and two counters reduce bias and counting errors (5); (6) frequency is count over total, and the total must be stated (6); (7) pooling gives a bigger sample and a better estimate (7); (8) separate categories go on a column graph with gaps, from zero, titled and labelled (8); (9) small samples scatter by chance; bigger samples land closer to the true value (9); (10) trend, pattern and relationship, with association not cause (10); (11) limitations, validity and reliability, and cautious wording (11).
- **Entry knowledge:** B11 (frequency = count over stated total, table and bar chart; allele frequency by copies; recessive phenotype frequency; SNPs; association, not cause; sample limits: size, representation, consistent definitions). Year 11 Working Scientifically: validity, reliability, graph conventions.
- **Learner-visible vocabulary (plain meaning at first use):** characteristic (a feature you can observe); sample (the people you actually counted); population (the larger group you want to describe); denominator is not spoken (said as "which total"); consent is said as "agreement"; validity (does it measure what it claims?), reliability (do repeats agree?), both recalled from P01 chemistry and Year 11 with a plain meaning.
- **Example data (on screen, labelled "example data"):** Class 1: 30 slips; attached 9, free 19, unsure 2; frequencies of the 28 classified: attached 32 percent (9/28 = 32.1), free 68 percent (19/28 = 67.9). Four classes, classified totals: class 1 9/28 (32 percent), class 2 6/27 (22 percent), class 3 8/29 (28 percent), class 4 7/26 (27 percent); pooled 30/110 = 27 percent (27.3). Bead bag: 200 beads, 50 red (25 percent). Samples of 10: 1, 4, 2, 3 red (10, 40, 20, 30 percent). Samples of 50: 11, 14, 13 red (22, 28, 26 percent).
- **Attempt system (site task, unchanged):** group A 18 attached, 42 free; group B 30 attached, 30 free. Demands: frequency of attached in each group, compare, one limitation. Answer: A 18/60 = 30 percent; B 30/60 = 50 percent; attached more common in group B in these samples; limitation: 60 per group from one school is a small, possibly unrepresentative sample, so the difference could be chance. Crossed mistake: "18 / 42 = 43 percent" (divided by the other category, not the total).
- **Mistake scene:** none separate. Misconceptions blocked: a class frequency describes everyone (1, 11); a characteristic count gives allele frequencies (2); counting without definitions (4); a percentage without a stated total (6); a table difference proves a population difference (7, 9, 11, attempt); association proves cause (10); line graphs for categories (8).
- **Reuse:** B11's coded frequency table and bar chart look, its "in this sample" tag style, its pool bar; B3b's coded gene-pool tray bead look for the bead population (round beads, painted bead plates below); the explainer kit (`Plate`, `Callout`, `Mark`, `Countdown`, `HandText`, `DrawPath`, `scribbleEllipse`, `Enter`); B6a's `WrongChip`, `GreyTag`, `Cross`, `Cam`. Shared plates from bio-m5-p01: `m5x-clipboard-sheet-v1.png` (tally sheet), `m5x-pencil-v1.png`. New coded items: the definition line diagram, the slip (three boxes), tally table, frequency calculation lines, column graph, bead sample chart (dot strip), analysis card (trend, pattern, relationship), limitations card, attempt card.
- **No human figures:** slips fold and drop into the box by themselves; the mirror lies on a desk and shows only a soft highlight; counters are represented by two pencil tallies agreeing on the clipboard. No people.
- **Safety:** none physical. The ethical rules (agreement, anonymity, the right to say no, teacher approval) are scene 3.
- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Earlobe attachment is not a simple dominant-recessive trait: earlobes vary continuously from attached to free, and genome-wide studies (Shaffer et al. 2017, Am J Hum Genet 101:913: 6 loci in expert-scored cohorts, at least 49 with self-report data) show many genes contribute. Tongue rolling is not simple Mendelian either (twin studies show identical twins can differ; McDonald, Myths of Human Genetics). The video says "neither is a simple one-gene trait". Science check to confirm. (b) Self-classification with a mirror trades observer consistency for privacy; the "unsure" box and two counters partly compensate; a stronger method (one trained classifier using photographs) is not used, because photographs are identifying. (c) Excluding "unsure" from the total is a choice; the video says to state it. Including them gives 9/30 = 30 percent. (d) The bead simulation samples 10 or 50 beads, then returns them and shakes the bag (sampling with replacement between samples). Expected spread for n = 10 at p = 0.25: standard deviation about 14 percentage points; for n = 50 about 6. The example values fit. (e) Column graph: categorical data; gaps between columns; y-axis from zero. A pie chart is acceptable for one group's parts of a whole but poor for comparing groups (screen tag only). (f) "How common a characteristic is can differ between populations" is the site's point (sickle cell allele and malaria regions, B11 territory); the video names no ancestry groups.

Main question: *How do you collect, record and present data on how common a characteristic is, and how sure can you be about what it shows?*
Entry knowledge: B11 as above; Year 11 graphing, validity and reliability.
Stops after: choosing a visible, non-sensitive, non-identifying characteristic and saying why it is counted, not converted to alleles; stating the agreement and anonymity rules; writing definitions with an "unsure" category before counting; describing an anonymous slip method with two counters; recording a tally table and calculating frequencies with a stated total; pooling classes and saying why the pooled value is a better estimate; drawing a column graph with correct conventions; explaining with a bead model why small samples scatter; identifying a trend, a pattern and a relationship (association, not cause); naming limitations and improvements to validity and reliability; using cautious language; applying all of it to the site's two-group earlobe task. SNP data analysis stays with B11.
Handoff: DNA sequencing and profiling for inheritance studies (route entry bio-m5-b12).

## Script and shots

Cue words in **bold** trigger the visual change. Exact frames are set from the recording's word timings. Board layout (three zones, constant): top-left the short question and the method step rail; the centre is the desk (a pale strip) for slips, box, mirror, tally sheet and bead bag; the right third holds the current card (definitions, table, graph, analysis, limitations), one at a time. Colour key: attached amber outline, free teal outline, unsure grey (letters and words always printed, never colour only). Every example number carries a small grey tag "example data".

### 1. hook (one class, one number)
> In one class, nine of the twenty-eight students who were counted have attached earlobes. Does that tell you how common attached earlobes are in everyone your age? **Not yet**. It depends on how the data were collected, how big the sample was, and how carefully each earlobe was judged. In this practical you'll collect, record and present frequency data, and learn to say exactly what it **does and doesn't show**.

Visual: the desk strip draws. A coded tally sheet on the painted clipboard (`m5x-clipboard-sheet-v1.png`) shows "attached: 9 of 28". On "everyone your age" the number zooms out to a wide coded field of small grey squares (abstract population, no people) with a "?" over it. On "Not yet" three chips write on under the field, one per cue: "how collected?", "how big?", "how judged?". On "does and doesn't show" the question writes on at the top left: "How common is it, and how sure are you?" Hold about 2 seconds.

### 2. aim and choosing a characteristic
> The aim: collect data on how common a characteristic is in a population, present it clearly, and identify trends, patterns and limitations. Choose a characteristic that's **easy to see**, quick to judge, and **not sensitive**, like earlobe shape or tongue rolling. Neither is a simple **one-gene trait**, so you're counting how common the characteristic is, not working out allele frequencies.

Visual: the aim card writes on in the right zone. On "easy to see" a checklist writes beside it: "visible", "quick to judge", "not sensitive", "can't identify anyone", each with a teal tick. Two candidate chips drop in: "earlobe shape" and "tongue rolling" (words only, no pictures). A crossed chip "health conditions" fades in (tag "sensitive: not for a class survey"). On "one-gene trait" a small grey tag writes: "many genes involved: count the characteristic; allele frequencies, see the roan cattle (B11)". Hold about 1 second.

### 3. agreement and privacy
> Data about people needs their **agreement**. Get your teacher to approve the plan, tell everyone what's being collected and why, and let anyone **say no** without giving a reason. Record **no names**, just one tick on an unnamed slip. That way no result can be traced back to a person, and nobody feels pressured to take part.

Visual: step rail lights "1. Plan and agree". A coded plan card writes on: "teacher approval", "say what and why", "anyone can say no". On "say no" one blank slip stays on the desk, unticked, with the tag "no reason needed". On "no names" a painted blank slip (from `m5x-slip-box-v1.png`, item 2) enlarges with a coded "name: ___" line that takes a grey cross, leaving three tick boxes. Tag: "anonymous: can't be traced". Hold about 1 second.

### 4. define the categories before counting
> Next, write the definitions before anyone counts. A **free** earlobe hangs below the point where it joins the side of the head. An **attached** earlobe joins the head directly, with no hanging part. Some are in between, so add a third box: **unsure**. Clear definitions matter, because if two people judge the same earlobe differently, the numbers mean different things. So **test the definitions** first on a few practice diagrams, and if people disagree, sharpen the wording.

Visual: step rail "2. Define". The definition card writes in the right zone as a coded line diagram (no face, no head shape): in each of three panels, a vertical line tagged "side of head" and, beside it, a single ink outline of the lower half of an ear rim curving down into the lobe, with a pointer "earlobe" and a small dot "join point" where the lobe meets the line. On "free" the first panel draws: the lobe rounds and hangs below the dot, the hanging part shaded teal and tagged "hangs below the join"; on "attached" the second: the rim runs straight into the line at the dot, amber outline, tagged "joins directly"; on "unsure" the third: a short lobe barely below the dot, grey, tagged "in between". On "judge the same earlobe differently" two coded tick marks land in different boxes for the same grey curve, with a pen "?" between them. On "test the definitions" three small practice outline curves appear under the card with two coded tick rows beneath; two ticks agree, one pair disagrees and the definition line "hangs below the join" gains an underline (tag "sharpen the wording"). Hold about 1 second.

### 5. the method
> Now the method. Give each student a slip with three boxes, and show the definition card. Each student checks their earlobe in a **mirror**, or asks a partner to look, then ticks one box, folds the slip and drops it in a box. Then **two people count** the slips together onto a tally sheet, and check that their totals agree. Two counters catch mistakes that one person would miss.

Visual: step rail "3. Collect". The painted slip box (`m5x-slip-box-v1.png`, item 2) sits centre-left; the hand mirror (`m5x-hand-mirror-v1.png`, item 1) lies on the desk with only a soft highlight in its glass. On "or asks a partner to look" a small grey tag writes beside the mirror: "earlobes are visible anyway; the slip stays unnamed". On "ticks one box" a coded tick lands in a slip's box; the slip folds and drops into the box slot (no hand); several more follow. On "two people count" the slips tip out in a neat pile and two coded tally columns build side by side on the clipboard, one per counter, in two pencil colours (`m5x-pencil-v1.png` small); on "totals agree" both columns end "30" with a teal tick between them. Hold about 1 second.

### 6. record: the table and the frequency
> Record the counts in a table: category, tally, count and frequency. Our example class returned **thirty** slips: nine attached, nineteen free and two unsure. Frequency is the count divided by the total, times one hundred. But **which total**? Leave out the unsure slips, since they fit neither box, and say so in your table. Nine out of twenty-eight is **thirty-two per cent** attached, and nineteen out of twenty-eight is sixty-eight per cent free.

Visual: step rail "4. Record". The table writes in the right zone (B11 table look): columns "category / tally / count / frequency (%)"; rows "attached / IIII IIII / 9", "free / IIII IIII IIII IIII / 19", "unsure / II / 2", "total slips / 30". On "count divided by the total" the formula line writes: "frequency = count / total x 100". On "which total" the 30 and a new "classified: 28" ring with a "?"; on "Leave out the unsure slips" the unsure row greys and a table footnote writes: "frequency of the 28 classified (2 unsure left out)". On "thirty-two per cent" the working "9 / 28 x 100 = 32%" writes in the attached row; then "19 / 28 x 100 = 68%" in the free row; a small check "32 + 68 = 100". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 7. pool the year group
> One class is a small sample, so **pool** the results from four classes. Their attached frequencies were thirty-two, twenty-two, twenty-eight and twenty-seven per cent. Altogether, that's thirty attached out of a hundred and ten students, about **twenty-seven per cent**. The class values jump around, but the pooled value rests on four times as many people, so it's a **better estimate**.

Visual: the table shrinks to a strip; a four-row class table writes: "class 1: 9 / 28 = 32%", "class 2: 6 / 27 = 22%", "class 3: 8 / 29 = 28%", "class 4: 7 / 26 = 27%", then a bold total row "pooled: 30 / 110 = 27%". On "pool" the four tally sheets slide together into one. On "jump around" the four percentages pulse in turn; on "better estimate" the pooled row rings teal with the tag "bigger sample". Grey tag: "add the counts, not the percentages". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 8. present: a column graph
> To present it, use a **column graph**, because the classes are separate groups, not points on a continuous scale. Put the four classes and the pooled total along the bottom, and the frequency of attached earlobes, in per cent, up the side, **starting from zero**. Leave gaps between the columns, give the graph a title, and label both axes, with units on the frequency axis.

Visual: step rail "5. Present". A small tag on "column graph": "column graph (a bar chart with vertical bars)". A coded column graph builds in the right zone: x-axis "group" with "class 1, class 2, class 3, class 4, pooled"; y-axis "frequency of attached earlobes (%)" from 0 to 40 in steps of 10; columns rise to 32, 22, 28, 27 and 27 (the pooled column in a darker fill); gaps between columns. On "starting from zero" the y-axis zero tick rings; a small inset shows the same columns on an axis starting at 20 with the differences looking huge, grey cross, tag "axis must start at zero". Title writes: "Frequency of attached earlobes in four Year 12 classes (example data)". A crossed chip "line graph" (tag "categories, not a continuous scale"). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 9. why a bigger sample helps: a bead model
> You saw earlier that small samples bounce around. Now **test it yourself** with beads. Fill a bag with two hundred beads, a quarter of them red, so you know the true frequency. Without looking, draw ten, count the red ones, put them back and shake. Ten-bead samples gave ten, forty, twenty and thirty per cent red. Fifty-bead samples gave twenty-two, twenty-eight and twenty-six. **Small samples scatter** widely by chance. Bigger samples land closer to the truth.

Visual: a small grey tag "B11: small samples bounce" writes at the top of the right zone on "You saw earlier". The painted bead bag (`m5x-bead-bag-v1.png`, item 3) sits centre with a coded tag "200 beads: 50 red = 25%". On "draw ten" ten bead plates (`m5x-bead-red-v1.png`, `m5x-bead-white-v1.png`, items 4 and 5) tumble out into a row (one red); they slide back in and the bag gives a small shake. A dot-strip chart builds in the right zone: a horizontal axis 0 to 50 percent with a dashed line at 25 tagged "true value". On "Ten-bead samples" four amber dots land at 10, 40, 20 and 30 (row "samples of 10"); on "Fifty-bead samples" three teal dots land at 22, 28 and 26 (row "samples of 50"). On "Small samples scatter" a bracket spans the amber dots (tag "wide spread") and a narrow bracket spans the teal dots (tag "close to 25%"). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 10a. analyse: trend and pattern
> Now analyse. A **trend** is the overall direction in the data: free earlobes were more common in every class. A **pattern** is a feature that repeats: every class sat between twenty-two and thirty-two per cent attached.

Visual: step rail "6. Analyse". The analysis card writes in the right zone, one row per cue, each pen-linked to the graph: "Trend (overall direction): free more common in every class" (the four columns' free complements show above each attached column as pale outlines); "Pattern (a repeated feature): every class 22% to 32% attached" (a bracket across the column tops). Hold about 1 second.

### 10b. analyse: a relationship
> A **relationship** is when two characteristics change together, so each slip needs both, like earlobes and tongue rolling. Say twenty of the thirty students with attached earlobes could roll their tongue, sixty-seven per cent, and fifty-four of the eighty with free earlobes could, sixty-eight per cent. Almost the same, so this sample shows **no relationship**. And even if they had gone together, that would be an association, not proof that one causes the other.

Visual: the analysis card stays; its third row writes: "Relationship (two characteristics change together): needs both on each slip" (a coded slip with two rows of boxes, "earlobes" and "tongue rolling"). On "Say twenty" a 2 x 2 count grid fills: rows "attached (30)", "free (80)", columns "can roll", "can't roll": 20, 10 / 54, 26 (tag "example data"); on "sixty-seven" and "sixty-eight" two short bars draw beside the grid, "attached: 20 / 30 = 67%" and "free: 54 / 80 = 68%", nearly level; on "no relationship" a bracket joins the two bar tops with the tag "about the same: no relationship in this sample". On "association, not proof" a tag writes: "association, not cause (B11)". A sentence-starter strip writes along the bottom: "In this sample, ... was more common than ..." Hold about 1 second.

### 11. limitations, validity and reliability
> Then the limits. A hundred and ten students from **one school** is still a small sample, and one school may not represent other places, because how common a characteristic is can differ between populations. People judge their own earlobes differently, and in-between ones are hard to call. Clear definitions make the data more **valid**, because then it measures what it claims to. Two counters, pooling and recounts make it more **reliable**, so repeats agree. And always write "in this sample", not "in all people".

Visual: the limitations card writes, three columns "Limitation / Why it matters / Improvement", one row per cue: "one school, 110 students / may not represent other places / sample several schools"; "self-judging, in-between lobes / classification errors / clear definition card, unsure box, practise on the diagrams first"; then two summary chips: "valid: measures what it claims (clear definitions, unsure box)" and "reliable: repeats agree (two counters, pool classes, recount)". On "in this sample" a pair of chips: "in this sample" (teal tick) and "in all people" (grey cross). Hold about 2 seconds (reading hold).

Calculate the frequency of attached earlobes in each group, compare the groups, and give one limitation of the data. Pause here to answer.

Visual: the board clears: no tables, graphs or cards from earlier. The attempt card writes on: a two-row table "group / attached / free": "A / 18 / 42", "B / 30 / 30". Demands: "(a) Frequency of attached earlobes in each group." "(b) Compare the groups." "(c) One limitation of the data." Three answer boxes. Nothing answer-bearing: no totals column, no percentages, no formula. **The countdown ring starts after "Pause here to answer." and runs for 10 seconds.** The card stays on screen during and after the countdown.

### 13. feedback (brief)
> Each group has sixty people. Group A: eighteen out of sixty is **thirty per cent**. Group B: thirty out of sixty is **fifty per cent**. So attached earlobes were more common in group B, in these samples. One limitation: sixty people from one school is a small sample, so the difference could be chance. If you divided eighteen by forty-two, you used the wrong total.

Visual: the card shrinks to a strip. On "sixty people" a totals column writes on: "60", "60". On "thirty per cent" the working "18 / 60 x 100 = 30%" writes in row A; on "fifty per cent" "30 / 60 x 100 = 50%" in row B. Answer lines: "(b) higher in group B (50% vs 30%) in these samples"; "(c) small sample from one school; difference could be chance". On "eighteen by forty-two" the chip "18 / 42 = 43%" writes on and takes a grey scribble cross, tag "divide by the whole group". Hold about 1 second.

### 14. decision rule and handoff
> So, to collect frequency data well: get agreement and keep it anonymous, define the categories first, count with two people, divide by the right total and say which one, graph it clearly, and name the limits. **Next**: how DNA sequencing and profiling show inheritance directly in the DNA.

Visual: the plan card writes, one line per cue: "Agree; anonymous." "Define first (with unsure)." "Two counters." "Frequency = count / stated total." "Column graph from zero." "Name the limits; 'in this sample'." The column graph returns small along the bottom. On "Next" a chip "Next: DNA sequencing and profiling" appears with a small coded base-tile row. Hold about 1 second.

### 15. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Choose a visible, quick, non-sensitive characteristic that cannot identify anyone (earlobe shape, tongue rolling). Neither is a one-gene trait: count the characteristic, not alleles.
2. Ethics: teacher approval; say what and why; anyone can say no; no names (anonymous slips).
3. Method: definitions before counting, with an "unsure" box; self-check with a mirror; fold slips into a box; two people count and check totals agree.
4. Frequency = count / total x 100; state the total (for example, "of the 28 classified"). Pool classes by adding counts. Present categories on a column graph: gaps, y-axis from zero, title, labelled axes with units.
5. Small samples scatter by chance; bigger samples give better estimates. Analyse: trend (overall direction), pattern (a repeated feature), relationship (two characteristics change together; association, not cause). Limits: one school, small sample, classification errors. Valid: clear definitions. Reliable: two counters, pool, repeat. Write "in this sample".

Memory aid (on the notes screen): *Agree, define, count twice, state the total.* Condition tag: "a frequency describes the sample, not every person".
