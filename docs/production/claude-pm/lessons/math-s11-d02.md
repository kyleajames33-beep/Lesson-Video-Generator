### 1. hook: who sees the answers?
> Our survey now asks your name, and where your phone charges overnight. Picture answering honestly. You were scrolling in bed until one in the morning, and your name is right there on the answer. Would you tick the truth? Some people wouldn't, and that changes the data. Collecting data about people comes with responsibilities, to the people and to the results. So what privacy, bias and ethics questions come with a survey like **this**?

### 2. privacy: collect only what you need
> Start with privacy, people's right to control who sees information about them. The first rule is simple. Only collect what you actually need. Our question is about sleep, so we don't need anyone's name. Drop it, and the survey becomes anonymous, meaning no one, not even the person running it, can tell whose answer is whose. But be careful. Leaving out names isn't always enough. Say the survey also asks if you're in the band, and if you board at the school. In the band leaves twenty-four students. A boarder in the band, just three. Add that one of them slept four hours, and everyone knows who it **is**. So report results for groups, never for one person. And if you do need names, say to follow someone up, keep them separate and private. That's called confidential.

### 3. ethics: consent and honest reporting
> Next, ethics, which means treating the people who give you data fairly and honestly. People are doing you a favour, so they need consent, agreeing to take part, knowing what it's for. Tell them what the survey is for, who will see the answers, and that taking part is their choice. They can skip any question, or stop. At school, students under eighteen also need the school's permission, and often a parent's. Don't ask anything that could embarrass or hurt someone for no good reason. And when the results come in, report all of them, including the ones you didn't expect. Reporting only the answers that suit your story is just as unfair as asking an unfair **question**.

### 4. bias: questions that push
> Bias is a lean in the method that pushes results one way. Some of it comes from who you ask, and that's coming up. But a lot comes from how you ask. Here's a leading question, one that hints at the answer it wants. Don't you agree that phones in bedrooms ruin sleep? Ask our twenty students that, and sixteen might agree, just to be agreeable. Now ask it neutrally. Where does your phone charge overnight? Twelve say their bedroom, and eight say somewhere else. That's data we can actually **use**. Missing options lean results too. If the only choices are bedroom and kitchen, where does everyone else go? And remember the name field. People who feel judged give the answer that sounds good. So privacy isn't just polite. Feeling safe is what makes people **honest**.

### 5. responding to diverse groups
> A survey also has to work for everyone in the target population, not just students like the person who wrote it. Our phone question assumes every student has their own phone and their own bedroom. Some share a room, and some don't have a phone. So add options like I share a room, I don't have a phone, and prefer not to say. Families also have different routines. Some students care for younger siblings, work late shifts, or keep cultural or religious practices that change when they sleep. The survey shouldn't treat any of that as wrong. Some students read English as a second language, or use a screen reader. Plain English, a translation, or a paper copy lets them answer too. And before it goes out, try the survey with a few students from different groups, and fix whatever confuses them. Leave a group out, and the results describe fewer people than you **claimed**.

### 6. putting it together: a survey audit
> Let's audit the whole survey. Do we need every question? The name's gone. Is it anonymous, or at least confidential, and reported in groups? Yes. Does everyone know what it's for, and that they can skip or stop? Yes. Is every question neutral, with an option for every answer? Yes. Does it work for every group in the target population? With the new options, the translation and the paper copy, it does. Five ticks. Now the answers we collect have a fair chance of being true, and of describing the people we said they **would**.
