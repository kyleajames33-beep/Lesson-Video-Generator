- **Site errors found (this read).** Root to add to `../site-corrections-bio-y11.md`.
  1. L12 hook: "a freshwater perch ... a saltwater snapper in the same family". Perches (for example Percidae, or Australia's Percichthyidae) and snappers (Sparidae or Lutjanidae) are different families. Proposed: "a saltwater snapper, another bony fish".
  2. L12 "Freshwater vs Saltwater Solutions", the diagram, the summary and SA Q1 model answer: saltwater fish "produce small volumes of concentrated urine". Marine bony fish cannot make urine more concentrated than their blood (they have no loop of Henle); their urine is small in volume and about as concentrated as their body fluids, carrying mainly magnesium and sulfate ions, while gills remove the sodium chloride. Proposed: "produce small volumes of urine (no more concentrated than their blood)". The same correction applies to the barramundi paragraph and SA Q2 ("small volumes of concentrated urine").
  3. L12 "The Osmotic Problem": "A saltwater (marine) animal has body fluids less salty (hypotonic) than seawater, so water constantly moves out and salts move in." True for marine bony fish, not for sharks and rays (body fluids about as concentrated as seawater, using retained urea) or most marine invertebrates (osmoconformers). Proposed: "A marine bony fish has body fluids ...". The route's v15 stop boundary asks for this.
  4. L12 "Why this matters": "only species able to switch their osmoregulation (like the salmon) can thrive there". Salmon are not native to Australia and the page has already used the barramundi. Proposed: "(like the barramundi)". Minor.
  5. L12 SA Q3 model answer: "the rising internal solute concentration would denature enzymes". Rising ion concentration disrupts enzyme activity; "denature" overstates the usual effect. Proposed: "would disrupt enzyme activity". Minor; science check to judge.

### 9. not every aquatic animal
> One warning: this is the bony fish story. Other sea animals solve the salt problem differently. ### 10. switching: barramundi
> Some fish can switch. Barramundi hatch in salty coastal water, many swim up rivers to grow, and they return to the sea to spawn. Each move means reversing the gill pumps and switching between lots of dilute urine and only a little urine. That takes days. So a fish moved suddenly between fresh and salt water can die, even though it would survive after a slow change. And it's why most bony fish live in only one kind of water: the two sets of fixes run in opposite directions, so they can't both run at once.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (3 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Gills (2).** Large, thin surface for gas exchange; water and ions cross; a fish cannot seal its gills and still breathe (site b1). Correct.
- **Gradients (3, 4, 6, 7).** Freshwater teleost plasma about 300 mOsm per kg against fresh water of a few mOsm; marine teleost plasma about 350 to 400 mOsm per kg against seawater about 1,000 to 1,100: "about three times saltier" (6). Science check to confirm the factor (fallback: "much saltier", shorter).
- **"Drinks almost nothing" (1, 5).** Freshwater teleosts drink very little (some water is swallowed with food); the site's "never drinks at all" and "do not drink" overstate. Spoken "almost nothing" throughout. Correct.
- **Freshwater kidneys (5).** Large volumes of dilute urine; the kidney reabsorbs most filtered salts. Correct.
- **Gill uptake (5).** Ionocytes ("chloride cells") actively take up Na⁺ and Cl⁻ from fresh water using ATP-driven transport. Correct at Year 11 ("cells in its gills pump salts in by active transport").
- **Marine drinking and gut (7).** Marine teleosts drink seawater; the gut absorbs NaCl and water follows, so water is absorbed "along with more salt". Correct.
- **Marine gills (7).** Ionocytes actively secrete Cl⁻ (Na⁺ follows); ATP-driven. Correct at Year 11 ("pump the extra salt back out").
- **Marine urine (7, 10, 11a).** Small volume, roughly isosmotic to plasma (not concentrated); excretes Mg²⁺ and SO₄²⁻. Spoken "only a little urine"; the site's "concentrated urine" is site error 2. Correct.
- **Sharks (9).** Elasmobranchs retain urea (and TMAO) so body fluids are about isosmotic, slightly hyperosmotic, to seawater; they gain a little water rather than losing it; a rectal gland secretes NaCl. "About as concentrated as seawater, and they lose very little water": correct (science check may prefer "they don't lose water"). Port Jackson shark (Heterodontus portusjacksoni) is an Australian elasmobranch: correct choice for the plate.
- **Salt glands (9).** Sea turtles: lachrymal salt glands near the eye secrete a fluid about twice as salty as seawater ("tears"). Seabirds: supraorbital nasal salt glands, secretion drips from the nostrils. Reptile and bird kidneys cannot produce urine more concentrated than seawater (site b2). Shearwaters (for example the short-tailed shearwater, Ardenna tenuirostris) are Australian seabirds with salt glands. Correct.
- **Invertebrates (9, screen only).** Many marine invertebrates are osmoconformers. Correct.
- **Barramundi (10).** Lates calcarifer is catadromous: spawns in coastal and estuarine salt water; many juveniles move into fresh water to grow, and adults return to the sea to spawn; some populations stay in estuaries (hence "many"). Acclimation to salinity change takes days; abrupt transfer can cause mortality. Science check to confirm "days".

Values chosen inside these ranges; tag "illustrative data". Science check to confirm the ranges.
