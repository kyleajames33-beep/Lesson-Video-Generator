### 6. the response eases: negative feedback
> Glucose leaves the blood, so blood glucose falls back towards its normal range. As it falls, the beta cells detect less of a rise, so they release less insulin. Insulin drops back to a low background level, not to zero. That's negative feedback. The rise caused a response that lowered it, and the response eased as the rise **disappeared**.

### 9. why two hormones
> Why two hormones? Think back to temperature. Your body warms itself by shivering and cools itself by sweating, so it can push either way. Glucose control works the same way. Insulin pushes glucose down, and glucagon pushes it up. With only one hormone, the body could push one way, or just ease off and wait. And in reality, both hormones are present at some level all the time. It's the balance between them that **shifts**.

### 10. a whole day: steered, not fixed
> Put a whole day together. Each meal sends glucose up, and insulin follows a little after it. Between meals, glucagon rises, and glucose is held up. The line isn't flat. ### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Blood glucose: usually about 4 to 6 mmol/L between meals. Glucose: fuel. Glycogen: stored glucose (liver, muscle). Glucagon: a hormone.
