## Decisions

- **Site sources used:** lesson 02 card 2b (Part A iron thiocyanate forward and back, Part B burning magnesium; "going one way is not evidence of reversibility, coming back is"; "reversible in principle, and reversible under the conditions available to you"; the control tube "colour judgements are unreliable from memory"); lesson 05 card 3b (stock of equal volumes of 0.002 mol/L Fe(NO3)3 and KSCN, "pale ... so a change can be seen in either direction", six tubes, tube 1 the untouched reference, solid KSCN and solid Fe(NO3)3, 0.1 mol/L NaOH dropwise, the dilution control "same number of drops of distilled water", a hot bath at about 60 C and an ice bath, return to room temperature, white tile); lesson 05 cobalt chloride paper and the memory aid "Pink in the rain, blue in the desert"; both safety boxes (glasses and gloves, never acidify thiocyanate, water bath not a naked flame, tongs or holder for hot tubes, look away from burning magnesium, never water on a magnesium fire, labelled residues container).
- **Site departures (recorded here only):** (1) The temperature test uses the C3 cobalt solution, [Co(H2O)6]2+ (pink) + 4Cl- ⇌ [CoCl4]2- (blue) + 6H2O, forward endothermic, in a stoppered tube, instead of heating the iron thiocyanate tube 6. The cobalt change (pink, purple, blue) is far easier to see, it is the syllabus example "heating cobalt(II) chloride hydrate" in its usual school form, and C3 already taught its direction. The iron tube 6 is mentioned on screen as an alternative. (2) The hydrate reversibility test (heat pink CoCl2·6H2O crystals to blue, add water back) is the C3 "oven/water-removal side example" that C3 deferred to a practical home; this is that home. It is framed explicitly as an **open** reversibility test, not an equilibrium, which avoids the site's CoCl2·6H2O(s) ⇌ CoCl2(s) + 6H2O(g) equilibrium claim and keeps C1's closed-system rule intact. (3) The site's reversal step for Part A (restore Fe3+ after NaOH) is not used: the rust-brown Fe(OH)3 left in the tube muddies the colour comparison and the step needs care to explain (added Fe3+ first reacts with leftover OH-). The cobalt hydrate and the cobalt temperature return carry the reversibility evidence more cleanly.
- **Main teaching move:** two investigations, one question each. (A) *Is it reversible?* Push it one way, then reverse the change and see whether the start comes back (cobalt hydrate), against an irreversible contrast (burning magnesium). (B) *How does an equilibrium respond to a disturbance, and how do you know the change is real?* Reference tube, one change per tube, side-by-side judging, the dilution control, temperature in a sealed tube. Then validity, reliability and accuracy in plain words, the risk assessment, and two classic exam questions.
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) an unchanged appearance cannot tell a running equilibrium from a finished reaction, so evidence needs a push (1); (2) the reversibility test: change, then reverse the change; one change proves nothing, a return does (2); (3) burning magnesium does not come back under any school conditions, so it is treated as irreversible; the hydrate test was open, so it shows reversibility, not an equilibrium (3); (4) an equilibrium investigation needs a closed system, and the method must say what is sealed (4); (5) a pale stock, an untouched reference and one change per tube, judged side by side (5); (6) independent, dependent and controlled variables (6); (7) expected results with the C3 reason in a clause (7); (8) adding a solution dilutes, so a paler tube is not yet evidence; the dilution control separates a shift from dilution (8); (9) temperature in a sealed tube, with the return to room temperature as a second reversibility check (9); (10) validity, reliability, accuracy (10); (11) risk assessment (11); memory aids (12); two attempts with brief feedback (13 to 16); decision rule and handoff (17); key notes (18).
- **Entry knowledge:** C1 (dynamic equilibrium needs a closed system; equal rates; a finished reaction is static), C2 (reversible reactions approach equilibrium), C3 (adding a reactant shifts right and deepens the iron thiocyanate red; removing one shifts left; heating favours the reaction that takes in heat; the cobalt solution is pink cold, blue hot, purple at room temperature; Le Chatelier's principle), C4a (sealed NO2 syringe and tubes), and Year 11 Working Scientifically (an independent variable is changed, a dependent variable is measured, controlled variables are kept the same; a risk assessment lists hazards and controls). The words validity, reliability and accuracy are given a plain meaning here even if met in Year 11.
- **Learner-visible vocabulary (each with a plain meaning at first use):** reversible (can be made to run back to the starting substances by changing the conditions); irreversible (no available change brings the start back); open system, closed system (recalled from C1 in a clause: matter can or cannot get in or out); reference tube (a tube that gets no change, used for every comparison); dilution control (a tube that gets the same volume of plain water); independent variable (what you change), dependent variable (what you observe or measure), controlled variables (what you keep the same); validity (does the test measure what it claims to?), reliability (do repeats give the same result?), accuracy (how close a result is to the true value); hazard (what could cause harm), risk assessment (each hazard, the harm and the control).
- **Expected results table (on screen in scene 7 and the notes, all against tube 1):** tube 2 + Fe(NO3)3 crystals: deeper red (shift right; the yellow-brown tint of the added iron also adds a little colour); tube 3 + KSCN crystals: deeper red (shift right); tube 4 + 0.1 mol/L NaOH dropwise: paler, with a rust-brown solid (Fe3+ removed as Fe(OH)3, shift left); tube 5 + the same number of drops of water: very slightly paler (dilution only); cobalt tube: hot bath about 60 C blue, ice bath pink, room temperature purple again; sealed NO2 tube: hot browner, ice paler (C4a and C9 plates). Optional on screen only: the C4a NO2 syringe (teacher demonstration) for volume and pressure; the iron tube heated as the site's tube 6 (paler when hot, because the iron thiocyanate forward reaction gives out heat; not spoken).
- **Attempt systems (unseen, every condition visible):** (A1) a tube made paler by ten drops of silver nitrate solution, concluded "shifted left": what extra tube makes the conclusion valid, and why? Answer: an identical tube with ten drops of water (dilution control). Crossed mistake: "repeat it three times" (that improves reliability, not validity). (A2) heated pink cobalt chloride crystals turn blue, concluded "reversible": evaluate. Answer: one direction only; add water to the cooled blue solid and see pink return; the open tube shows reversibility, not an equilibrium. Crossed mistake: "correct, the colour changed".

- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Cobalt chloride hydrate: CoCl2·6H2O is pink to red-violet; on heating it loses water (via the violet dihydrate) to blue anhydrous CoCl2; water condenses near the cooler mouth of the tube; adding water to the cooled blue solid restores pink and releases heat (hydration is exothermic, dehydration endothermic). "The tube warms slightly" is said, not a temperature. (b) Magnesium oxide can be reduced to magnesium industrially (for example by heating with silicon, or by electrolysis of the chloride made from it), so the video says "under any conditions you can use in a school lab" (site: "reversible in principle" versus "under the conditions available to you"). (c) The cobalt solution is usually made by the teacher from cobalt chloride and concentrated hydrochloric acid (or a high chloride salt solution); the stoppered tube is supplied ready. It must be kept away from thiocyanate (acid plus thiocyanate). (d) Iron(III) nitrate solid adds its own yellow-brown tint (C3 note); the deeper red in tube 2 is mainly the shift, and the table says so. (e) Ten drops is about 0.5 mL; into about 5 mL of stock that is about a 9 percent dilution, so the dilution control is only slightly paler, while NaOH removal fades the red strongly. (f) The iron thiocyanate forward reaction is exothermic (mildly), so the site's heated tube 6 goes paler; not spoken, because the cobalt test carries temperature. (g) Pronunciation: "thiocyanate" (thigh-oh-SIGH-an-ate); "iron three plus"; "cobalt chloride"; "nitrogen dioxide"; "Le Chatelier" as C3 if spoken (it is not spoken here). (h) "Validity", "reliability" and "accuracy" follow common NESA usage: validity, the extent to which a test investigates what it claims to, with only the independent variable changed and the others controlled; reliability, consistency on repetition; accuracy, closeness to the true or accepted value. Precision is not introduced.

Main question: *How do you show in the lab that a reaction is reversible, and that an equilibrium really responds to a disturbance, with evidence you can trust?*
Entry knowledge (C1 to C4 and Year 11): closed systems and dynamic equilibrium (C1); shift directions for adding or removing a substance and for heating or cooling, the iron thiocyanate and cobalt solution colours (C3); sealed NO2 tubes and syringe (C4a); variables and risk assessments (Year 11 Working Scientifically).
Stops after: stating the reversibility test (change, then reverse the change; a return is the evidence) and the magnesium contrast; stating whether a set-up is open or closed and why an equilibrium test needs a closed one; planning the six-tube disturbance investigation with a reference tube, one change per tube, side-by-side judging and a dilution control; naming the independent, dependent and controlled variables; predicting the results and giving the C3 reason in a clause; defining validity, reliability and accuracy for this investigation; writing a risk assessment row (hazard, harm, control) for the real hazards. No K, no Q, no colourimeter data (P02), no conduct.
Handoff: putting a number on where an equilibrium sits (route chem-m5-c05), without naming K (C3 and C4b convention).

### 1. hook (does a still tube prove anything?)
> This tube of iron thiocyanate has sat at the same pale red for an hour. Is that proof that it's an equilibrium, with both reactions still running? Not on its own. A finished reaction looks still, too. To show that a reaction can run both ways, and that it responds to a change, you have to **push it** and watch what happens. So how do you plan an investigation with evidence you can trust?

### 2. the reversibility test (push it, then bring it back)
> Here's the test for reversibility. Change the conditions so the reaction runs one way, then **reverse** the change and see whether the starting substance comes back. Heat pink cobalt chloride crystals in a dry test tube. Water leaves the crystals as steam, droplets collect near the top, and the solid turns **blue**. Going one way proves nothing yet. Now add a few drops of water to the cooled blue solid. It turns **pink again**, and the tube warms slightly. It went there and came back, so the reaction is **reversible**.

Visual: the "push it, bring it back" loop writes at the top: an arrow out ("change") and an arrow back ("reverse the change"), with "start comes back?" at the end. On "Heat pink cobalt chloride crystals" the painted clamped tube of pink crystals (`p01-cocl2-crystals-pink-v1.png`, Codex item 1) sits over the painted burner (`m5x-bunsen-burner-v1.png`, Codex item 3) on the clamp stand; on "droplets" small coded droplets bead near the mouth (or the plate's own droplets in item 2); on "blue" the plate crossfades to `p01-cocl2-crystals-blue-v1.png` (optional process clip) and the first loop arrow lights. ### 3. the contrast: burning magnesium (and an open tube)
> Compare burning magnesium. It burns with a blinding white flame and leaves white magnesium oxide powder. Add water, heat it, cool it: nothing you can do in a school lab brings shiny magnesium back. So it's **irreversible**. And notice that the cobalt test ran in an **open** tube. The steam escaped, so we had to add water back. That's fine for showing reversibility, but it isn't an equilibrium.

### 4. closed systems for equilibrium
> To study an equilibrium, nothing that takes part can escape. In a tube of dissolved ions, the ions stay in the solution, and a gas mixture needs a **sealed** tube or syringe. So write the condition into your method: what's sealed, and what could get in or out. That matters, because a response to a disturbance only makes sense if the system is **closed**.

Visual: three plates in a row, each with the dashed boundary outline: the iron tube (ions stay in the water; tag "ions cannot leave"), the sealed NO2 tube (`c9-no2-tube-brown-v1.png`; tag "sealed gas"), the syringe (`c4a-syringe-barrel-v1.png` with `c4a-syringe-plunger-v1.png`, tip sealed; tag "sealed syringe"). On "write the condition" a method line writes in pen: "System: closed. Sealed: ___. Can enter or leave: ___." On "closed" each boundary turns solid teal with the tag "closed: matter stays in; heat can still pass" (C1 recall). Hold about 1 second.

### 5. the disturbance set-up (one reference, one change per tube)
> Now the disturbance investigation. Mix equal volumes of dilute iron three nitrate and potassium thiocyanate to get a pale red stock. Pale is deliberate: you need to see a change in **either direction**. Pour equal amounts into six identical tubes. Tube one is the **reference**, and you never touch it. Every other tube gets **one change**, and you judge every colour **side by side** with tube one against a white tile, because colour from memory is unreliable.

Visual: the two reagent bottles (`m5x-bottle-fe-v1.png`, `m5x-bottle-scn-v1.png`) with coded labels "0.002 mol/L Fe(NO3)3" and "0.002 mol/L KSCN"; on "equal volumes" two coded pours meet in the painted beaker (`m6-beaker-v1.png`, coded pale red fill). ### 6. the variables
> So, the variables. The **independent variable** is what you change: which substance you add, or the temperature. The **dependent variable** is what you observe: the colour, compared with the reference. The **controlled variables** are everything you keep the same: the volume of stock in each tube, the tube size, the background and lighting, and how long you wait before judging.

Visual: the variables card writes in the right zone, one row per cue: "Independent (what you change): substance added, or temperature"; "Dependent (what you observe): colour compared with tube 1"; "Controlled (kept the same): volume of stock, tube size, white tile and lighting, time before judging". ### 10. validity, reliability and accuracy
> Three words exam questions love. **Validity**: does the test really measure what it claims to? The reference tube, one change per tube and the dilution control make your conclusion valid. **Reliability**: do you get the same result when you repeat it? Repeat each tube, compare with other groups, and have two people judge the colours. **Accuracy**: how close is your result to the true value? Judging by eye is only rough. A colourimeter gives a number, and you'll see that later in the module.

Visual: three cards write in turn in the right zone, each with its plain question and this investigation's answer. "Validity: does it test what it claims? Only one change per tube; reference; dilution control." (pen links to tubes 1, 5). "Reliability: same result on repeats? Repeat each tube; compare groups; two observers." (three small coded tube rows appear, matching). "Accuracy: close to the true value? Eye judgement is rough; a colourimeter gives a number." (the painted colourimeter, `m5x-colourimeter-v1.png`, small, with a pen "later" tag). A crossed chip under the reliability card: "repeating fixes a missing control". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 11. the risk assessment
> Before any of this, you write a risk assessment: each hazard, the harm it could do, and how you'll control it. Cobalt chloride is harmful if swallowed, may cause cancer if its dust is breathed in, and can cause allergies, so use small amounts, avoid making dust, and wear gloves. Never let thiocyanate touch an **acid**, because that can release a very toxic gas. Sodium hydroxide, even dilute, can harm your **eyes**, so wear safety glasses. The hot bath can scald, so use a test tube holder. Never look straight at burning magnesium. And all the waste goes into a labelled container, never down the sink.

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Reversibility test: change the conditions, then reverse the change. A return to the start is the evidence; one change is not. Cobalt chloride crystals: heat, pink to blue (water driven off); add water, blue to pink (warms). Burning magnesium: no school condition brings magnesium back, so it is treated as irreversible.
2. An equilibrium investigation needs a closed system (ions in solution, sealed gas tube or syringe). State what is sealed. The heated open crystal tube shows reversibility, not an equilibrium.
3. Disturbance method: pale Fe3+/SCN- stock in six identical tubes; tube 1 is the untouched reference; one change per tube; judge side by side on a white tile. Independent variable: what is added, or temperature. Dependent variable: colour compared with tube 1. Controlled: stock volume, tube size, background and lighting, time before judging.

Sealed NO2: hot browner, ice paler.
5. Validity: tests what it claims (one change, reference, dilution control). Reliability: repeats agree (repeat tubes, other groups, two observers). Accuracy: close to the true value (eye judgement is rough; a colourimeter gives numbers).
6. Risks: cobalt chloride harmful if swallowed, may cause cancer if dust inhaled, allergies (small amounts, no dust, gloves); thiocyanate plus acid releases a very toxic gas (never mix); NaOH harms eyes (glasses); hot bath scalds (holder); burning magnesium (never look at the flame); waste into the labelled residues container.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a still tube is not evidence: a finished reaction also looks still (1). Why a return is the evidence of reversibility: going one way happens in irreversible changes too; only coming back shows the reverse reaction can run (2, 3). Why the closed system: a response to a disturbance only means something if nothing escapes (4). Why a pale stock: so a change can show in either direction (5). Why side by side: colour memory is unreliable (5). Why the results go the way they do: the C3 cause in a clause (adding a reactant shifts right; removing one shifts left; heating favours the reaction that takes in heat) (7, 9). Why the dilution control: adding a solution adds volume, which alone makes a tube paler (8). Why solids in tubes 2 and 3: volume hardly changes (8). Why validity differs from reliability: repeats cannot fix a missing control (10, 14).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** unchanged appearance means equilibrium (1); one change proves reversibility (2, 16); an open tube is an equilibrium (3, 16); paler means a left shift without a dilution control (8, 14); repeats fix validity (10, 14); colour judged from memory (5); blue for wet (12).

## Safety facts check (self-check, 18 Oct; science check to confirm)

Every hazard in scene 11 and the notes was checked against supplier safety data sheets (GHS/CLP classifications vary a little by supplier and product form; the video states only the hazards common to all of them) and against the site's own safety boxes (L02 card 2b, L05 card 3b), which follow school-laboratory practice.

- **Cobalt(II) chloride hexahydrate:** SDSs (Sigma-Aldrich, Loba Chemie, Morphisto 2 percent solution, Duchefa) agree on H350i (may cause cancer by inhalation), H334 (may cause allergy or asthma symptoms or breathing difficulties if inhaled), H317 (may cause an allergic skin reaction), H341 (suspected of causing genetic defects) and H360F or H360FD (may damage fertility; FD adds the unborn child), with H302 (harmful if swallowed) and H410 or H411 (aquatic) on most.
