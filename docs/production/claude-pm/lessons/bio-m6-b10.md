  2. New (this read). Site text (L09 card 2 Plant Case and Analyse model): the Bt cotton discussion gives no resistance management. Proposed site wording: "raising the risk of resistance evolving in target pests, which Australian growers manage by planting non-Bt refuge crops under a resistance management plan."   3. New (this read). Site text (L09 Discuss model, step 3): "modification can improve animal welfare directly, as with cattle edited to be born hornless so that painful dehorning is unnecessary", with the note "The hornless cattle case is valuable because it puts welfare on the pro side ... and markers reward". The case is real (Carlson et al. 2016), but in 2019 US Food and Drug Administration scientists found that the bacterial plasmid used in the editing had been inserted at the target site in one edited bull's genome, including antibiotic resistance genes (Norris et al. 2020), and four of six offspring studied inherited it (Young et al. 2020). Presenting the case without this omits the main evidence about its outcome, and "markers reward" is an unsupported marking claim. Proposed site wording: "... as with cattle edited to carry a naturally occurring hornless allele, so that painful dehorning is unnecessary; later checks found unintended bacterial DNA inserted beside the edit in one bull, showing why outcomes must be verified." Scene 7 teaches this.
  4. New (this read). Site text (L09 Discuss model, step 4): "Modified animals may suffer welfare problems from the trait itself, such as skeletal stress in fast-growing lines." Leg and skeletal problems in fast-growing lines are best documented in conventionally bred broiler chickens, not genetically modified animals, so the example does not support a claim about modification. Proposed site wording: "... from the trait itself; for example, pigs given a human growth hormone gene in the 1980s (the Beltsville pigs) developed joint and other health problems." Scene 8 makes no trait-harm claim.
  5. New, minor (this read). Site text (L09 Analyse model, step 3): "A safety trial can establish that a modified crop is not harmful to eat". Trials can find no evidence of harm under tested conditions; they cannot prove harmlessness. Proposed site wording: "A safety assessment can show that no harm was detected when the crop was eaten under tested conditions". Scene 9 says "Evidence can answer these" without claiming proof.

Not an error to fix; noted so attempt 2 uses "evaluate", which does require a judgement.

### 1. hook (two biotechnologies that work)
> Two biotechnologies. A cotton plant that makes its own protein against **caterpillars**. And a dairy bull whose DNA was **edited** so his calves would be born without horns. Both work. But working isn't the same as being a good idea. So how should we **judge** plant and animal biotechnology, and why does an animal case need something extra?

Visual: no people. `m6-bt-cotton-v1.png` at the left; on "caterpillars" the new `m6-helicoverpa-larva-boll-v1.png` appears small beside it and takes a grey stop bar (no tag). `b3b-dairy-bull-side-v1.png` at the right, labelled small in code "a hornless dairy bull" (an ordinary hornless bull plate, not a portrait of the edited bull); on "edited" a short `SlimStrand` draws under it with one cream band ringed (tag "hornless allele"), and a small calf head outline with no horn buds sits beside it. ### 2. what a judgement needs
> A judgement needs three things, each one concrete. A **benefit**: what improves, and by how much. A **harm** or risk: what could go wrong. And for each, **who**: the people, animals or ecosystems affected, called **stakeholders**. One technology can help one group and cost another, so a list of pros and cons isn't enough: say who gets which.

Visual: the `JudgeBoard` draws empty: three column heads write on one per cue, "benefit", "harm or risk", "who". On "stakeholders" the term writes under the "who" head with the gloss "anyone or anything affected", and four grey stakeholder icons line up beneath it (tractor, spray can, bee, building). On "help one group and cost another" a sample benefit chip and a sample harm chip (blank outlines) draw lines to two different icons, so the lines cross the board. On "pros and cons" a two-column "pros | cons" list outline fades in beside the board and takes a grey cross (caption "say who gets which"). Hold one second.

### 3. Bt cotton: the benefit, and who gets it
> Start with the plant. Bt cotton carries a gene from a soil bacterium. Its cells make a protein that's toxic to certain caterpillars that eat the plant. It only works after binding to sites in those insects' guts, which people and most other animals lack. Australian growers once sprayed insecticide many times a season. Together with better pest management, Bt cotton has cut that by about **eighty-five per cent**. That benefits growers, farm workers, nearby communities, and the beneficial insects that broad sprays used to kill.

Visual: the board resets with `m6-bt-cotton-v1.png` pinned at the top. On "toxic to certain caterpillars" the larva plate sits on a boll and a coded protein glyph from the leaf reaches it; the larva greys (tag "Bt protein: certain caterpillars only"). On "many times a season" a coded spray-count bar draws, "before Bt" (tall, spray-can icons stacked). On "eighty-five per cent" a second bar "with Bt" draws much shorter (tag "since 1996: insecticide use down about 85% (CSIRO)", replacing the protein tag; no other number). ### 4. Bt cotton: the risks, and who carries them
> Now the risks. Caterpillars that happen to carry alleles for resistance survive, and pass them on: natural **selection**. Over generations, resistance could spread until Bt stops working. So Australian growers must also plant **refuges**, areas of non-Bt crops where susceptible caterpillars survive and mate with any resistant ones, keeping resistance alleles **rare**. Growers also pay a **licence fee** each season to the company that owns the trait. And Bt doesn't fix one thing: a field of one crop still holds little **biodiversity**.

On "refuges" the selection tag fades and the refuge strip fills with open glyphs that survive; at the boundary, open and filled glyphs pair up and their offspring glyphs are mostly open (on "rare", tag "refuge: keeps resistance alleles rare"). On "biodiversity" the fee chip and its tag dim, and `m6-field-monoculture-mixed-v1.png` appears small with its monoculture half ringed, and a third chip writes "still a monoculture (not caused by Bt)", joined to the field-strip icon. ### 5. a verdict with a condition
> So, a judgement. For Australian cotton, the benefit is large and measured, and it reaches several groups. The main risk, resistance, is real but managed by refuges. Growers pay the fee, yet they've chosen Bt for most Australian cotton. On that evidence, Bt cotton is **justified**, as long as resistance management keeps working. Notice that a strong verdict comes with a **condition**.

### 6. the animal case: hornless dairy cattle
> Now the animal. Most dairy cattle grow horns, and horns can injure other cattle and the people who handle them. So calves usually have their horn buds removed, which is **painful**. Some beef breeds, like Angus, are naturally hornless, because of one **dominant** allele. Crossing dairy cattle with Angus would also bring in beef alleles that lower milk yield. So scientists **edited** just that hornless allele into dairy cattle cells, and produced two bulls, born in 2015, whose calves could inherit it. Here the benefit goes to the animals themselves: no dehorning.

### 7. checking what actually happened
> But in 2019, government scientists checking one bull's DNA found something no one had intended. Bacterial DNA used in the editing, including **antibiotic resistance genes**, had been inserted beside the edit, and four of his six tested calves had inherited it. The calves were healthy, but the company hadn't checked for it. So the idea can still be justified, but only for animals checked to carry the edit alone. Judge what a biotechnology actually did, by **checking the evidence**.

### 8. what an animal case adds
> That's what makes animal cases different. An animal can **suffer**, or be spared suffering, so its welfare is a criterion of its own, beside the benefits to people. Producing edited animals also involves embryos and surrogate cows, and many embryos don't survive. And some people object to editing animals at all, **whatever** the outcome.

Visual: the board stays, with a fourth column sliding in at the right, "the animal's welfare", for animal cases only. ### 9. three kinds of question
> Those objections sort into three kinds of question. **Consequences**: what actually happens, to health, yield or the environment. Evidence can answer these. **Distribution**: who gains and who pays, like the licence fee. Rules and policy decide those. And **principle**: is it acceptable at all? Evidence can't settle that one.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (20 Oct) against the sources named and standard texts (Campbell Biology; OpenStax Biology 2e, 17.1). The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Bt cotton mode of action.** Cry proteins from *Bacillus thuringiensis* are toxic to certain insect larvae (Lepidoptera such as *Helicoverpa armigera* and *H. punctigera* for the Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab in Bollgard varieties); they need activation in an alkaline insect gut and binding to specific gut receptors that mammals lack (OGTR risk assessments). "Toxic to certain caterpillars that eat the plant, but not to people or most other animals": correct.
- **Bt cotton in Australia.** Ingard (one gene, Cry1Ac) was introduced in 1996; Bollgard II (two genes) in 2003-04; Bollgard 3 in 2016-17. Over 90 per cent of Australian cotton is now biotech. Revision 2: CSIRO states that its pest management research and the delivery of insect-resistant GM cotton "have helped reduce insecticide use by 85 per cent in the Australian cotton industry" (csiro.au, Cotton pest management); the ICAC 2010 Australia report gives 80 to 90 per cent fewer sprays and 65 to 75 per cent less active ingredient per hectare with Bollgard II. Narration: "Together with better pest management, Bt cotton has cut that by about eighty-five per cent" (see `../checks/m6b10-claude-science-check.md`). "Australian growers once sprayed insecticide many times a season": conventional cotton commonly received ten or more sprays per season against *Helicoverpa* in the early 1990s.
- **Refuges.** Australian Bt cotton growers must plant refuge crops (non-Bt cotton, pigeon pea or sorghum) under a resistance management plan, a condition of registration (APVMA; CRDC). "Australian growers must also plant refuges, areas of non-Bt crops where susceptible caterpillars survive and breed with any resistant ones": correct (refuges can include non-cotton host crops; "non-Bt crops" covers them).
- **Licence fee.** Australian growers sign a technology user agreement and pay a technology fee per hectare for the Bollgard traits each season. "Growers also pay a licence fee each season to the company that owns the trait": correct for Australia (site correction 1); no worldwide claim.
- **Monoculture.** A field of one crop has low plant diversity; "the farm's biodiversity stays low" refers to the crop field itself and does not claim Bt cotton reduces biodiversity beyond conventional cotton (it may increase beneficial insects by reducing sprays). Correct as stated; b12 and b13 evaluate biodiversity.
- **Hornless dairy cattle.** Most Holstein-Friesian dairy cattle carry horns; calves are commonly disbudded or dehorned, which causes pain (pain relief requirements vary). The Celtic POLLED variant (a dominant allele, an insertion-deletion on chromosome 1) makes Angus and some other breeds naturally hornless. Carlson et al. (2016, Nature Biotechnology 34:479) edited the Celtic POLLED allele into Holstein fibroblasts (TALENs) and produced two bulls (2015) by nuclear transfer. "In 2015, scientists edited that hornless allele into the cells of dairy cattle, and produced bulls whose calves could inherit it": correct (cloning is not named; b16).
- **The plasmid finding.** Norris et al. (2020, Nature Biotechnology 38:163) reported that FDA scientists analysing the genome of one edited bull in 2019 found the donor plasmid, including antibiotic resistance genes (neomycin and ampicillin resistance), integrated at the POLLED target site. Young et al. (2020, Nature Biotechnology 38:225) studied six calves of that bull: all were hornless and healthy, and four inherited the plasmid sequence. Revision 2 narration: "four of his six tested calves had inherited it. The calves were healthy, but the company hadn't checked for it": correct (confirmed in the science check).
- **Embryos and surrogates.** Producing edited cattle by nuclear transfer or embryo editing involves embryo transfer into surrogate cows, with low success rates per embryo (often a few per cent to tens of per cent reaching birth). "Many embryos don't survive": correct as stated.
- **Golden Rice.** Ye et al. (2000, Science 287:303) reported rice engineered to make beta-carotene in the endosperm; Golden Rice 2 (Paine et al. 2005) produces much more. Vitamin A deficiency is a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness (WHO). The card states only the purpose and the deficiency; no regulatory or licence fact appears on the card. Correct.
- **AquAdvantage salmon.** Atlantic salmon carrying a Chinook salmon growth hormone gene under the control of an ocean pout antifreeze protein promoter; reach market size in about 16 to 18 months against about 28 to 36 for conventional farmed Atlantic salmon; produced as all-female triploid (sterile) fish and raised in land-based tanks; approved by the US FDA in 2015 and by Health Canada in 2016. "Reach market size faster ... produced as sterile females, raised in tanks on land": correct as stated in the stem, with no figure and no claim about current sales (the company ended its farming operations in 2024; author note).
