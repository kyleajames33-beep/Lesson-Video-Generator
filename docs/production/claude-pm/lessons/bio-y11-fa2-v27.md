### 3. why it's called negative
> The body would overshoot and get too hot. Because the response opposes the change, it switches itself down as it succeeds. This is called negative feedback: the response acts in the opposite direction to the change. Negative doesn't mean bad, and it doesn't mean something is decreasing. A rise gets pushed down, and a fall gets pushed up. That's how the body maintains homeostasis, keeping its internal conditions within a stable **range**.

That's dangerous overheating, which can progress to heatstroke. Negative feedback keeps a variable in range, but only while the effectors can keep **up**.

### 11. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Negative feedback: the response opposes the change (a rise is pushed down, a fall is pushed up). As the variable returns towards normal, the change shrinks and the response eases. Negative means opposite, not bad.
- Homeostasis: keeping internal conditions within a stable range.
- Temperature rises: receptors (skin, hypothalamus) → hypothalamus → sweat glands and skin arterioles → sweating (evaporation takes heat) and vasodilation (more heat lost) → temperature falls back towards normal. Temperature falls: → skeletal muscles and skin arterioles → shivering and vasoconstriction → temperature rises back.
- Drawing a loop: variable and normal range; both directions; five components in order with real structures; arrows; an arrow back to the variable.
- Not perfectly fixed: feedback corrections take time, so values wobble within a normal range around a set point. The set point can shift (daily rhythm, fever). Responses have limits (humid heat: dangerous overheating, which can progress to heatstroke).

Memory: Negative means opposite, not bad. Corrections take time, so it wobbles.
