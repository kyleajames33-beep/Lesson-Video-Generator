### 2. the only fuel, and a tiny store
> Start inside a muscle fibre. A fibre can't spend glucose or fat directly. The only fuel it can use is **ATP**: adenosine with three phosphates attached. Breaking off the third phosphate releases the energy for contraction, leaving ADP and a loose phosphate. The catch is the store. A muscle holds only about two seconds of ATP at full effort, so it can't rely on a store. It has to rebuild ATP as fast as it's used, by putting the phosphate back on. An **energy system** is a way of doing that, and there are three. The faster a system can rebuild ATP, the less it can rebuild in total.

### 7. lactic acid, and three myths
> The syllabus calls this the lactic acid system, so use that name. But be clear about the culprit. Fast glycolysis makes **lactate** and hydrogen ions, and it's the hydrogen ions that impair contraction. Lactate is actually a fuel. It's carried to other fibres, the heart and the liver, and used again, which is why it's mostly cleared within about an hour, and faster with a light cool-down than sitting still. And next-day soreness isn't lactic acid either. It peaks a day or two later, long after the lactate's gone. It comes from tiny tears in the fibres, mostly after eccentric work.

### 8. a shift, not a switch
> Here's the mistake examiners see most. The systems don't take turns. The sprinter's demand for ATP stays the same, and in the first few seconds, PCr supplies nearly all of it. As the store runs low, its share falls, and glycolysis, which was running all along, rises to fill the gap. Nothing switches on or off. Both shares are above zero the whole time, and together they always add up to what the movement needs. That's why even a hundred raises blood lactate.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Hook (1, 4).** Elite 100 m sprinters reach peak speed around 50 to 70 m and decelerate over the final 20 m (race analysis; the trace is "illustrative pattern"); about 10 s for a 100 m.
- **ATP (2).** ATP → ADP + P + energy; stored ATP lasts about 2 s at maximal effort (textbook ranges 1 to 3 s); resynthesis by three systems.
- **ATP-PCr (3, 4).** PCr + ADP → ATP + creatine; 1 ATP per PCr; about 10 to 12 s; 95 to 100% intensity; about 50% resynthesis at 30 s and most by 2 to 3 min (full recovery can take longer, 3 to 5 min or more; the video says "most"); resynthesis is aerobic; fatigue by depletion (stores run low, not empty, by 10 s; site correction 18). Inorganic phosphate accumulation as a fatigue contributor is an author note, not learner copy.
- **Glycolytic (5, 6).** Glucose and glycogen (muscle and liver); 2 ATP per glucose anaerobically (net; 3 per glucose unit from muscle glycogen, author note); predominant about 10 s to 2 min; 85 to 95%; 30 to 60 min to clear by-products, glycogen up to a day or more; fatigue by hydrogen ion accumulation lowering pH, inhibiting glycolytic enzymes and calcium binding; 400 m at 45 to 60 s mostly glycolytic.
