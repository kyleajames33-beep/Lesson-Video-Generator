### 2. the aim comes first, and it's goal specific
> A session is planned in an order: aim, safety, warm-up, the main work, and cool-down. The aim comes first, and the syllabus says it should be **goal specific**. That means it states what the session should change, so you can check afterwards whether it worked. Compare three tries. "Do some fitness work" is an activity, not an aim. "Improve fitness" is a direction, but any session would satisfy it. Now this: improve the ability to repeat five-second sprints with fifteen seconds' recovery, measured as the drop-off across ten repetitions. That fixes the quality trained, the work-to-rest structure, the volume and the measure. And it decides everything after it. If you can't say what evidence would show it worked, you don't have an aim yet, only an activity.

### 3. one athlete's aim, a squad's aim
> The same aim plays out differently in the two settings. For the tennis player, it's their own goal, from their own testing and their own season, so it can be very narrow: one weakness, the whole hour. For the netball squad, it's a team goal, chosen because it serves how the team plays, and it has to be worth doing for most of the eighteen. Individual needs are met inside it by **differentiation**: the same drill with different distances, loads, repetitions or roles. And evaluation differs too. One athlete is compared with their own past results, so small changes count. A squad needs team measures plus a sample of individuals, because an average can hide both the player who improved most and the one who barely trained.

### 5. warm-up and cool-down, in the two settings
> Earlier you saw what a warm-up and cool-down do: a general warm-up raises temperature and blood flow, a specific one rehearses the session's movements, and a cool-down returns the body gradually towards rest. What changes here is how they're delivered. For one athlete, the warm-up can be built around their own history and the exact session ahead. For a squad, there's a common core, plus extra work for anyone returning from injury. A squad warm-up also takes longer, because organising eighteen people takes time. And it has to be simple enough to do correctly without the coach watching. The cool-down differs too. One athlete can extend it into flexibility or rehab work. For a squad, it's often the only reliable moment for whole-team feedback and plans for next session.
