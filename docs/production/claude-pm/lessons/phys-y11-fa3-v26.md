### 1. hook (one wire, many turns)
> Last time, we found that one straight wire makes a weak field, just microtesla, curving round it in circles. Now wind that wire into a long coil, with turn after turn side by side. Inside the coil, what will the field look like? Weak and curved, like one wire? Or strong and **straight**?

### 2. what a solenoid is
> This is a solenoid. It's a long coil of wire, wound into many turns, and one turn is a single complete loop. Look at what it's made of. The wire is insulated: it has a thin coating, so current can't pass between wires that touch. The turns are packed close together, all the same size, and all wound the same way. And the coil is much longer than it is **wide**.

On "packed close together" chip "turns close together"; on "all the same size" two turns outline, equal; on "wound the same way" a coded arrow runs round three turns in the same sense. ### 3. why each part matters
> Each part has a job. Suppose the wire were bare. Touching turns would let the current take a short cut, straight across from turn to turn, and most turns would carry no current at all. The insulation forces it to go all the way round every turn. And because the turns are close together and wound the same way, every turn carries current in the same direction round the coil, so their fields can work **together**.

On "together" chip "every turn: current the same way round".

### 4. one loop's field
> To see what the coil does, start with a single loop, cut in half. At the top, the current comes out of the screen. At the bottom, it goes back in. Each side has its own circles of field, like a straight wire. But look between them, in the middle of the loop. Both sets of circles point the same way there, so their fields **add**.

### 5. many loops: strong and even inside
> Now add more turns beside it. Between two neighbouring turns, their circles point in opposite directions, so there the fields nearly cancel. But inside the coil, every turn's field points the same way, along the axis, the line down the middle. All those fields add. So inside, the field lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced. That's a uniform field: the same strength and direction everywhere, away from the ends. Near each end the lines spread out, and that's why a solenoid is made long: most of its inside stays even. And because so many turns add, it's **strong**.

### 6. outside: a bar magnet
> And outside? The lines come out of one end, spread out, and loop round to the other end. That's exactly the shape of a bar magnet's field. Outside, the lines are spread far apart, so the field there is weak. And just like a magnet's, the lines never stop. Inside they run from the south end to the north end, and outside they loop back from north to **south**.

### 8. finding the poles
> The right end. The lines come out there, so it's north. There's a quick way to check from the end. Look straight at an end of the coil. If the current goes round anticlockwise, that end is north. If it goes clockwise, it's south. The letters help: draw arrows on the ends of an N, and they go anticlockwise. On an S, they go clockwise. Or use the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current goes round the turns, and your thumb points to the north **end**.

### 9. reading a side view
> Exam diagrams usually show the coil from the side, with arrows on the front of the turns. Here, the current goes down the front of each turn. To find the poles, turn the coil so you're looking at its right end. The current that went down the front now goes round anticlockwise, so the right end is north. The right-hand rule agrees: curl your fingers down the front of the turns, and your thumb points to the **right**.

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. A simple solenoid is a long coil of many closely spaced, equal turns of insulated wire, all wound the same way, much longer than it is wide.
2. The insulation stops current short-cutting between touching turns, so it flows round every turn.
3. Inside, each turn's field points the same way along the axis and adds (between neighbouring turns the fields cancel), so the field is strong and nearly uniform: straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines, away from the ends.
4. Outside, the field is weak and shaped like a bar magnet's. The lines are continuous: inside from S to N, outside from N to S.
