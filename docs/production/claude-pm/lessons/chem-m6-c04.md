### 2. what neutralising does, and how to choose
> Neutralising means using up the extra **hydrogen ions**, so the pH moves back towards seven. Choosing the base comes down to four questions. Is it **safe** where it's going? Is it **cheap** enough for the amount needed? Does it work **gently**, without overshooting and making things too basic? And what does it **leave behind**: a salt, water, a gas, or a solid?

### 3. the stomach (antacids)

Antacids use bases that barely dissolve in water, so they react mainly with the acid they meet. **Magnesium hydroxide**, from the acid reactions video, gives a salt and water. **Calcium carbonate** gives a salt, water and carbon dioxide, and that gas is why carbonate antacids can make you **burp**.

### 5. agricultural lime
> The fix is **agricultural lime**, which is crushed limestone, calcium carbonate. Each carbonate ion accepts **two hydrogen ions** and breaks into water and carbon dioxide, so hydrogen ions are used up, and the **pH rises**. As the soil becomes less acidic, the aluminium goes back into solid compounds, and the roots recover. Limestone is cheap and safe to spread, and it barely dissolves unless there's acid to react with, so extra lime doesn't make the soil **strongly basic**.

### 6. the chimney (where acid rain comes from)
> The chimney is a different kind of problem. Coal contains a little sulfur, and when the coal burns, the sulfur becomes **sulfur dioxide** gas. In the air, sulfur dioxide dissolves in water droplets to make **sulfurous acid**, and some is oxidised further to **sulfuric acid**. That falls as **acid rain**, which lowers the pH of lakes and streams, and eats away limestone buildings.

### 7. the scrubber (an acidic oxide meets a base)
> So power stations remove it before it leaves the chimney, in a **scrubber**. The exhaust gas passes up through a spray of calcium hydroxide **slurry**, a solid base stirred through water. Calcium hydroxide plus sulfur dioxide gives **calcium sulfite and water**. Sulfur dioxide has no hydrogen of its own, so it can't donate a proton. It's an **acidic oxide**: a non-metal oxide that makes an acid in water and reacts with bases to make a salt. In the slurry's water it first makes **sulfurous acid**, and the hydroxide neutralises that. The calcium sulfite is a **solid**, so it's collected, and it's often turned into gypsum for plasterboard.

### 9. hydrated lime treatment (neutralise and precipitate)
> Treatment plants add **hydrated lime**, calcium hydroxide, to the drainage water. Its hydroxide ions neutralise the hydrogen ions, and as the pH rises, the metal ions meet more hydroxide and form **insoluble hydroxides**, like the rust brown iron hydroxide precipitate you've seen before. The solid settles as **sludge**, so one treatment fixes the pH and **removes the metals**. Why not limestone, like the paddock? It works slowly and stops near neutral, but some metals only drop out at a higher pH. Hydrated lime dissolves more and acts faster, so the plant **controls the dose**. Waste that's too basic, from places like cement works, goes the other way: it's neutralised with an acid, or carbon dioxide.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Neutralising uses up excess H⁺ (or OH⁻), so pH moves towards 7. Choose a base that is safe, cheap and gentle, and check what it leaves behind.
2. Antacids: Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O; CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ (only carbonates give CO₂). NaOH is caustic.
3. Acidic soil (below about pH 5.5, dissolved Al³⁺ harms roots): agricultural lime, CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O + CO₂. The carbonate accepts the H⁺.
4. Flue gas: SO₂ is an acidic oxide (no H of its own; in water it makes H₂SO₃). Scrubber: Ca(OH)₂ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + H₂O; the solid is collected. With limestone: CaCO₃ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + CO₂.
5. Acid mine drainage: hydrated lime (faster, reaches a higher pH) neutralises H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, and the higher pH precipitates metals: Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃(s).

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why neutralising helps: excess H⁺ is used up (2). Why not NaOH: caustic (3). Why antacids act gently: they barely dissolve, so they react where acid meets them (3). Why carbonate antacids cause burping: CO₂ from carbonic acid (3). Why soil turns acidic: leaching and bacteria acting on ammonium fertiliser release H⁺ (4). Why acidic soil stunts plants: dissolved aluminium damages roots (4). Why lime raises pH: carbonate accepts two H⁺ (5). Why extra lime does not overshoot: limestone barely dissolves without acid (5). Where acid rain comes from: sulfur in coal, SO₂, sulfurous and sulfuric acid (6). How SO₂ is neutralised although it has no hydrogen: it makes sulfurous acid in the slurry water first (7). Why mine water gets hydrated lime, not limestone: faster, and a higher pH drops out more metals (9). Why scrubber products are easy to handle: a solid (7).
