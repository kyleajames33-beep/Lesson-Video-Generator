### 1. the puzzle (opening)
> Last time, mitosis gave us two daughter cells, each with the parent's four chromosomes. That's perfect for growth, but sperm and eggs must carry half. Before meiosis, the cell copies its DNA too, just like before mitosis. So how can a cell that copies its chromosomes end up with half as many?

### 11. tracking the numbers

### 16. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Meiosis: DNA copied once, then two divisions, so one diploid cell (2n) makes four haploid cells (n). In animals these become gametes.
- Homologous pair: two matching chromosomes, one from each parent, with the same genes in the same order.
- Meiosis I (prophase I, metaphase I, anaphase I, telophase I + cytokinesis): partners pair (crossing over can swap matching pieces), line up in pairs, then separate. This halves the number; chromosomes are still two chromatids.
- Meiosis II (no copying first; prophase II, metaphase II, anaphase II, telophase II + cytokinesis): single chromosomes line up, centromeres split, sister chromatids separate.
- Model 2n = 4: 4 chromosomes (8 chromatids) → 2 cells of 2 (4 chromatids) → 4 cells of 2 (2 chromatids). Human 46 → 23.
- Mitosis: 1 division, 2 cells, same number, partners never pair. Meiosis: 2 divisions, 4 cells, half the number.
