## Decisions

- **Learner question (route rewrite, audit UB1, kept).** "What chemistry removes toxins when Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples prepare poisonous plant foods?" The answer, in order: some toxins dissolve in water, so soaking and washing carry them away (5, 8, 9); grinding exposes more of the food to the water (6, 7); fresh water keeps the outside concentration low so leaching keeps going (8); heat can break some toxin molecules apart, which is decomposition, or wreck an enzyme that makes a toxin or destroys a vitamin (10); so preparations combine physical and chemical steps (12).

Defined: leaching is removing a soluble substance from a solid by passing water through it. - **Grinding (scenes 6, 7).** 3 s prediction: a whole wedge or chopped shreds, which colours the water faster? Answer: the shreds. Cause in plain words: more surface touches the water, and toxin deep inside has a shorter path out. Grinding and pounding are physical. Surface area is not named as a rate factor (that is m3-v15, later); this is the plain geometric idea only.
- **Running or replaced water (scene 8; concentration recall, m2-v10).** Particles move from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated (plain definition of diffusion; the word is on screen once). In still water the toxin builds up outside, the difference shrinks and leaching slows; fresh or running water keeps the outside concentration near zero, so the difference stays large. A coded two-tank model shows it live (simulation below). Repeating washes is the same idea.
- **Heat (scene 10).** Two effects, both treated as chemical: (1) some toxin molecules decompose when heated, breaking into smaller molecules that are not toxic: a decomposition reaction, AB → A + B, from the toolkit (m3-v02); (2) heat changes an enzyme's folded shape for good (denaturing), so it can no longer speed up its reaction; the protein becomes a different structure (site classification; author note c). Hedged as "some toxins": heat does not destroy every toxin, which is why preparations combine steps.
- **Mistake (scene 11, site common error).** "Heating burns off the toxin" crossed: burning is combustion, a reaction with oxygen that makes carbon dioxide and water (m3-v04); heating a food decomposes some molecules or changes an enzyme; nothing has to burn. Also "soaking destroys the toxin" is crossed in attempt 1 feedback (it moves the toxin into the water unchanged).

Chemistry explains part of why the steps work; it does not replace the knowledge, and it cannot by itself show that a food is safe to eat (site). The knowledge belongs to the communities who hold it, and they are the experts on it; science increasingly learns by working with knowledge holders. Author notes (not learner copy):
(a) General terms (owner rule, 18 Nov 2026): the site's two case studies are not used. The chemistry said is true in general: many plant toxins are glycosides (a sugar part makes them polar and water-soluble); some plants contain enzymes that release a toxin from a precursor when the tissue is damaged, or that destroy a vitamin; heating denatures most enzymes and decomposes some toxins. "Some" is said each time; no claim that every toxin behaves the same way.
(b) Leaching is physical (site table): the toxin dissolves and diffuses out with its identity unchanged. The soaking water is toxic (site key test) and the video says so in a plain clause.
(c) Denaturation as a chemical change: the site classes heat denaturation as chemical (protein structure and function permanently changed). Some textbooks discuss it as a change of shape without new covalent bonds. The video says "treated as a chemical change, because the protein becomes a different structure for good" and the science check confirms the wording against the NSW course materials.
(d) Hydrolysis (water splitting a glycoside) is the site's second chemical process; it is not named here, to keep one new chemical idea (decomposition, which the syllabus toolkit already holds). The 2025 point calls the nardoo case a decomposition, which fits this framing.
(e) Surface area: said as "more surface touching the water, and a shorter path out"; rate language and collision theory belong to m3-v13 to v15 (later), so this is not a forward reference.
(f) Not a recipe: no times, temperatures, quantities, plants or containers are given anywhere, and the practice question says "a documented preparation" with no detail a reader could follow.
(g) Pronunciations: "toxin" (TOK-sin); "denature" (dee-NAY-cher); "F e two O three" spoken as "iron three oxide".

### 3. two kinds of toxin
> A chemist starts by asking what the toxin is like. Some plant toxins are small polar molecules, and many of them carry a sugar part covered in O H groups, so they dissolve easily in **water**. Other plants contain an enzyme, a protein that speeds up one reaction, and that reaction releases a toxin or destroys a **vitamin**. So the two big questions are, does it dissolve in water, and does heat change **it**?

### 5. leaching
> Start with water. Grind the food and soak it, and the polar toxin molecules are pulled out by water molecules, just like any polar substance dissolving. The water carries them away. Removing a soluble substance from a solid this way is called **leaching**. The toxin molecules leave unchanged, so it's a physical change, and the water now holds the toxin. You've seen leaching in a kitchen. Chopped red cabbage in warm water turns the water **purple**.

### 7. grinding opens it up
> The chopped shreds win. Cutting or grinding gives far more surface touching the water, and a molecule deep inside has a much shorter path out. Nothing new forms, so grinding and pounding are physical changes too. For a toxic food, more surface means the toxin leaves **faster**.

### 8. why fresh water matters
> Now the water itself. Particles spread from where they're more concentrated to where they're less concentrated. That's diffusion. In still water, the toxin builds up outside the food, so the difference gets smaller, and leaching slows down and stops. In running water, or water that's replaced again and again, the outside stays almost toxin-free, so the difference stays big and the toxin keeps leaving. That's why fresh water **matters**.

### 9. where the toxin goes
> Notice where the toxin ends up. Leaching doesn't destroy it. The same molecules are now dissolved in the water, at a low concentration if there's lots of water. So the used water is toxic, and it's thrown away, never **drunk**.

Visual: the right tank's outflow collects in a coded grey basin labelled "used water: toxic" (red outline). Concentration tag "c = n ÷ V: small n in a large V, low c" (from an earlier video's concentration line, 36 px). On "drunk" a red hand tag "discard it".

### 10. heat breaks things
> Heat does something different. It can break some toxin molecules into smaller molecules that aren't toxic. One substance becomes two, which is a decomposition, straight from our toolkit. Heat also changes an enzyme's folded shape for good. That's called denaturing. The enzyme can't speed up its reaction any more, and because the protein becomes a different structure, it's treated as a chemical **change**. But heat doesn't destroy every toxin, which is why preparations combine several **steps**.

Visual: stage change: a coded heat strip glows orange under the lens. ### 11. the trap: burning
> A common slip is to say heating burns the toxin off. Burning is combustion, a reaction with oxygen that makes carbon dioxide and water. Cooking a food breaks some molecules apart or changes an enzyme. Nothing has to **burn**.

Visual: a chip "heating burns off the toxin" fades in. The combustion strip at the top right pulses and a small line writes: "combustion: + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O". On "burn" the chip is struck in red (red hand tag "decompose or denature, not burn"). The two heat tiles in the chemical column pulse.

### 12. the whole picture
> So here's the whole picture. Grinding, soaking and fresh water are physical steps that move the toxin out, unchanged. Heating is chemical, because it makes new substances, or wrecks an enzyme. Grind to open, wash to carry, heat to **break**.
