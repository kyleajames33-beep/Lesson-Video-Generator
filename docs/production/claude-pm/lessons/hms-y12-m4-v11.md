### 2. three phases, and what each is for
> An athlete can't be at their best all year, so the year is divided into phases with different aims. That's **periodisation**. The **off-season** is for recovery, rehab and keeping a base, with low volume and intensity. But it isn't total rest. As you saw earlier, plasma volume and stroke volume drop within a couple of weeks, so a short break should lead into lighter, varied training. **Pre-season** builds the fitness the season needs. Volume is high early, then falls as intensity rises, moving from general work to sport-specific work. **In-season** is about maintaining fitness while competing, and keeping athletes available. Volume drops, intensity stays. Across the whole year, volume moves a lot, and intensity much less.

### 3. sub-phases: each block makes the next possible
> A phase isn't one block. Run a fourteen-week pre-season as one uniform block, and it either builds a base and never turns it into game fitness, or starts with game-speed work and has no base to handle it. So it's split into **sub-phases**. Weeks one to five, general preparation: high volume, moderate intensity, an aerobic base and general strength. Weeks six to ten, specific preparation: volume falls, intensity rises, sport-specific conditioning and power. Weeks eleven to thirteen, pre-competition: match-like intensity, tactics and trial games. And week fourteen, a taper. Each sub-phase makes the next one possible. The common school mistake is jumping straight to game-speed work because it looks real.

### 4. peaking and tapering
> **Peaking** means arranging training so the best performance arrives on a chosen day. **Tapering** is how: a planned cut in training before competition. Here's why it works. Hard training builds fitness, but it also builds fatigue, and fatigue hides the fitness. Fatigue clears faster than fitness fades. So a taper cuts enough work for fatigue to clear, but not so long that fitness starts to reverse. Cut volume, often by about a third to a half. Keep intensity, with short efforts at competition speed. Keep training frequency roughly the same. Run it for a few days to about two weeks. The classic error is cutting intensity too, which leaves an athlete rested but slow. The other is tapering too long. And a real peak is a narrow window, so a year can hold only a few.

### 5. sport-specific attributes fill the phases
> The phases are a container. What goes inside comes from the sport's **attributes**: the fitness components and skill requirements it actually demands. Tennis means repeated short, high-intensity efforts over matches of unpredictable length, so agility, speed, anaerobic capacity, an aerobic base for recovery, and shoulder and trunk strength. Its skills are individual, across three surfaces. Rugby league means repeated efforts with contact over eighty minutes, so strength and power for collisions, repeated sprints, and an aerobic base. Its skills are collective: structure and communication. And positions differ. A front-rower and a winger do different jobs, so the team plan is a common framework, with position and individual variation inside it. Same structure, different dose, over a year.

### 6. comparing the two years
> Now compare, and nearly every difference comes from one fact. The individual athlete chooses their calendar. The team is given one. So the individual sets phase boundaries around chosen target events, and can move them. The team's are set by the draw. The individual can peak a few times and treat other events as preparation. The team must compete every week, aiming for one peak at finals. So the individual does full tapers before target events, while the team does a small taper into each weekly game, and a fuller one only for finals. The individual's program is written for one person. The team's is a shared framework. And the same? Both need phases and sub-phases, both respect reversibility, both taper by cutting volume, and both fill the phases from the sport's attributes.
