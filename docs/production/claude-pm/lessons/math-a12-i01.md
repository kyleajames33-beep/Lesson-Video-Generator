- **Learner question kept (route).** "How does a family of primitives become one curve?" The answer, built in order: a gradient rule such as f′(x) = 2x is satisfied by many curves (hook); each is called a primitive, and finding one runs differentiation backwards (2); adding a constant slides a curve without tilting it, because the derivative of a constant is zero, so F(x) + C is a family of primitives (3); and every primitive is in that family, because the gap between two primitives has zero gradient everywhere and so is constant (4); primitives are found by reversing the power rule, sums and multiples (5, 6), written ∫f(x) dx = F(x) + C (6); one known point fixes C, which picks one curve (8).

### 3. why a constant can be added

### 5. reversing the power rule
> So how do we find one primitive? Reverse the power rule. Differentiating multiplies by the power, then lowers it by one. To undo it, raise the power by one, then divide by the new power. Try x squared. Raising the power gives x cubed. But x cubed differentiates to three x squared, three times too **big**. So divide by three. x cubed over three differentiates to x **squared**. Watch its gradient. At every x, the gradient of x cubed over three equals the height of x **squared**. In general, x to the n plus one, over n plus one, differentiates to x to the n. The n plus one cancels, as long as it isn't **zero**. So for x **above** zero, it works for every real n except minus **one**.

### 6. sums, multiples and the integral sign
> Two more facts handle whole polynomials. The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives. So if F is a primitive of f, and G is a primitive of g, then F plus G is a primitive of f plus **g**. And k times F is a primitive of k times **f**, for any constant k. Now the notation for the whole family. The integral of f of x, d x, equals F of x, plus **C**. The function inside is called the **integrand**, and d x says that x is the **variable**. This is called the indefinite integral, and finding it is called **integration**. So the integral of x to the n is x to the n plus one, over n plus one, plus C.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why many curves fit one gradient rule: sliding a curve up or down changes no gradient, because the derivative of a constant is zero (1, 3). Why F(x) + C is every primitive: the gap between two primitives has zero gradient everywhere, so it never rises or falls (4). Why the power rule raises and divides: it undoes multiply-by-the-power and lower-by-one, and the trial x³ is three times too big (5). Why n ≠ −1: n + 1 is the divisor (5). Why term by term works: derivatives of sums and multiples work term by term (6). Why one C: the constants combine (7). Why one point picks one curve: only one member of the family passes through it, and C is the slide needed at that x (8, 13).

## Memory aid check

- **"The gradient rule fixes the shape. One point fixes the height."** Written for this lesson. Meaning, as said in scene 9: integrating gives the family F(x) + C, whose members all have the same shape (the same gradient at every x); one known point fixes C, which is the curve's height. Assumptions checked: "shape" means the gradient at every x, which is identical for all members of F(x) + C (author note a); "one point fixes the height" holds when the family is a single F(x) + C, which is true on an interval (author note b; all functions in this video are polynomials on all real x); the point must be on the required curve (it is a given). It does not claim that a point fixes a curve when the domain is in separate pieces (not met here; i04 meets it). It helps a new case: given any f′ and a point, the student integrates for the shape and substitutes for the height, and it explains why C is not simply the y-value (the height is fixed at the given x, scene 8 and feedback 2). Three bean shoots on three bookcase shelves grow by the same rule, so their height graphs are one shape slid up. If a curve has gradient 2x at every x, which curve is it? x² works, but so does x² + 3, and infinitely many more. See why: sliding a curve up or down changes no gradient, because the derivative of a constant is zero, and any two primitives have a gap that never rises or falls, so on an interval they differ by a constant. Then build the power rule for primitives by reversing differentiation and prove it by differentiating (n ≠ −1), integrate sums term by term, meet the notation ∫f(x) dx = F(x) + C, and use one point on the curve to find C. Worked examples: ∫(3x² + 4x − 5) dx, and the curve with gradient 3x² − 4x + 2 through (1, 5). Two exam-style questions at the end.
