## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "Why do Group 1 metals react differently with water?" The answer, in order: they all make the same products (6), because each atom loses one outer electron (7); that electron is held less tightly down the group, so it is lost faster and the reaction is more vigorous (7); what you see also depends on conditions and the surface (10, 11).
- **Opening frame (equals v14's final frame).** The Group 1 first-ionisation-energy bars (Li 520, Na 496, K 419 kJ/mol) with an orange arrow "easier to lose the outer electron", `y11c-trough-water-v1.png` at the right with a "?" over the water, and the chip "Next: why do Group 1 metals react differently with water?".
- **Safety first, stated plainly (route practical; Revision 2, root decision on the brief 178 safety blocker).** Current NSW chemical-safety (CSIS) entries need a staff login and could not be verified, so the video uses the approved-video route. Scene 2: students never handle alkali metals; in school these reactions are shown only as a teacher demonstration or on video; the video shows them as illustrated clips (the aligned trough plates inside a coded clip frame). No metal size, distance or screen specification is given anywhere in learner copy. Alkali metals are stored under oil, never touched, and rubidium and caesium are never used in schools. The teacher is never pictured; the piece appears by coded motion.
- **Observations, one metal per scene (painted, aligned plates).** Lithium floats, fizzes steadily, slowly shrinks and doesn't melt. Sodium floats, melts into a silvery ball, skims about and fizzes fast; held still, it can catch fire with a yellow-orange flame. Potassium floats, melts, and the hydrogen catches fire at once with a lilac flame; it crackles and may spit. Each plate is the same trough at the same scale, so the cross-fades compare like with like. Data tags: melting points (Li 181, Na 98, K 63 °C) and densities (Li 0.53, Na 0.97, K 0.89 g/cm³), sourced (author note a).
- **One reaction, three speeds.** All three make hydrogen gas and a metal hydroxide; phenolphthalein turns pink because the hydroxide makes the water alkaline. Word equation, then 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g), with "Li" and "K" substituted on screen. Students know simple formulae and state symbols from Stage 5 and v07; the equation is shown, not balanced as a skill (balancing is Module 2).
- **Cause (site l18, linked to v14).** "Metals react by losing electrons." Each atom gives its one outer electron to water; down the group the outer electron is further out at the same core charge, so it is held less tightly (first ionisation energy 520, 496, 419 kJ/mol), lost more readily, and the reaction runs faster. Faster reaction releases heat faster: sodium and potassium melt (their melting points are low), and the heat ignites potassium's hydrogen. The video says "more vigorous" (faster, more violent), not "releases more energy overall" (author note c).
- **Prediction (3 s, scenes 8 and 9).** What would rubidium do? Answer from secondary sources: even more violent; rubidium and caesium react explosively. Their ionisation energies (403 and 376 kJ/mol) continue the trend.
- **Conditions and films distinguished (route stop).** Scene 10: a fair comparison needs the same size piece, the same water and temperature, and fresh surfaces; a bigger piece or warmer water changes what you see without changing the metal. Scene 11: alkali metals tarnish in air (a dull film forms as the surface reacts with oxygen and water vapour), so they are stored under oil; a freshly cut surface is shiny and dulls quickly; a thick film can slow the start of a reaction. The site's "oxide layer" caveat is used for Group 1 only (aluminium is named as a later example in a grey tag).
- **Memory aid:** "Further out, easier off, faster fizz." Checked in "Memory aid check".

Author notes (not learner copy):
(a) Physical data from the PubChem periodic table (retrieved 5 November 2026): melting points Li 453.65 K (180.5 °C), Na 370.95 K (97.8 °C), K 336.53 K (63.4 °C), Rb 312.46 K (39.3 °C), Cs 301.59 K (28.4 °C); densities Li 0.534, Na 0.97, K 0.89, Rb 1.53, Cs 1.93 g/cm³ (some tables give K as 0.862; both are below 1). Rubidium and caesium are denser than water and sink; not said in learner copy (secondary sources only).
(b) Observations as commonly reported for school demonstrations (for example the Royal Society of Chemistry's "Reactions of the alkali metals with water" demonstration notes): Li floats, fizzes, does not melt; Na melts into a ball and darts about, may ignite (yellow-orange flame) if held still, for example on wet filter paper; K melts and ignites at once with a lilac flame, often ending with a small crack or spit as the molten ball breaks up. The yellow-orange and lilac colours are the metal's emission in the flame (v16).
(c) Rate against extent: lithium's overall reaction with water is at least as thermodynamically favourable as potassium's (standard electrode potentials Li⁺/Li −3.04 V, K⁺/K −2.93 V, Na⁺/Na −2.71 V), yet it reacts slowest. Ionisation energy is one step of the energy cycle; hydration of the small Li⁺ ion is very favourable. Vigour (rate) follows the ease of losing the outer electron and the low melting points, which is the school-level explanation used on the site. Learner copy uses "reacts faster, more vigorously" and never claims potassium "releases more energy". Electrode potentials are Module 3 and Year 12.
(d) Equation: 2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g); MOH is fully dissociated in solution (M⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)). The electron hand-off inset is a simplification of a fast multi-step process at the metal surface.

> Here's the surprise. All three make the same two products. The gas is hydrogen. ### 7. why: one electron, held less tightly
> Why the different speeds? In each reaction, every metal atom loses its one outer electron, and becomes a positive **ion**. The water takes that electron, which is what makes the hydrogen and the hydroxide. Last time, we saw that going down the group, the outer electron is further from the nucleus, with the same core charge, so it's held less **tightly**. Potassium gives up its electron most readily, so its reaction runs **fastest**. A faster reaction also heats the metal faster, so sodium and potassium melt, and potassium's hydrogen catches **fire**.

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Group 1 metals (alkali metals) react with water to form a metal hydroxide solution (alkaline) and hydrogen gas: 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g). Teacher demonstration or video only; stored under oil.
2. Observations: Li floats and fizzes steadily; Na melts into a ball, darts about, fizzes fast; K melts and its hydrogen ignites with a lilac flame. Rb and Cs react explosively (secondary sources).
3. Reactivity increases down Group 1: each atom loses its one outer electron; down the group it is further from the nucleus at the same core charge, so it is held less tightly (lower first ionisation energy) and lost more readily.
4. Fair comparisons need the same piece size, water volume and temperature, and fresh surfaces. Tarnish (a film from reaction with air) can slow the start without changing the metal's reactivity.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Further out, easier off, faster fizz.*

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style).** "In a teacher demonstration, equal-sized, freshly cut pieces of lithium and potassium are added to separate troughs of water at the same temperature. Potassium reacts much more vigorously than lithium. Explain this difference in terms of atomic structure. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Both are Group 1 metals that react by losing their single outer electron (1). Potassium has more occupied shells (4 against 2), so its outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, with the same core charge (+1) (1). It is held less strongly (lower first ionisation energy, 419 against 520 kJ mol⁻¹), so potassium loses its electron more readily and reacts faster (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "potassium has more electrons to give away".

**Attempt 2 (scene 15; original wording, HSC style).** "Two pieces of sodium of equal mass are added to separate troughs of water at the same temperature. Piece A was freshly cut. Piece B had been exposed to air and its surface is dull. Predict which piece begins to react faster, and explain your prediction. (2 marks)"
Model answer: Piece A begins to react faster (1). Piece B is coated with tarnish (a film formed by reaction with oxygen and water vapour in air), which separates the metal from the water at first; piece A's fresh metal surface contacts the water immediately (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "the same rate, because they are the same element".
