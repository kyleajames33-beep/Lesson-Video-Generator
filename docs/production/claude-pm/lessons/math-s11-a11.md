### 1. hook: no formula fits
> This garden pond has a wobbly edge. It isn't a circle, or a rectangle, or any shape with a name, so there's no formula for its area. But you can still measure it. Lay a tape along it, and measure how wide it is at a few evenly spaced places. Surveyors, landscapers and pool builders do exactly this. Can a handful of widths tell us the area, and even how much water it holds? Yes, approximately. So how does the trapezoidal rule estimate an irregular area, or a **volume**?

### 2. one application: pretend it's straight
> Start with something simpler. A block of land has a straight front, eighteen metres deep, and two parallel side boundaries, twenty-eight metres and thirty-four metres long. The back is a bit curved. Pretend the back is straight. Then the block is a trapezium, and you know its area from earlier, the average of the two parallel sides, times the distance between them. In symbols, A is about equal to h over two, times d f plus d l. Here d f is the first length, twenty-eight. D l is the last length, thirty-four. And h is the perpendicular distance between them, eighteen. Half of eighteen is nine, and twenty-eight plus thirty-four is sixty-two. Nine times sixty-two is about five hundred and fifty-eight square **metres**. We say about, because the real back boundary isn't quite straight. The rule gives an estimate, a close answer, not an exact one. It's printed on the reference **sheet**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): a coded block plan (900 × 600 px): two parallel sides "28 m" and "34 m" (blue), the distance between them "18 m" (amber, with right-angle marks), a slightly curved back boundary. ### 3. up to four applications: the pond
> One trapezium is fine for a block with one curved side. The pond curves everywhere, so cut it into strips. Measure the width every two metres along its eight metre length. The widths are nought, three point two, four point one, three point six, and nought again, because the pond comes to a point at each end. That's four strips, so apply the rule four times. Each strip has its own first and last length, and h is two, the gap between widths. Strip one, two over two times nought plus three point two, is three point two. Strip two, three point two plus four point one, is seven point three. Strip three, four point one plus three point six, is seven point seven. Strip four, three point six plus nought, is three point six. A zero width just makes that strip a triangle, and the rule still works. Add the four, and the pond is about twenty-one point eight square **metres**. Forgetting to halve h doubles every strip, and that's the **slip**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): the pond plate (900 px wide) with a coded baseline "8 m" along it; on "every two metres" width lines drop at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 m labelled "0", "3.2", "4.1", "3.6", "0" (blue); h marks "2 m" between them (amber), hand tag "h: the gap". On "four strips" chords join the width tops and the four strips outline, hand tag "4 strips, 4 trapeziums". On "triangle" strip 1 pulses, hand tag "0 is fine". On "metres" row "A ≈ 3.2 + 7.3 + 7.7 + 3.6 = 21.8 m²" (framed green). ### 4. why more strips get closer
> Why bother with four strips? Watch what happens with fewer. With just one application across the whole pond, the first and last widths are both nought, so the estimate is nought square metres, which is useless. With two applications, four metres wide, you get sixteen point four. With four, twenty-one point eight. Each chord cuts straight across the curve, so narrower strips hug the edge more closely, and the slivers we're getting wrong get **smaller**. The syllabus asks for up to four applications, and four strips usually give a good **estimate**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 2): the pond with the application slider beneath it. On "just one application" the slider sets 1: one chord along the baseline from end to end; the readout "A ≈ 8/2 × (0 + 0) = 0 m²", hand tag "one strip: zero". On "two applications" the slider sets 2: chords from 0 to 4.1 to 0, readout "A ≈ 2 × 4.1 + 2 × 4.1 = 16.4 m²", large violet slivers showing what's missed. On "With four" the slider sets 4: readout "21.8 m²", slivers much thinner. On "smaller" hand tag "more strips, closer". On "estimate" a readout row "1: 0 · 2: 16.4 · 4: 21.8 m²".

### 5. volume: how much water?
> Now the water. If the pond is about nought point six of a metre deep all over, its volume is its area times its depth, just like a prism. Twenty-one point eight times nought point six is about thirteen point nought eight cubic metres. Every cubic metre is a kilolitre, so the pond holds about thirteen point nought eight kilolitres, a bit over thirteen thousand **litres**. That's the area rule doing a volume **job**. If the depth changed a lot from place to place, one depth wouldn't do. You'd measure a cross-section instead, and that's exactly what the next example does.

Visual: stage change: the pond plate tilts to a coded cutaway with an olive water level "0.6 m deep" (blue); grey chips "a09: V = Ah", "a03: 1 m³ = 1 kL". Rows: "V ≈ 21.8 × 0.6 = 13.08 m³"; on "litres" "= 13.08 kL ≈ 13 080 L" (framed green), with counters m³, kL, L rising. On "job" hand tag "area × depth".

### 6. capacity: a drainage channel
> Here's one where the shape changes across, but stays the same along. A concrete drainage channel is one point two metres across, and its depth is measured every nought point three of a metre: nought, nought point two five, nought point three two, nought point two six, and nought. Four strips, so four applications, each with h over two equal to nought point one five. The four areas add to nought point two four nine square metres. That's the cross-section. The channel runs fifty metres, so when it's full it holds nought point two four nine times fifty, about twelve point four five cubic metres, or twelve point four five **kilolitres** of water. Area, volume and capacity, all from the same **rule**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a trapezium: the edge is treated as straight between two widths (2). Why (h/2)(d_f + d_l): it's the average width times the gap (2). Why "≈": the real edge isn't straight (2). Why several applications: one trapezium misses the curve (3, 4). Why more strips are closer: chords hug the curve (4). Why area × length gives a volume: the cross-section stays the same (5, 6).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** h not halved (3, A1, A2 crossed); h as the whole length (3); "=" for "≈" (2); m² to ha (A1); m³ to kL (5, 6).
