## Decisions

- **Anchor: the two buses (d12's frame).** Same mean, different reliability. The range tells them apart (2 against 8), then a breakdown day shows the range's weakness, which motivates σ.
- **σ by meaning, then by calculator; no formula.** The point says "using a scientific calculator or other digital tools". σ is shown as deviation arms from each dot to the mean, combined into one "typical distance" (said as "roughly the typical distance of the values from the mean"; Year 12 z01 uses σ "only as how spread out"). The squaring and square root are not shown (site card 07 shows them; the site's own note says they are not assessed).
- **The calculator's statistics mode, named again (Year 12 b05's recall).** "The calculator's statistics mode" is said in those words: the same one-variable mode as d11, now reading the σx line. sx (the sample standard deviation) is on the same screen and is a different number; this course uses σ, the population standard deviation, so read σx (the crossed slip in scene 5 and A1). Spreadsheet: =STDEV.P(B2:B21) for σ (STDEV.S is the sample version, not used), and =MAX(B2:B21)−MIN(B2:B21) for the range.
- **Plain meanings at first use.** Measure of spread ("one number that says how spread out the values are"); range ("highest value minus lowest value"); deviation ("a value's distance from the mean"); standard deviation, σ, said "sigma" ("roughly the typical distance of the values from the mean; bigger means more spread out"); consistent ("values close together, so a small spread").

### 1. hook: same mean, same bus?
> Both buses average four minutes late. But Bus A is three, four or five minutes late every day, while Bus B is sometimes on time, and sometimes eight minutes late. If you had an exam at nine, you'd want Bus A. The mean can't tell them apart, because it only measures the centre. We need a second kind of number, a measure of spread, one number that says how spread out the values are. So how do the range and standard deviation measure **spread**?

### 2. the range
> The simplest measure of spread is the range. It's the highest value minus the lowest value. Bus A's latest day was five minutes, and its earliest was three, so its range is five minus three, two **minutes**. Bus B's range is eight minus nought, eight minutes. A bigger range means more spread, so Bus B is less consistent. The range is quick, and it's easy to explain, but notice what it uses. Only two values, the highest and the lowest. Everything in between is ignored. Its units are the data's units, here minutes.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): the two dot plots enlarge (about 1,200 px wide). On "highest value minus the lowest value" chip "range = highest − lowest" (hand tag "highest − lowest"). ### 3. the range's weakness
> That's the range's weakness. Say Bus A breaks down one day and arrives fifteen minutes late. The other five days are exactly the same, three to five minutes late. But the range jumps from two to twelve, making Bus A look less consistent than Bus B. One unusual day changed the whole measure. We need a measure of spread that listens to every value, not just the two most extreme ones.

Visual: Bus A's dot plot widens to 0 to 16; on "breaks down" Bus A's dot at the right (one of the 4s) slides out to 15 (a small coded breakdown icon above it); the amber bracket stretches from 3 to 15; row "range = 15 − 3 = 12 min" (amber, 52 px) and a ghost "was 2" beside it (hand tag "one bad day"). On "every value" the 15 slides back to 4 and the bracket returns to 2.

### 4. the standard deviation
> The standard deviation does that. Measure each value's distance from the mean. That's called its deviation. Bus A's days are all close to four, so its deviations are tiny, one, nought, nought, nought, one, nought. Bus B's are big, four, three, two, two, three, four. The standard deviation combines all those distances into one number, roughly the typical distance of the values from the mean. It's written with the Greek letter sigma. For Bus A, sigma is about nought point five eight minutes. For Bus B, about three point one one **minutes**. A bigger sigma means the values usually sit further from the mean, so the data are more spread out, and less consistent. Every value counts, so one odd day can't take over the way it does with the range. Sigma has the same units as the data, minutes here.

Visual: stage change (simulation 2): deviation arms (`DeviationArms`): each dot gets an amber arm to its plot's teal triangle with its length labelled (40 px). ### 5. σ on the calculator's statistics mode
> You won't calculate sigma by hand. You'll use the calculator's statistics mode, the same one-variable mode we used for the mean. Type in Bus B's six values. The results screen shows x bar, four, and below it, sigma x, three point one nought nine, which is three point one one to two decimal **places**. Watch out. Most calculators show a second standard deviation just below it, s x, three point four one. That's a sample version, and this course doesn't use it. Read sigma x. Try our sleep data, all twenty values. Sigma x is one point three nine one, so the standard deviation is one point three nine **hours**. Writing three point four one for Bus B, the s x line, is the classic **slip**.

### 6. what σ tells you
> Always say what sigma means, in context, with its units. Bus A's sigma, nought point five eight minutes, says it's usually within about half a minute of its mean. Bus B's, three point one one minutes, says it's often three minutes or more away from its mean. Our sleep data's sigma, one point three nine hours, says students' sleep typically differs from the mean of seven point two hours by about an hour and a half. A spreadsheet gives the same numbers. Equals STDEV dot P of the column gives sigma, and equals MAX minus MIN gives the **range**. Spreadsheets also have a STDEV dot S, the sample version again, so choose the dot P.
