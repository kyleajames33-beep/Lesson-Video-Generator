## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "Why does atomic radius change across a period and down a group?" It is already causal. The answer, in order: radius is a measured distance (2); two effects act on outer electrons (3); across a period the shells stay the same while the pull grows (4, 5); down a group each step adds a shell (7); ions change the electrons around the same nucleus (9, 10).
- **Opening frame (reconciled by root, 5 November).** v13 opens on v12's actual final frame: period 3 row and Li to K group 1 column outlined on `PeriodicGrid`, Na, Cl and K shell diagrams in dashed "radius?" circles. Scene 1 fades the circles and keeps K for the down-group beat.
- **Surprise first (site hook).** "Cram more protons and more electrons into an atom and it gets smaller." Chlorine has six more protons and six more electrons than sodium, yet its radius is 102 pm against sodium's 166 pm. The video then explains it.
- **Real data, not invented values (author note a).** Atomic radii are Cordero et al. (2008) covalent radii, the measure the site describes ("roughly half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms"). Ionic radii are Shannon (1976) six-coordinate values. On screen: Period 3 Na 166, Mg 141, Al 121, Si 111, P 107, S 105, Cl 102 pm; Group 1 Li 128, Na 166, K 203, Rb 220, Cs 244 pm; ions Na⁺ 102, Cl⁻ 181, O²⁻ 140, F⁻ 133, Mg²⁺ 72, Al³⁺ 54 pm. A grey Sources tag sits under each bar chart. Argon is left off the period chart (site complication 1: noble-gas radii are measured on a different basis), with a grey tag saying so.
- **Core charge as a counting estimate (author note b).** The site uses effective nuclear charge (Z_eff) qualitatively. Year 11 students need a number they can work out, so the video uses core charge: protons minus inner-shell electrons (sodium 11 − 10 = +1; chlorine 17 − 10 = +7; lithium 3 − 2 = +1; potassium 19 − 18 = +1). It is introduced as "a simple estimate of the pull an outer electron feels" and the term "effective nuclear charge" is named once as the exam phrase for the same idea. Assumptions checked in "Memory aid check".
- **Shielding in plain words.** "Inner electrons sit between the nucleus and the outer electrons, and their repulsion cancels part of the nucleus's pull." Electrons in the same shell shield each other only a little (site misconception card), which is the cause scene 5 uses.
- **Down a group (site).** Each step down Group 1 adds an occupied shell; core charge stays +1; the outer electron is further out and the extra shell outweighs the extra protons. The site's "more shielded" is kept but the main cause spoken is distance (extra shell), matching the site's fast rule ("extra occupied shells win").
- **Ions without "electron count only" (route stop).** Na → Na⁺: the whole third shell is lost (2,8,1 → 2,8), so the outer electrons are now in the second shell (site trap: "not one tiny dot"). Cl → Cl⁻: one extra electron in the same shell, same 17 protons, more repulsion, the cloud spreads. Isoelectronic set (site drill 5): five species with 10 electrons each shrink as proton number rises, so the same electron count gives different sizes. The ionic and atomic radii come from different measurements, so comparisons are of direction and rough size only (author note c).
- **In-teaching prediction (3 s, scenes 6 and 7).** Lithium or potassium: which atom is larger? (Site quick check 1.)
- **Memory aid:** "Across: same shells, stronger pull. Down: one more shell each step." (Site's fast rule, reworded so each half names its cause.) Checked in "Memory aid check".
- **Attempts (original wording, HSC style).** A1: magnesium 141 pm and sulfur 105 pm; explain why sulfur atoms are smaller even though they have more electrons. Crossed: "sulfur has fewer shells". A2: oxygen atom 66 pm; predict and explain the size of O²⁻. Answer: larger (140 pm). Crossed: "O²⁻ has an extra shell".
- **Mistake scene:** none separate. "More electrons make a bigger atom" (5, from the site's misconception card); "Na⁺ is Na minus one tiny dot" (9); "electron count alone sets ion size" (10); "sulfur has fewer shells" (crossed in 13); "an anion gains a shell" (crossed in 15).

And go down a column, from lithium to potassium, and atoms get **larger**. So why does atomic radius change across a period and down a **group**?

### 2. what an atomic radius measures
> An atom has no hard edge, so chemists measure the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms bonded together, and take **half**. That's the atomic radius. It's tiny, so we use picometres. A picometre is a millionth of a millionth of a **metre**. Every value we compare today is measured the same way, so the comparison is fair.

Visual: board clears to two chlorine atoms (soft electron clouds, overlapping slightly, nuclei as red dots). On "metre" a small unit strip writes: "1 pm = 0.000 000 000 001 m", grey tag "covalent radius; Sources: Cordero et al., 2008".

### 3. two effects on an outer electron
> Two things decide how close the outer electrons sit. The first is the nucleus. Its positive protons attract the negative electrons, and more protons pull harder. The second is shielding. Inner electrons sit between the nucleus and the outer electrons, and their repulsion cancels part of the nucleus's **pull**. A simple way to estimate the pull an outer electron feels is the core charge: the number of protons, minus the number of inner **electrons**. For sodium, that's eleven minus ten, so plus **one**. Exam answers call this net pull the effective nuclear **charge**.

Visual: board clears to a large sodium shell model (11 red protons in the nucleus labelled "11+", shells 2, 8, 1). ### 4. across a period: same shells, stronger pull
> Now go across Period three, from sodium to chlorine. Each step adds one proton to the nucleus. The new electron goes into the same third shell, so the inner shells don't change, and there are still ten inner **electrons**. So the core charge climbs, from plus one for sodium to plus seven for **chlorine**. A stronger pull on the same shell draws the outer electrons closer, and the atom gets **smaller**.

Visual: the sodium model moves left; a strip of seven small shell atoms writes across: Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, each with three shells. ### 7. down a group: one more shell each step
> Potassium. Going down Group one, each element has one more occupied shell than the one above, so its outer electron sits further from the **nucleus**. Yes, potassium has more protons, but it also has more inner electrons shielding them. Its core charge is nineteen minus eighteen, plus one, the same as lithium's. With the same core charge, the extra shell **wins**. So atomic radius increases down a **group**.

### 8. saying it in an exam
> In an exam, don't just say the atom gets smaller. Name the cause. Across a period, the number of protons increases while electrons go into the same shell, so the stronger effective nuclear charge pulls the outer electrons **closer**. Down a group, each element has an extra occupied shell, so the outer electrons are further from the **nucleus**.

### 9. atoms into ions
> Atoms can also gain or lose electrons to form ions. When sodium loses its one outer electron, it doesn't just lose a dot. It loses the whole third shell. Its outer electrons are now in the second shell, and the sodium ion is much **smaller**. Chlorine goes the other way. It gains one electron into its outer shell, but the nucleus still has seventeen protons. The extra electron adds repulsion between the outer electrons, so they spread out, and the chloride ion is **larger**.

### 10. same electrons, different sizes
> So is it all about how many electrons there are? Look at five ions that each have exactly ten electrons: oxide, fluoride, sodium, magnesium and aluminium **ions**. They're called isoelectronic, which means same number of electrons. Their sizes still fall, because the number of protons rises, from eight up to thirteen. More protons pull the same ten electrons in **tighter**. So electron count alone never tells you the size. Protons and shells matter too.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Atomic radius: half the distance between the nuclei of two identical bonded atoms of an element (in pm; 1 pm = 10⁻¹² m).
2. Across a period, radius decreases: protons increase, electrons enter the same shell, shielding barely changes, so the effective nuclear charge (core charge = protons − inner electrons) rises and pulls the outer electrons closer.
3. Down a group, radius increases: each element has an extra occupied shell, so the outer electrons are further from the nucleus (core charge stays the same).
4. Cations are smaller than their atoms (often a whole shell is lost); anions are larger (same protons, more electron repulsion). For isoelectronic ions, more protons means a smaller ion.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Across: same shells, stronger pull. Down: one more shell each step.*

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 12; original wording, HSC style).** "The atomic radius of magnesium is 141 pm and the atomic radius of sulfur is 105 pm. Explain why sulfur atoms are smaller than magnesium atoms, even though they contain more electrons. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Magnesium and sulfur are both in Period 3, so their outer electrons occupy the same (third) shell (1). Sulfur has 16 protons and magnesium 12, with 10 inner electrons in each, so sulfur's outer electrons feel a greater effective nuclear charge (core charge +6 against +2); the added electrons are in the same shell and shield each other little (1). The stronger attraction pulls sulfur's outer electrons closer to the nucleus, so its radius is smaller (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "sulfur has fewer shells".

**Attempt 2 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style).** "An oxygen atom has an atomic radius of 66 pm. Predict whether the oxide ion, O²⁻, is larger or smaller than the oxygen atom. Explain your prediction. (2 marks)"
Model answer: Larger (O²⁻ is about 140 pm) (1). The ion has the same 8 protons but 2 more electrons in the same outer shell (2,6 → 2,8); the extra electron repulsion, with the same nuclear attraction, makes the electron cloud spread out (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "O²⁻ has an extra shell".

## Memory aid check

- **"Across: same shells, stronger pull. Down: one more shell each step."** Adapted from the site's fast rule. Meaning, as said in scene 11: across a period the number of occupied shells is fixed while core charge rises, so outer electrons are pulled in; down a group each step adds an occupied shell at the same core charge, so outer electrons are further out. Assumptions checked: applies to main-group elements (Groups 1, 2 and 13 to 17); fails across the d-block (tag in scene 4); "stronger pull" is the core-charge estimate, which assumes complete shielding by inner shells and none within the outer shell (author note b), true in direction for Period 2 and 3; noble gases are excluded because their radii are measured differently. It helps a new case: any two main-group atoms in the same period or group, and A1 directly. Introduced after both causes are taught.
- **Considered, not used:** "smaller to the right, bigger going down" (a direction without a cause, which the site's exam card warns against); a magnet analogy (implies a single attraction and hides shielding).
