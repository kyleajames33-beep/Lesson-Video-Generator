- **Learner question kept (route).** "When is a continuous join differentiable?" The answer, built in order: two joins can both be unbroken, yet only one straightens when you zoom in (hook); differentiable at a means the secant gradient settles on one finite value from both sides, giving one non-vertical tangent (2); at the smooth join both sides settle on 2 (3); at the corner the sides settle on 2 and 1, so there is no derivative (4); if the pieces don't meet, the rise stays at least 1 while the run shrinks, so the gradient blows up: a jump is never differentiable, which is why differentiable implies continuous (5); a smooth-looking curve can still fail if its tangent is vertical (6); so at a join, check that the pieces meet, then that their gradients match (7).

- **Author notes (not learner copy):**

### 2. what differentiable means
> Here's the test. Take the point P, at x equals a, and a nearby point Q, a small step h along. The gradient of the line through them is f of a plus h, minus f of a, all over h. You met that in Year 11. Now shrink h towards zero, from the right, and also from the **left**, because h can be negative. If those gradients settle on one finite number, from both sides, f is differentiable at a. That number is f prime of a, and the curve has a single tangent there that isn't **vertical**. If they don't settle, there's no derivative at a.

### 3. the smooth join

### 4. the corner
> Now the second join, where the right piece is y equals x. From the left, nothing changes. The gradients still head to **two**. But from the right, every secant gradient is exactly **one**. Two from one side, one from the other. There's no single number, so there's no derivative at x equals one. The curve is unbroken, but it isn't differentiable there. That's what a corner is: two different one-sided **gradients**.

### 5. a join that doesn't meet
> What if the gradients match, but the pieces don't meet? Here, x squared runs up to one, one. Then the right piece is y equals two x, which starts at height **two**. Its gradient is two, the same as x squared at the join. A function is continuous at a when, from both sides, the curve heads to the value f of a itself. Here, from the right it heads to two, but f of one is one. So it isn't continuous. Now look at the secant from P, to a point on the right piece. The rise never drops below **one**, while the run shrinks to zero. So the gradient blows up, to twelve, then a hundred and two, and **beyond**. No derivative. This is why a function that's differentiable at a point must be continuous there. If the gradients settle on a finite number, the rise is that gradient times the run. So the rise shrinks to zero with the run, and the curve can't **jump**.

### 6. a vertical tangent

So there's no derivative there **either**.

### 7. the method at a join
> So at a join, check two things, in order. First, do the pieces meet? Compare the value at the join with the height the other piece heads to. If they don't meet, stop. It's not differentiable. Different gradients mean a **corner**. Continuity on its own isn't enough.

### 15. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. f is differentiable at x = a if lim_{h→0} (f(a + h) − f(a))/h exists: the secant gradients settle on one finite value from both sides. Then the curve has a single non-vertical tangent at P(a, f(a)).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 10; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "The function g is defined by g(x) = 3 − x² for x ≤ 1 and g(x) = x + 1 for x > 1. (a) Determine whether g is continuous at x = 1. (b) Determine whether g is differentiable at x = 1. Justify your answers."
Model answer: (a) g(1) = 3 − 1² = 2 (x = 1 is in the first piece). As x → 1 from the right, x + 1 → 2. The values agree, so g is continuous at x = 1. **Attempt 2 (scene 12; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "The function f is defined by f(x) = e^(2x) for x ≤ 0 and f(x) = ax + b for x > 0, where a and b are constants. Find the values of a and b for which f is differentiable at x = 0."
