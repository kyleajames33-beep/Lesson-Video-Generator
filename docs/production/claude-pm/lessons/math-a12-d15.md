- **Author notes (not learner copy):**
  (a) Differentiable implies continuous: if f′(a) exists, f(a + h) − f(a) = [(f(a + h) − f(a))/h] × h → f′(a) × 0 = 0. The narration says "the rise is that gradient times the run", where "that gradient" means the secant gradient, which is close to the settled value for small h. The statement is exact as a limit statement; no epsilon language.
  (b) Comparing piece derivatives at the join is valid when f is continuous at a and each piece is differentiable on an open interval reaching a from its side and extends differentiably to a (true for polynomials and exponentials): then the one-sided difference quotients equal the pieces' one-sided derivatives (mean value theorem; not named). Without continuity it fails: the jump example has piece gradients 2 and 2, but the right difference quotient from P(1, 1) is (2(1 + h) − 1)/h = 2 + 1/h → +∞. Hence "meet, then match" in that order.
  (c) One-sided quotients at x = 1: smooth join left ((1 + h)² − 1)/h = 2 + h, right (2(1 + h) − 1 − 1)/h = 2; corner right ((1 + h) − 1)/h = 1; jump right 2 + 1/h (4, 12, 102 at h = 0.5, 0.1, 0.01). The rise on the jump is 1 + 2h > 1.
  (d) Cube root: (h^(1/3) − 0)/h = h^(−2/3) > 0 for h ≠ 0 (both sides), 4.642, 21.544, 100.0 at h = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001; derivative (1/3)x^(−2/3) undefined at 0. ### 2. what differentiable means
> Here's the test. Take the point P, at x equals a, and a nearby point Q, a small step h along. The gradient of the line through them is f of a plus h, minus f of a, all over h. You met that in Year 11. Now shrink h towards zero, from the right, and also from the **left**, because h can be negative. If those gradients settle on one finite number, from both sides, f is differentiable at a. That number is f prime of a, and the curve has a single tangent there that isn't **vertical**. If they don't settle, there's no derivative at a.

### 5. a join that doesn't meet
> What if the gradients match, but the pieces don't meet? Here, x squared runs up to one, one. Then the right piece is y equals two x, which starts at height **two**. On a track, that's a **step**. Here, from the right it heads to two, but f of one is one. So it isn't continuous. Now look at the secant from P, to a point on the right piece. ### 7. the method at a join
> So at a join, check two things, in order. First, do the pieces meet? Compare the value at the join with the height the other piece heads to. If they don't meet, stop. It's not differentiable. Second, do the gradients match? Each piece here is smooth on its own side, so differentiate each one, and evaluate both at the join. Equal gradients mean differentiable. Different gradients mean a **corner**. Continuity on its own isn't enough.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why an unbroken join can lack a tangent: the curve's direction can change suddenly (hook zoom, 4). Why the definition needs both sides: Q can approach from either side, and the limit must be the same (2). Why the smooth join is differentiable: the line is x²'s tangent, so both sides settle on 2 (3). Why a jump is never differentiable: the rise stays at least 1 while the run shrinks, so the gradient blows up; equivalently, a finite gradient times a shrinking run is a shrinking rise (5). Why the cube root fails: its tangent is vertical, so the gradient grows without limit (6). Why "meet" comes first: matching piece gradients can hide a jump (5, 7). ## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 10; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "The function g is defined by g(x) = 3 − x² for x ≤ 1 and g(x) = x + 1 for x > 1. (a) Determine whether g is continuous at x = 1. (b) Determine whether g is differentiable at x = 1. Justify your answers."
Model answer: (a) g(1) = 3 − 1² = 2 (x = 1 is in the first piece). As x → 1 from the right, x + 1 → 2. The values agree, so g is continuous at x = 1. (b) The left gradient is −2x = −2 at x = 1; the right gradient is 1. Since −2 ≠ 1, g is not differentiable at x = 1 (a corner at (1, 2)). Classic mistake (crossed): "continuous, so differentiable".

**Attempt 2 (scene 12; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "The function f is defined by f(x) = e^(2x) for x ≤ 0 and f(x) = ax + b for x > 0, where a and b are constants. Find the values of a and b for which f is differentiable at x = 0."
Model answer: Gradients match: d/dx e^(2x) = 2e^(2x) = 2 at x = 0, and the line has gradient a, so a = 2. Values meet: f(0) = e⁰ = 1, and the line approaches a(0) + b = b, so b = 1. Hence a = 2, b = 1 (the line y = 2x + 1). Classic mistake (crossed): "a = 2, b = 0".

## Memory aid check

- **"Meet, then match."** Written for this lesson.
