Main question: *Every one of these changes is a single base, so why can one keep the same amino acid, one swap an amino acid, and one stop the protein?*
Entry knowledge: see above. Students start Year 12 and have watched B6b, B6c, B7, b01 and b02. They know that codons are read in threes from AUG, that most amino acids have several codons, the stop codons, how to read the chart with mRNA codons, the sickle cell chain from B7, and that a mutation is a change in base sequence. They have not met the names of point mutation types, the reading frame or frameshifts.
Stops after: defining a point mutation and its three processes (substitution, insertion, deletion); explaining that a substitution within a coding codon changes that codon, which may give the same amino acid (silent, because the code is degenerate), a different amino acid (missense, sickle cell) or a stop codon (nonsense, beta-globin codon 39, chain cut short); explaining that an insertion or deletion of one base regroups every codon after it (frameshift), so most later amino acids change and an early stop often appears; explaining that adding or removing a multiple of three bases keeps the frame and adds or removes amino acids; explaining that the effect depends on type and position (near the start of a gene, in an active site), so "point" describes the scale of the change, not the size of the effect; applying this to an unseen classification of three mutant sequences with a chart excerpt, and to the cystic fibrosis three-base deletion against the Tay-Sachs four-base insertion.
Handoff: chromosomal mutations, changes to whole segments or numbers of chromosomes (route entry bio-m6-b04).

### 1. hook (three changes, one gene)
> In the gene for one chain of haemoglobin, change one base in the sixth codon, and you can get sickle cell disease. Change a different base in that same codon, and the amino acid stays the same. Change one base in codon thirty-nine, and the chain stops partway, causing a blood disorder called beta thalassaemia. Every one of these is a single base. So why are the results so different?

Visual: the reading board draws in, labelled once at the far left ("gene (DNA)", "mRNA", "polypeptide"): a `SlimStrand` with a gene band tagged "beta-globin gene"; above it a short stretch of mRNA codons around codon 6 ("... CCU GAG GAG ...", position numbers small under the brackets, "5" "6" "7"; traditional numbering from the first amino acid of the finished chain, as B7 and HSC papers use) and a bead row (computed: Pro, Glu, Glu). On "sixth codon" codon 6's bracket glows; its middle letter turns amber and swaps "A to U" in the mRNA (DNA callout "A to T"); its bead flips to "Val" with an amber ring, and the painted `m6-red-cells-normal-sickle-v1.png` slides in small at the right, cropped to the sickle half (tag "sickle cell disease"). ### 2. point mutation: three ways to change a base
> Each of these is a **point mutation**, a change to one base, or a few bases, in a gene's DNA. There are three ways it can happen. A **substitution** swaps one base for another. An **insertion** adds a base, and a **deletion** removes one. They come from copying mistakes, or from mutagens, like the base analogues and the chemicals that slip between bases, from the last video. Remember, a ribosome reads the mRNA copy of the gene in codons of three, starting at the start codon. So the real question is, what does the change do to the codons?

Visual: the board returns to a short neutral message (mRNA "AUG AAA CCU GGA UUU", DNA strand beneath, beads hidden). On "point mutation" the term writes on with the gloss "a change to one or a few bases". Three short demonstrations play on the DNA strand, one per cue, each returning to normal before the next: on "swaps" one rung turns amber with a `BaseSwap` callout (tag "substitution"); on "adds" the strand opens a gap and one amber rung slides in, the strand lengthening by one pitch (tag "insertion"); on "removes" one rung lifts out and the strand closes up (tag "deletion"). Each tag fades as the next lands, so one tag shows at a time; after "deletion" the three names sit in a small column at the left. On "copying mistakes, or from mutagens" a grey tag writes on beneath: "b02: base analogues, chemicals between bases"; it fades on "Remember", and the term and gloss fade with it. On "codons of three" the painted ribosome (flipped, small) settles on the mRNA row at AUG with the amber `FrameWindow` under the first codon, then steps one codon right (no tag). On "what does the change do to the codons?" each codon bracket pulses once. Hold one second.

### 3. silent: same amino acid
> Start with substitutions. A substitution inside a coding codon changes that codon, so read the new codon to see what it codes for: the same amino acid, a different one, or a stop. Most amino acids have more than one codon, often differing only in the third base. So G A G becoming G A A still codes for glutamate. That's a **silent** mutation. The base sequence changed, but the amino acid sequence didn't.

Visual: the beta-globin board returns at codon 6 (mRNA "GAG", bead "Glu"); the row labels fade out. On "changes that codon" only codon 6's bracket stays bright; the others dim slightly. On "more than one codon" B6c's chart excerpt card slides in at the right with two rows, "GAA Glu" and "GAG Glu" (computed), the third letters amber (tag "third base often doesn't matter"). On "G A G becoming G A A" the last letter swaps amber "G to A" in the mRNA (DNA callout "G to A"); the pointer lights "GAA Glu"; the bead stays "Glu" and takes a small tick. On "silent" the term writes on with the gloss "same amino acid". On "didn't" a two-line comparison writes on under the board: "bases: changed", "amino acids: same". The chart card leaves. Hold one second.

### 4. missense: one amino acid swapped
> Now change the middle base instead, so G A G becomes G U G. The chart gives valine, not glutamate. Glutamate carries a charge, but valine avoids water. One amino acid is swapped for another, and that's a **missense** mutation. You've seen where this one leads. With two copies, the changed haemoglobin sticks together when oxygen is low, and red cells bend into sickles. But not every missense change matters this much. It depends on where the amino acid sits, and how different the new one is.

Visual: codon 6 resets to "GAG". On "the middle base" the middle letter swaps amber "A to U" (DNA callout "A to T"); the chart card returns with "GAG Glu" and "GUG Val" (computed); on "valine" the pointer lights "GUG Val" and the bead flips from "Glu" to "Val" with an amber ring. The chart card leaves on "valine". On "carries a charge" the Glu bead (ghosted beside it) shows a small "charged" label and the Val bead a pale grey "avoids water" label; both labels fade on "missense", when the term writes on with the gloss "a different amino acid". On "You've seen where this one leads" the sickle half of the hook's `m6-red-cells-normal-sickle-v1.png` returns small at the top right (no B7 strip, no grey tag); on "two copies" a tag "two copies" writes on above it. On "not every missense change matters this much" the plate, its tag and the term fade, and two small cases play under the board: a folded protein blob with an amber pocket where a changed bead sits (tag "in the active site: likely to matter"), and the same blob with a changed bead far from the pocket, swapped for a similar one (tag "similar amino acid, away from the site: may not"). Hold one second.

### 5. nonsense: an early stop
> A third kind of substitution creates an early stop. In codon thirty-nine, C A G codes for glutamine. Change the C to U, and it reads U A G, a stop codon, U Are Gone. The ribosome stops there, so the chain is cut short after thirty-eight amino acids, and it can't fold into a working beta chain. That's a **nonsense** mutation. A person with two copies makes no normal beta chains, so they have too little working haemoglobin.

Visual: the board slides along the gene band to codons 37 to 41 (mRNA "UGG ACC CAG AGG UUC", computed Trp, Thr, Gln, Arg, Phe; small position numbers). On "C A G codes for glutamine" codon 39 glows with its "Gln" bead. On "Change the C to U" its first letter swaps amber "C to U" (DNA callout "C to T"); the chart card slides in with "CAG Gln" and "UAG stop" (computed; the stop row grey); on "a stop codon" the pointer lights "UAG stop" and a small handwritten "U Are Gone" writes on inside that chart row (no grey tag). On "The ribosome stops there" the chart card leaves; the riding ribosome reaches codon 39 and halts; the bead chain ends at codon 38 and the beads after it fade to dashed outlines (tag "38 amino acids, then stop"). On "can't fold" the short chain curls into a small loose blob beside a full folded beta chain outline, and the tag changes to "too short to fold". On "nonsense" that tag fades and the term writes on with the gloss "a stop codon, chain cut short". On "two copies" B7's haemoglobin blob shows two alpha lobes and two dashed, empty beta lobes (one tag "two copies: no normal beta chains"). Hold one second.

### 6. frameshift: one base lost
> Insertions and deletions act very differently. Here's a short message that codes for five amino acids. Now delete one base from the second codon. The ribosome still reads in threes from the start codon, so every codon from there on is different, and the amino acids after it are mostly wrong. A stop codon often turns up early, too. That's a **frameshift**, because the reading frame, the way the bases are grouped into codons, has shifted. Inserting one base does the same thing.

Visual: the board shows the site's message as mRNA: "5' AUG AAA CCU GGA UUU 3'" with brackets, the DNA strand beneath and five beads above (computed Met, Lys, Pro, Gly, Phe; tag "normal"). On "delete one base" one "A" of the second codon (its last letter) lifts away amber and its rung leaves the DNA strand; the letters to its right slide left by one place. On "reads in threes from the start codon" the ribosome starts at AUG and steps along with the `FrameWindow`; the brackets regroup one codon at a time under the window into "AUG AAC CUG GAU UU" (computed). On "every codon from there on is different" each regrouped codon flashes amber as the window reaches it; the beads after Met flip one by one to "Asn", "Leu", "Asp" (computed), each with an amber ring, and the original five-bead row stays above in grey for comparison. ### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Point mutation: a change to one or a few bases in DNA, by substitution (one base swapped), insertion (bases added) or deletion (bases removed).
- A substitution within a coding codon changes that codon; read the new codon. Silent: the same amino acid (the code is degenerate). Missense: a different amino acid (sickle cell, GAG to GUG). Nonsense: a stop codon, so the chain is cut short (beta thalassaemia, CAG to UAG).
- An insertion or deletion that is not a multiple of three causes a frameshift: every codon after it is regrouped, most later amino acids change and an early stop often appears. A multiple of three keeps the frame and adds or removes amino acids.
- The effect depends on type and position (near the start of a gene, or in an active site, matters more). "Point" describes the scale of the change, not the size of the effect.

Memory: Silent same, missense misses, nonsense stops. THE FAT CAT: lose one letter, everything after it shifts.
