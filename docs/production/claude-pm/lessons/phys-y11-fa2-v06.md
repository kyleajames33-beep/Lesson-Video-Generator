### 1. hook (period or wavelength?)
> Last time, we found that v equals f lambda needs a wavelength and a frequency. Now watch one point on the cord, and plot its height as time goes by. The graph comes out wave-shaped, peak after peak. So here's the question. On this graph, is the gap between two peaks the wave's wavelength, or its **period**?

Visual: opens on v05's last frame (the 2.0 Hz cord with the purple point at 2.00 m, the dashed line to the time axis, the purple trace about one and a quarter cycles long; chips "period?", "wavelength?"; the "Next" chip). The chip becomes the heading: "Displacement-time graphs". The trace grows to two full cycles; two peak markers appear with a dashed span between them. On "period" the chips become "wavelength?" and "period?" beside the span, and a 3-second countdown ring.

### 2. one point, moment by moment
> Let's see how the graph is made. The marked point only moves up and down. At every instant, we plot its displacement, how far it is from its rest position, and which way. Above rest is positive. Below rest is negative. And along the bottom, the axis is **time**. So this graph is the story of one point over time. It isn't a photo of the cord. The cord's shape is spread out along distance. This graph is spread out along time, even though the two curves look **alike**.

Visual: the trace clears and restarts slowly (tag "slowed down"): each moment the point's height is carried by the dashed line and lands as a purple dot at the right end of the scrolling axis. Vertical axis "displacement y (cm)" with "+ above rest" and "− below rest"; the grey dashed zero line labelled "rest position" (gloss "displacement: how far from rest, and which way"). On "time" the horizontal axis label "time t (s)" glows. On "photo" a frozen copy of the cord (tag "one instant") slides in above the trace, its own axis "position along the cord (m)"; on "alike" the two axis labels pulse in turn, "metres" and "seconds" ringed.

### 3. reading the period and amplitude
> So the gap between two peaks is a time. It's the time for the point to go up, down and back up again, one full oscillation. That's the **period**. Here the peaks are at zero point one two five seconds and zero point six two five seconds, so the period is zero point five oh seconds. That matches the driver, at two point oh hertz. Any matching pair of points works, like two upward crossings of zero. But a peak to the next trough is only half a period. The amplitude is the height of a peak above the zero line, three point oh **centimetres**.

Visual: the photo panel fades. The "period?" chip fills. On "period" a purple bracket on the time axis between the two peaks, "T"; pointers "0.125 s" and "0.625 s"; row "T = 0.625 − 0.125 = 0.50 s"; the driver's readout "2.0 Hz" and a grey row "f = 1/T = 2.0 Hz". On "upward crossings" the bracket slides to two upward zero crossings (0 s and 0.50 s), the same length. On "half a period" a grey span peak to trough "T ÷ 2 = 0.25 s"; a chip "peak to trough = T" fades in and is crossed. On "centimetres" a teal bracket from the zero line up to a peak, "A = 3.0 cm" (tag "trace drawn to its own scale").

### 4. worked example: reading a graph
> Let's read one. Task: a displacement-time graph shows a particle reaching peaks of plus four centimetres, with one peak at zero point one oh seconds and the next at zero point three oh seconds. Find the amplitude, the period and the frequency. The amplitude is the peak height, four centimetres, or zero point zero four **metres**. The period is the time between the peaks, zero point three oh minus zero point one oh, zero point two oh **seconds**. And f is one over T, one over zero point two oh, five point oh **hertz**.

### 5. prediction: where is it fastest?
> Now look closer at the curve. The point keeps changing speed. When is it moving fastest: at a peak, or as it passes through **zero**?

### 6. the slope shows the motion
> From Focus Area 1, the gradient of a displacement-time graph is the velocity. At a peak, the graph is flat for an instant, so the point has stopped, just for a moment, as it turns around. As it passes through zero, the graph is at its steepest, so the point is moving fastest, right through its rest position. And the slope's direction tells you which way it's going. Where the graph is rising, the point is moving **up**. Where it's falling, the point is moving **down**.

Visual: the "through zero?" chip fills. A short dark grey tangent segment rides along the trace (gloss "gradient = velocity (FA1)"); a velocity arrow on the cord point grows and shrinks in step (tag "the point's velocity, not the wave's speed"). On "stopped" the run freezes at a peak: the tangent flat, the arrow gone, chip "v = 0, turning". On "steepest" the run freezes at a zero crossing: the tangent steepest, the arrow longest, chip "fastest". On "up" a rising section highlights with the arrow pointing up; on "down" a falling section with the arrow pointing down.

### 7. a longitudinal wave gives the same graph
> What about a longitudinal wave? Follow one coil on a spring. It moves back and forth along the spring, not up and down. But we can still plot its displacement against time. Take forward, the way the wave travels, as positive, and backward as negative. The graph is the same smooth curve, read in exactly the same way: the period between matching peaks, and the amplitude from the zero line. So a wave-shaped graph doesn't mean the particles move sideways. And on its own, the graph can't even tell you which kind of wave it **is**.

Visual: board change: v04's spring on its driver across the top (tag "slowed down"), one coil purple over a grey home tick. Its teal displacement arrow points forward and back along the spring; on "plot" the arrow rotates up onto the graph's vertical axis (axis label "displacement (cm), forward +"; an amber outline arrow "wave travels →" by the spring), and the purple trace draws the same curve. On "same way" a purple "T" bracket and a teal "A" bracket appear. On "sideways" a ghost chip "a sound wave can't make a smooth curve" fades in and is crossed. On "is" the spring's trace and the cord's trace (scene 3) sit side by side with the source pictures greyed: the two graphs are identical; a chip "transverse or longitudinal? the graph doesn't say".

### 8. comparing two graphs
> Graphs also let us compare waves. Here's the same point with the driver at two point oh hertz, and then at four point oh hertz. The faster driver's peaks are twice as close together in time. So its period halves, to zero point two five seconds, and its frequency doubles. The peaks are just as tall, so the amplitude hasn't changed. But neither graph shows a **wavelength**, because there's no distance anywhere on these axes. To get one, you need the speed. On this cord, that's three point oh metres per second. So at four point oh hertz, lambda is v over f, zero point seven five **metres**.

Visual: back to the cord; one time axis with two traces: "2.0 Hz" (purple) and "4.0 Hz" (a second purple shade, dashed). ### 15. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. A displacement-time graph follows one particle: its displacement from rest (with a sign) against time. It is not a picture of the wave.
2. Period T: the time between matching points (peak to next peak). Peak to trough is T ÷ 2. f = 1/T.
3. Amplitude A: the height of a peak above the zero line (the rest position).
4. Gradient = the particle's velocity: zero at a peak or trough (turning), greatest through zero.
5. Longitudinal waves give the same curve (forward +). No wavelength can be read: λ needs a distance axis, or λ = v/f.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 10; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "The graph shows the displacement of one coil of a long spring against time, as a longitudinal wave passes. Model answer: (a) A = 8.0 mm (the peak height above the zero line). T = 0.65 − 0.15 = 0.50 s (successive peaks). f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 0.50 = 2.0 Hz. (b) t = 0.40 s is half a period after the peak at 0.15 s, so the coil is at a trough: 8.0 mm from its rest position, displaced against the direction of travel. The graph is flat there, so the coil is momentarily at rest, about to move forward again. Classic mistake (crossed): "T = 0.65 s" (the time of the second peak, not the gap).
