### 2. a scale is a ratio
> The scale says one to twenty-five thousand. It's a ratio, comparing a length on the map with the matching real length. And like any ratio, both sides are in the same unit. So one centimetre on the map stands for twenty-five thousand centimetres on the **ground**. One millimetre stands for twenty-five thousand millimetres. That's why a scale has no units. It works in whatever unit you measure **with**. In other words, the map is twenty-five thousand times smaller than the real **land**.

### 6. finding the scale
> You can also turn a scale bar back into a ratio. Two centimetres stands for five hundred metres. Same unit first, so five hundred metres is fifty thousand **centimetres**. Then one centimetre stands for fifty thousand divided by two, twenty-five thousand centimetres. So the scale is one to twenty-five **thousand**, matching the printed one. To find a scale, put both lengths in the same unit, then divide the real length by the map **length**.

### 7. worked example: a bike ride
> Here's an exam-style one. Two towns are seven point five centimetres apart on a map with a scale of one to a hundred thousand. Find the real distance in kilometres. A cyclist averages eighteen kilometres an hour. How long would the ride take, in minutes? Map to ground, so multiply. Seven point five times a hundred thousand is seven hundred and fifty thousand **centimetres**. Divide by a hundred, then by a thousand, and that's seven point five **kilometres**. Time is distance divided by speed, so that's seven point five divided by eighteen, of an hour. Multiply by sixty to change hours to minutes, and it's twenty-five **minutes**. Dividing by a hundred thousand is the slip. Going from the map to the ground, the distance gets **bigger**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why no units: both terms share one unit (2). Why multiply: each map centimetre stands for n real ones (3). Why convert after: the product is still in the map's unit (3).
