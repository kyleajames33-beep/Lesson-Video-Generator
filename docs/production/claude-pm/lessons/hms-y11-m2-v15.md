### 5. quality, when the thing measured is a body
> Now quality. Earlier, you met three checks: did you measure the right thing, would you get it again, and can you trust the source? They come apart. A scale that always reads three kilos heavy is perfectly reliable, and wrong every time. So reliable doesn't mean valid. For heart rate, validity means controlling what else could change it: a control group, the same time of day, and no caffeine, hard exercise or poor sleep beforehand, because each can shift the result on its own. Reliability means the same measurer, the same method, repeated and averaged. Credibility means reporting your full method and every result. The class had none of these. Their conclusion is true, but their investigation isn't why we believe it.

### 7. the debate: is fitness testing worth it?
> Now the bigger question: should we test fitness at all? The answer changes with the group. For elite athletes, testing sets training intensities and shows whether they're recovering, though they can end up training for the test. For school students, a test can give a baseline and teach self-monitoring, but public, ranked testing can humiliate the students who most need encouragement. For adults, screening can catch heart risk early, but a poor score can put someone off starting, and a maximal test without screening is risky. For older adults, balance and chair-rise tests predict falls, but tests designed for young athletes aren't valid or safe for them. Two ideas settle most of it. The harm is usually in the purpose and the comparison, not the measurement. Comparing yourself with your own earlier result, privately, is very different from a public ranking. And a test is only worth doing if a decision follows from it.

### 9d. feedback, item two
> Data: players start at different times, count their own way, and heart rate falls during a long count, so the results aren't reliable. Fix: one start signal and a fifteen-second count, or monitors.
