### 1. hook (the meter can't count it)
> The label on this white vinegar says five per cent acidity. How could you check how much ethanoic acid is really in it? A pH meter won't do it well. Ethanoic acid is **weak**, so most of it stays as whole molecules, and the meter only senses the few that have ionised. You could work back through **K a**, but a reading of two point four could mean anything from about zero point seven to one point one moles per litre. You need a method that counts **every** acid molecule. That's a **titration**. So how does it work, and how do its numbers give a concentration?

### 2. the idea and the apparatus
> The idea is to react the unknown with a solution whose concentration you know exactly, a **standard solution**, and measure precisely how much it takes. ### 3. equivalence, endpoint, and why it counts every molecule

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Titration: an exact volume (aliquot) of one solution reacts with the other solution, added from a burette, until the equivalence point. One of the two is a standard solution. The indicator's colour change is the endpoint, very close to the equivalence point.
2. A primary standard is very pure, stable in air and does not take up water while weighed, so its mass gives its moles exactly (for example, anhydrous Na₂CO₃ dried in an oven). NaOH absorbs water and CO₂, so its solution is standardised first.

4. Four steps: balanced equation and mole ratio; n(known) = c × V (V in litres); n(unknown) from the ratio; c(unknown) = n ÷ V.
5. A titration counts all of the acid, weak or strong: the titre depends on the moles of acid, not its strength.
