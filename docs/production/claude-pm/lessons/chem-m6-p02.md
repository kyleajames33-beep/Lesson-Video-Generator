### 1. hook (the peak you never see)
> Here's an odd thing about this experiment. When you mix an acid and a base in a foam cup, the thermometer doesn't show the highest temperature the mixture could reach. Heat starts **escaping** the moment it's made, so by the time the reading peaks, some is already gone. That's partly why the last video's answer fell short of minus fifty seven. So how do you set up this experiment, and how do you measure a temperature you **never actually see**?

### 2. the set-up, and why each part is there
> Here's the set-up. Fifty millilitres of one mole per litre hydrochloric acid goes into a polystyrene foam cup, standing in a **beaker** so it can't tip. Foam is full of trapped air, so it lets very little heat through: it's an **insulator**. A **lid** stops warm air and vapour carrying heat away, with holes for the temperature probe and a stirrer. Measure both solutions with **measuring cylinders**, because the moles and the mass both come from those volumes.

### 3. the starting temperature
> Before mixing, measure the temperature of **each** solution separately, and wait until both are steady. If the acid reads twenty one point four and the base twenty one point six, use the **average**, twenty one point five, because you're mixing equal volumes. Why bother? A warm base would raise the temperature even with **no reaction** at all.

Visual: the cup (acid inside) left with readout "21.4 °C"; the NaOH cylinder right with a coded probe outline and its own readout "21.6 °C". On "each" both readouts pulse in turn. On "average" a line writes in the right zone: "T(initial) = (21.4 + 21.6) ÷ 2 = 21.5 °C". On "no reaction" a crossed chip "use the acid's temperature only" fades in (caption "a warmer base adds heat without reacting"). Hold about 1 second.

### 4. mix and keep logging

That's our best estimate of the temperature with no heat lost. Extending a line past your measured points is called **extrapolation**. It assumes the cup was losing heat at about the **same rate** during the rise as afterwards.

### 8. the variables in a repeated measurement
> So what are the variables? Here you aren't changing anything between runs: you're measuring **one value**, three times. So the **controlled variables** matter most: the same concentrations and volumes, the same starting temperature, and the same cup, lid, stirring and timing.

### 9. errors: follow the chain
> Why is minus fifty five still short of minus fifty seven? Follow each error through the **chain**: what it does to delta T, then q, then delta H. Heat escaping through the walls and lid makes delta T too **small**, so q is too small, and delta H comes out **less negative**. The cup and probe soak up a little heat too, with the same effect. Most errors here push the same way, but **not every one**: treating the mixture as pure water is a small error that can go either way. And never write "**human error**". Name the actual cause.

Visual: the error-chain row writes across the right zone: four linked boxes "source → ΔT → q → ΔH". ### 10. validity, reliability and accuracy
> Now the three words from the equilibrium practical. **Validity**: does the experiment measure what it's meant to? Here, the rise must come from the reaction, with the heat kept in the solution: equal starting temperatures, an insulated lidded cup, and extrapolation. **Reliability**: do repeats agree? Three runs gave minus fifty five, minus fifty four and minus fifty six, so the mean, minus fifty five, is reliable. **Accuracy**: how close is it to the accepted value, about minus fifty seven? Repeats can't fix that. Cutting **heat loss** can.

Visual: three cards write in turn in the right zone, each with its plain question and this practical's answer, the previous card dimming: "Validity: measures what it should? Improve by reducing heat loss." On "heat loss" a chip "more repeats make it more accurate" fades in and is crossed (caption "repeats improve reliability; heat loss still lowers every run"). A small grey footer cites the meanings: "NESA glossary: reliability, repeated measurements under identical circumstances give similar results." Hold about 2 seconds.

### 11. the risk assessment
> Before you start, the risk assessment. One mole per litre sodium hydroxide is **corrosive**: it can cause severe skin burns and eye damage. ### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Set-up: 50.0 mL each of 1.00 mol/L HCl and NaOH (measuring cylinders); lidded polystyrene cup standing in a beaker (foam insulates; lid keeps warm air in). Measure both starting temperatures; use the average.
2. Mix quickly, lid on, stir; record every 30 s until well past the peak. Plot temperature against time and extrapolate the straight cooling line back to the moment of mixing (assumes the same cooling rate during the rise).
3. Example: T(initial) 21.5 °C, extrapolated 28.1 °C, ΔT = 6.6 °C, q = 2758.8 J, n(H₂O) = 0.0500 mol, ΔH = −55 kJ/mol (highest reading gives −53 kJ/mol). Accepted about −57 kJ/mol.
4. Errors: source → ΔT → q → ΔH. Heat loss and heat absorbed by the cup and probe make ΔH less negative. Not every error does (the pure-water assumption can go either way). Never "human error".
5. Validity: rise from the reaction only, heat kept in. Reliability: repeats agree (−55, −54, −56 kJ/mol). Accuracy: closeness to −57; improve by reducing heat loss, not by repeating. Risk: 1.00 mol/L NaOH is corrosive (H314): eye protection, rinse splashes; support the cup; stir with a stirrer.

## Safety facts check (self-check, 21 Oct; science check to confirm)

- **Sodium hydroxide, 1.00 mol/L:** 40.0 g/L, about 3.9 percent by mass (density about 1.04 g/mL).
