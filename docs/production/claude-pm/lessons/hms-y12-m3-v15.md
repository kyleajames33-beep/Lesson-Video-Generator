### 2. measuring: what counts as health
> Start with measuring. **Health technology** means any device, test or system used to prevent, find, treat or manage illness. What we can measure decides what gets attention. Once a blood test or a clinic visit, measurement now happens all day on a wrist, and population data is how we know any health trend at all. Three things follow. What gets measured gets managed, so problems without a clean number, like pain, fatigue or loneliness, get less. Measurement moves the line for being ill. Blood pressure and blood sugar have thresholds, so lower the threshold and many people become patients overnight, with no change in their bodies. And more data isn't better information. A watch mostly records normal variation, so telling a real signal from noise is a clinical skill.

### 3. monitoring: care leaves the building
> Monitoring is measuring again and again, and that changes what care can be. A check-up every three months samples a condition four times a year. A glucose sensor or a home blood pressure cuff sees it all the time, so treatment can change when the condition changes, not when the calendar says. With telehealth, **remote monitoring** shrinks distance for any care that doesn't need hands, which matters most in rural areas. It shifts information to the person, and work too. But it has three limits. Data without a responder isn't care. If nobody's funded to read it, it's just numbers. Constant data about your body can raise anxiety. And it needs devices, internet and confidence, the **digital divide**, which is often worst exactly where distance is worst.

### 4. early diagnosis: is earlier always better?
> Early diagnosis is one of the biggest real gains of the last fifty years. Better imaging finds disease while it's treatable, and screening programs find bowel, breast and cervical cancer before symptoms. But earlier isn't automatically better. **Overdiagnosis** means correctly finding a condition that would never have harmed the person. They get treatment's harms with none of its benefit, and they'll believe the test saved them. Then there are **false positives**, results that wrongly say disease is there. Take a thousand people, where ten have a condition. A good test finds nine of them. But if it wrongly flags five per cent of the rest, that's about fifty false alarms. So most positive results are wrong. And earlier knowledge doesn't help if earlier treatment doesn't work better, while sharper scans find harmless surprises that all need chasing. So the real test isn't whether it finds more disease. It's whether the people it finds live longer or better.

### 5. precision surgery, and who gets it first
> **Precision surgery** means keyhole, image-guided and robot-assisted operations. Smaller wounds mean less pain, fewer complications and shorter stays, which is a big part of why hospitals changed shape. But the equipment is expensive, needs training and a steady volume of cases, so it gathers in big city centres, and rural patients travel. Not every new technique beats the old one. Some give the same results at higher cost, so newer and better are separate claims. And benefits arrive first where the money and volume are, so a real advance can widen a gap for years before it narrows it. Shorter stays also mean fuller hospitals, with less room to surge.

### 6. the relationship: four propositions
> So what is the relationship? Four propositions. One: technology brings real gains and is a leading driver of rising costs, and those are the same fact, because it mostly adds to what we can do. Two: it changes where care happens, and who holds the information. Three: it changes what counts as illness, sometimes saving a life, sometimes overdiagnosing, and only outcome evidence can tell which. Four: it reaches the best-off first, so it widens gaps before it narrows them, unless someone acts deliberately. And remember technology is also the world we live in. It has taken activity out of work and travel, and screens can cut into sleep. So technology raises the ceiling on what health systems can achieve, but it doesn't decide who reaches it.
