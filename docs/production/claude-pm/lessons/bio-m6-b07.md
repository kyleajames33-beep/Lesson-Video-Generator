### 2. three processes, three jobs
> Three processes produce it, and you've met them all. A mutation changes the base sequence, so it's the only one that can make a brand-new allele. Meiosis shuffles the alleles a parent already has into gametes that differ, by crossing over and independent assortment. And fertilisation joins one gamete from each parent, at random. So how do the three fit together? Let's follow one new allele, from the moment it appears.

### 3. one new allele, half the gametes
> Suppose one sperm carries a mutation in a beak gene, and it fertilises an egg. The chick that grows has the new allele in every cell, because every cell is copied from that first one. It sits on one chromosome of a pair, with the original allele on the partner chromosome. When this bird makes its own gametes, meiosis one separates the partners. So about half its gametes get the new allele, and half get the original.

### 4. new neighbours, new combinations
> Meiosis doesn't just pass the new allele on, it gives it new neighbours. In crossing over, partner chromosomes swap matching pieces, so the new allele can end up beside different alleles of nearby genes. Independent assortment then sends its chromosome into a gamete with either partner of every other pair. So one bird spreads its new allele across many combinations, and none of that makes another new allele.

### 5. fertilisation: a genotype that never existed
> Then fertilisation joins each gamete with one from another bird. If the carrier mates with a bird that lacks the new allele, no chick can get two copies, but the allele spreads to some of its chicks. Later, when two of those carriers mate, a chick can inherit two copies, with a one in four chance each time. So if the new allele is recessive, its effect stays hidden until two carriers' gametes meet. Fertilisation has made a genotype that never existed before, from alleles that did.

### 6. how much does each add in one generation?
> So how much does each process add in one generation? In humans, scientists have counted by reading the DNA of parents and their children. Each child carries, on average, about sixty to seventy new mutations, out of roughly six billion bases. Meanwhile, each parent has two different versions at millions of places in their DNA. Meiosis can sort them into more than eight million chromosome combinations, before crossing over adds more, and fertilisation multiplies the two parents' choices together. So when two siblings differ, almost all of those differences were already in their parents, reshuffled.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Mutation changes the base sequence and is the only process that makes new alleles. Meiosis (crossing over and independent assortment) and fertilisation (random joining of gametes) make new combinations of existing alleles.
- A new allele in a germ-line cell gives a carrier with one copy. Meiosis I separates the partner chromosomes, so half the carrier's gametes carry the allele. Two copies need two carriers' gametes to meet (1 in 4 for two carriers).
- In one generation, almost all the differences between offspring come from reshuffling (more than 8 million chromosome combinations per human parent, against about 60 to 70 new mutations per child).
- Every allele first arose by mutation: mutation is the ultimate source of variation; meiosis and fertilisation are the immediate source.
- Bacteria reproduce asexually, with no meiosis or fertilisation: new variation comes from mutation and gene transfer.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (19 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Griffiths et al., Introduction to Genetic Analysis) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Daphne Major, 1977.** Boag PT, Grant PR (1981) Science 214:82 to 85: the 1977 drought on Daphne Major; *Geospiza fortis* fell from about 1,200 to about 180 birds (about 85 per cent); small soft seeds were depleted and the remaining seeds were larger and harder (notably *Tribulus cistoides*); larger birds with deeper beaks survived better. Offspring hatched in 1978 had beaks about 4 to 5 per cent deeper on average than the pre-drought population (Grant 1986; Grant and Grant 2014). "Most of the medium ground finches died", "survivors tended to have deeper beaks", "chicks hatched with deeper beaks on average": correct. Beak depth is heritable (Boag 1983, heritability about 0.7 to 0.8). "No new mutation was needed": the response drew on existing heritable variation (author note: the beak-size variation involves alleles such as an *HMGA2* haplotype, Lamichhaney et al. 2016, which predate the drought; hybridisation with *G. scandens* adds variation, Grant and Grant 2002). The Grants' study began in 1973 (not said). "Medium ground finch" is the common name of *G. fortis*.
- **Segregation of a new allele (scenes 3 and 5, 10b).** A heterozygote passes each allele to about half its gametes (homologues separate in meiosis I); Nn × NN gives 1/2 Nn; Nn × Nn gives 1/4 nn. Computed by the cross function. "About half" in narration: correct (expected proportion). 10a's stem makes the animal heterozygous in every cell, so germ-line mosaicism (a mutation in one spermatogonial stem cell reaches only some sperm) does not arise; author note.
- **Crossing over gives the allele new neighbours (scene 4).** Crossing over between non-sister chromatids recombines alleles of linked genes; independent assortment recombines alleles on different chromosomes. Correct (B5b). The four gamete labels are computed from the stated chromosomes (E d on teal after one crossover between d and e) and one assortment choice; science check to confirm the screen labels match one crossover plus both line-ups.
- **New mutations per human child (scene 6).** Parent-offspring trio sequencing: about 60 to 70 de novo point mutations per child, rising with the father's age (Kong et al. 2012, Nature 488:471, mean about 63 at an average paternal age of about 30; Jónsson et al. 2017, Nature 549:519, about 1.29 × 10⁻⁸ per base pair per generation, about 70 per genome). Diploid genome about 6.2 billion base pairs (haploid about 3.1 billion); "roughly six billion bases" for the two copies a child carries: correct. Small insertions and deletions add a few more (author note). Confirmed by the Claude science check: Jónsson et al. found 108,778 de novo mutations in 1,548 probands, about 70 each; the narration says "on average" because the count varies widely with the father's age (about 1.5 more per year). Parents' differing sites (Revision 2): two copies of a human genome differ at about one base in a thousand, so a person is heterozygous at about 3 to 4 million sites ("millions of places").
- **More than 8 million combinations (scene 6).** 2 to the power 23 = 8,388,608 per parent (assortment alone); one couple's whole-chromosome pairings 2 to the power 46 = 70,368,744,177,664, about 70 trillion (B8). Crossing over adds more. Correct; "fertilisation multiplies the two parents' choices together": correct.
- **Siblings (scene 6).** Full siblings share about half their DNA identical by descent; their differences arise from parental heterozygous sites recombined and assorted differently, plus each child's tens of new mutations. "Almost all of those differences were already in their parents, reshuffled": correct (a parent is heterozygous at millions of sites, against tens of new mutations per child).
- **Ultimate and immediate source (scene 7).** Every allele originated as a mutation; recombination generates the combinations present in each generation. Standard framing (site Analyse model). "Any allele the population lost, by chance or selection, would never be made again" if mutation stopped: correct (gene flow could re-introduce an allele from another population only if it still existed there; it would not be "made"; author note).
- **Asexual organisms (scene 8).** Bacterial binary fission gives genetically identical daughters except for new mutations; horizontal gene transfer (transformation, conjugation, transduction) adds genes, including on plasmids. Large populations and short generation times mean many new mutations arise in a population each day. Correct (OpenStax Microbiology 11.6; site correction 4). Note: Revision 2 narrows the learner claim to bacteria, because asexual eukaryotes (such as plants grown from runners) rarely gain genes by transfer; "it matters even more in asexual organisms" (10d) stays, since it holds generally (author note).
- **Recessive hidden in carriers (scene 5).** A recessive allele's phenotype appears only in homozygotes (B9). Correct for a fully recessive autosomal allele; the narration says "if the new allele is recessive".
