### 1. hook (a name is a set of instructions)
> The canister under this camping stove holds **butane**, often mixed with propane. That name isn't a brand. It's a code: but means four carbons, and ane means only single bonds between them. Chemists name carbon compounds this way, so a name like **three-methylpent-one-ene** is really a set of drawing instructions. So how do you read a name like that, and write one from a drawing?

### 2. carbon's four bonds, chains and branches
> You know from Year 11 that carbon has four valence electrons, so each carbon forms **four** covalent bonds. Carbon can bond to other carbons, so it builds long **chains**, and a chain can grow **branches**. That's why there are millions of carbon compounds, and why each one needs a name that points to just one structure. Real chains aren't straight, either. The bonds spread out, so the carbons **zigzag**.

Visual: the board clears. On "four" one carbon draws with four bond lines and four grey H atoms (tag "4 bonds per carbon", Year 11 chip "from Year 11" small). On "chains" carbons join one by one into a four-carbon chain, H atoms rearranging so every carbon keeps four bonds. On "branches" a fifth carbon attaches to the second carbon as an amber branch (tag "branch"). On "zigzag" the flat drawing lifts into the ink ball-and-stick of butane (ported engine3d math, paper-toned atoms, ink bonds), which turns slowly through about 60° so the zigzag is visible, then flattens back to a zigzag line (tag "real chains zigzag"). No bond angles labelled. Bright at the end: the zigzag and its tag. Hold about 1 second.

### 3. three ways to draw a chain
> There are three common ways to draw a chain. A **full structural formula** shows every atom and every bond. A **condensed formula** writes each carbon with its hydrogens: C H three, C H two, C H two, C H three. A **skeletal formula** keeps just the zigzag. Every corner and every end is a carbon, and the hydrogens are left out. You put them back in your head, enough to give each carbon **four bonds**: three on an end carbon, two on a corner, and one where a branch joins.

Visual: task line "Butane, three ways". On "full structural formula" butane draws in full (four C, ten H, every line); tag "every atom, every bond". On "condensed formula" the structure board morphs: each carbon's hydrogens slide in beside it to read "CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃" (tag "carbons with their hydrogens"; the full drawing dims to a small kept thumbnail at the left). On "skeletal formula" the condensed line morphs into a zigzag of three strokes; the carbons shrink into the corners and ends (tag "corners and ends = carbons"). On "four bonds" the implied-H counter runs: grey H ghosts appear on the left end carbon (three) and the next corner (two), each ghost ticking a "bonds: 4" chip, then fade; on "branch joins" a one-stroke branch grows from the second corner and that corner's ghosts drop to one (tag "branch point: 1 H", dims), then the branch retracts. Bright at the end: the skeletal zigzag, the H ghosts and the "bonds: 4" chip. Hold about 1 second.

### 4. rule 1: the stem counts the carbons
> Now the naming rules, one at a time. Rule one: the start of the name, the stem, counts the carbons in the main chain. **Meth** is one, eth two, prop three, but four. A way to remember those: **Monkeys Eat Peeled Bananas**. From five, the stems match shapes you know: **pent** as in pentagon, hex as in hexagon, hept as in heptagon, and oct, as in octagon, for eight.

Visual: the board clears. Rule chip "Rule 1 of 5: the stem counts carbons". A stem ladder writes down the left, each line lighting on its word and then dimming: "meth 1", "eth 2", "prop 3", "but 4". ## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why carbon compounds need systematic names: four bonds and carbon-carbon bonding make chains and branches, so millions of structures exist and each name must point to one (2). Why skeletal formulas zigzag: real chains zigzag (2). Why you can leave hydrogens out: every carbon has four bonds, so the missing bonds are hydrogens (3), and a C=C uses two of them, so its carbons carry one fewer hydrogen (9, applied in feedback 1). Why the main chain must contain the double bond: the ending describes the main chain's bonds (9). Why the stem counts the main chain, not every carbon (6: a branch drawn hanging down can belong to the longest chain). Why number from the nearer end: the rule gives each name one set of numbers, the lowest, so everyone writes the same name (7). Why ethene and propene need no number: there is only one place the double bond can go (9). Why the double bond decides the numbering: the ending names the bond, so it gets first claim on the low numbers (10). Why count after drawing: a missed branch leaves the formula short of atoms, so the drawing cannot be the named molecule (11).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** the main chain is the horizontal row (6); 3-methylbutane (7, crossed); alphabetical order by locant (8); leaving the double bond out of the main chain (9); numbering for the branch over the double bond (10, crossed; feedback 1, crossed); C₆H₁₄ for 2,2-dimethylpentane (11, crossed); 2-ethylpentane and 4-methylhexane (attempt 2 and feedback 2, crossed).

Title: IUPAC Naming of Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes: Skeletal Formulas and Locants | Year 12 Chemistry

Description: Full lesson, notes and practice questions: https://hscscience.com.au/subjects/chemistry/year12/module7/lesson01

A name like 3-methylpent-1-ene is a set of drawing instructions. Learn the three ways to draw a carbon chain (full structural, condensed and skeletal), then the naming rules one at a time: the stem counts the carbons, the ending shows the bonds, find the longest chain even when it bends, number from the nearer end, put branches in alphabetical order, and give a double or triple bond the lowest number. Then turn a name back into a molecule and check it by counting. Two exam-style questions at the end.
