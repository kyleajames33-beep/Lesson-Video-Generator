So what can data from half a million people show that one family, or one hospital, never could? And how far can we trust it?

### 2. why scale matters
> A project like this is called a large-scale collaborative project: many researchers and sites pool their data. Scale matters because most variants only nudge risk a little. In a small study, chance alone can make one group look different, so a small real effect gets lost in the noise. With hundreds of thousands of people, a real difference stands out from chance. That gives stronger inference, meaning a conclusion drawn from evidence. Stronger, but never certain.

Visual: on "many researchers and sites pool their data" five site badges each send a stream of chips into one central bar; the term writes on with the gloss "many groups pooling data". On "only nudge risk a little" two bars appear, "carriers" and "non-carriers", differing by a sliver (illustrative; tag "a small real difference"). On "In a small study" B11's `SampleBounce` runs with n = 200: repeated estimates of the difference scatter widely, some below zero (computed with seeded `rng`; as the first dots land the tag "each dot: one small study's result" writes on, then "200 people: chance swamps it"). ### 3. the sickle cell puzzle
> Pooling data isn't new. Here's the classic case. The sickle cell allele causes sickle cell anaemia when someone inherits two copies. A harmful allele like that should become rare, because natural selection removes it. But when frequency data from many countries were combined into one map, the allele turned out to be common across large parts of Africa, and also found around the Mediterranean, the Middle East and India. A pattern that a simple model can't explain is exactly what makes scientists dig deeper.

Visual: the map board. `m6-red-cells-normal-sickle-v1.png` sits small at the upper left (tag "two copies: sickle cell anaemia"). On "should become rare" a small coded line graph draws a frequency falling towards zero over generations (tag "expected: selection removes it"; grey tag "Year 11: natural selection"). ### 4. three kinds of evidence
> Studies like this combine three kinds of evidence. Family trees show how it's inherited: unaffected parents can have an affected child, so it's recessive, and carriers are healthy. DNA data show what changed: one base in the haemoglobin gene. And frequency data show where it's common. No single kind answers the whole question. The conclusion comes from all three agreeing.

### 5. the malaria overlay
> So why is it common in those places? Lay a map of malaria over the allele map, and across Africa, they line up closely. Malaria is caused by a parasite that a mosquito passes on, and it grows inside red blood cells. In carriers, infected cells tend to sickle and get cleared, so carriers don't get the anaemia, and they're much less likely to die of severe malaria. So where malaria is common, carriers survive and have children more often, and the allele stays common. This is called heterozygote advantage.

Visual: on "Lay a map of malaria" a hatched overlay (`WorldShade` second layer, hatching not colour, tag "historical malaria") slides over the allele map; on "across Africa" the overlap regions ring amber in Africa only, and a grey tag writes on over Asia and the Americas: "weaker match outside Africa" (source tag "Piel et al. 2010"). On "infected cells tend to sickle and get cleared" a small coded inset shows one infected red cell deforming into a sickle shape and fading out (tag "infected carrier cells sickle and are removed"). On "carriers don't get the anaemia" three genotype chips line up: "two normal alleles", "carrier (one sickle allele)", "two sickle alleles", each with two small bars, "anaemia" and "severe malaria risk": the carrier's anaemia bar is near zero and its malaria bar is short (illustrative heights; grey tag "illustrative"). ### 6. a match isn't proof
> But two maps that line up aren't proof on their own. Two patterns can match by coincidence, just like any association. Scientists trust the malaria explanation because other studies showed directly that carriers really do resist severe malaria. And a high frequency can have another cause. In a small, isolated group, an allele can become common just by chance, because a few of the founders happened to carry it.

### 7. protecting a threatened species
> The same kind of data helps protect threatened species. Tasmanian devils are being killed by a contagious facial cancer. Genetic data from many devils showed they have very little genetic diversity, meaning few different alleles, after past population crashes and isolation. That matters, because with fewer alleles there's less chance that some animals carry one that helps against a new disease. So breeders use the data to pair devils that are less related, keeping as much diversity as possible. But diversity only improves the odds. It doesn't guarantee survival.

### 8. what shared variants say about human history
> Large data also show how human populations are related. The 1000 Genomes Project sequenced the genomes of 2,504 people from 26 populations around the world. Populations that share more variants are likely to share more recent ancestors. African populations carry the most variation, and most common variants found elsewhere are also found in Africa. That fits modern humans first evolving in Africa: each group that left was a small set of founders, carrying only part of that variety. And the reference genome isn't everyone's genome: a typical person differs from it at four to five million places.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Large-scale collaborative project: many researchers and sites pool data (e.g. UK Biobank, about 500,000 people). Large samples detect small effects that chance hides in small ones: stronger inference, not certainty.
- Disease inheritance (sickle cell): pedigrees (recessive), DNA (one base change) and frequency maps agree. Common where malaria was common: carriers resist severe malaria (heterozygote advantage). A matching map is not proof (founders, chance).
- Conservation: low genetic diversity (few alleles) signals past crashes or isolation; guides breeding of less related animals and moving animals between groups. Diversity improves the odds; it does not guarantee survival.
- Human evolution: more shared variants suggests more recent common ancestry; African populations carry the most variation, and most common variants elsewhere are also found there (1000 Genomes): the founders who left carried part of it. The reference genome is a composite of a few donors.
- Big data improve: pattern detection, trend confidence, comparison. They don't remove: sample bias, method limits, uncertainty for one person.

Memory: Big data sharpen the trend; they don't predict a person. Common but harmful? Carrier advantage, or chance in a small group.

- **UK Biobank (hook, scene 9).** Recruited about 500,000 people aged 40 to 69 in 2006 to 2010 across the UK (ukbiobank.ac.uk, "who we are"); blood, urine and saliva samples, linked health records; genotyping array of "around 850,000" directly measured variants (UK Biobank genetic data page; pack check); data available to approved bona fide researchers worldwide, including companies, for health-related research in the public interest (site correction B10). "In Britain": recruitment centres were in England, Scotland and Wales, so "Britain" is correct (not "the UK" strictly, since Northern Ireland had no centre; check). Healthy volunteer bias: Fry et al. 2017, American Journal of Epidemiology 186:1026, found participants less likely to be obese, smoke or drink daily, with fewer self-reported conditions and lower mortality than the general population. Science check (Revision 2): confirmed; recruitment 2006 to 2010, so the hook says "began recruiting in 2006"; "around 850,000" is UK Biobank's own rounded figure (the Axiom array lists about 821,000 markers); healthy volunteer bias confirmed (Fry et al. 2017).
- **Scale (scene 2).** Statistical point only: the standard error of a frequency difference falls with the square root of sample size, so a small difference that is within chance variation at n = 200 can be clearly detected at n = 200,000. The simulation is illustrative (seeded `rng`; true difference set at, for example, 0.20 against 0.22). Correct as stated.
- **Sickle allele map (scene 3, 5).** Piel FB et al. (2010), "Global distribution of the sickle cell gene and geographical confirmation of the malaria hypothesis", Nature Communications 1:104: pooled population surveys (a collaborative data synthesis, Malaria Atlas Project) mapped HbS allele frequency; highest in sub-Saharan Africa, also in the Mediterranean, the Middle East and India; a geographic association with historical malaria endemicity was strong in Africa (weaker elsewhere, attributed to migration and other factors). Science check (Revision 2): 773 data points; highest predicted HbS allele frequency 18.18% (northern Angola); above 9% across a belt from southern Ghana to northern Zambia and in patches of Saudi Arabia and India; the malaria association is strong in Africa and unresolved in the Americas and Asia, so scene 5 now says "across Africa". The paper's classes have no published numeric breaks, so the legend is tagged "bands simplified". The map board must draw bands from the paper's published figure, not invented shading; if the coded map cannot be sourced cleanly, fall back to region labels with banded chips (author note for root).
- **Inheritance and molecular facts (scene 4).** Sickle cell anaemia is autosomal recessive; carriers (HbAS) are usually healthy; a single base substitution in the beta-globin gene (HBB) changes codon 6 (Glu to Val). Correct (B7, B12 conventions).
- **Heterozygote advantage (scene 5, 6).** HbAS carriers are strongly protected against severe and fatal Plasmodium falciparum malaria (for example Williams et al. 2005, Journal of Infectious Diseases, about 90 per cent protection against severe malaria in Kenyan children); not protected from infection itself, so the narration says "much less likely to die of severe malaria" and "resist severe malaria". Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites passed on by Anopheles mosquitoes and infecting red blood cells (after a liver stage; author note). Correct as stated. Scene 5's mechanism (Revision 2), "infected cells tend to sickle and get cleared": parasitised HbAS cells sickle more readily and are removed by the spleen faster (Luzzatto 2012, Mediterr J Hematol Infect Dis 4:e2012065), one of several proposed mechanisms (author note; not on screen). Scene 6 no longer claims a historical order (Allison 1954 gave geographic and direct evidence together).
- **Founders (scene 6).** Founder effect: an allele frequency elevated in a population descended from a few founders, by chance (Module 6 term). Correct as a general statement.
- **Tasmanian devil (scene 7).** Devil facial tumour disease first observed in 1996 (north-east Tasmania), a transmissible cancer spread by biting; devils have very low genetic diversity (Jones et al. 2004, Molecular Ecology, microsatellites; Miller et al. 2011, PNAS, genome sequencing), attributed to historical declines and long isolation on Tasmania; the Save the Tasmanian Devil Program's insurance population is managed with pedigree and genetic data to retain diversity (for example Hogg et al. 2015 onwards). "Past population crashes and isolation": check wording against sources (some authors attribute low diversity to climate-linked declines thousands of years ago and to isolation, not to a single crash). No numbers are spoken. Science check (Revision 2): supported (Miller et al. 2011; Bruniche-Olsen et al. 2014); low diversity predates the tumour disease; in 2017 diverse insurance-population devils were released to boost wild diversity.
- **1000 Genomes (scene 8).** The 1000 Genomes Project Consortium (2015), "A global reference for human genetic variation", Nature 526:68: 2,504 individuals from 26 populations in five continental groups; "a typical genome differs from the reference human genome at 4.1 million to 5.0 million sites"; individuals of African ancestry have the largest number of variant sites; most common variants are shared across continents. Science check (Revision 2): common variants are largely shared across continents and rare variants are mostly population-restricted, so the narration now says "most common variants found elsewhere are also found in Africa" (the earlier "mostly carry a subset" was too broad); the founder cause matches the serial founder model. Bars: use the paper's per-genome variant site counts by continental group.
- **Reference genome (scene 8).** The Human Genome Project reference is a composite from a small number of donors (most from one donor library). Correct (site misconception card).
