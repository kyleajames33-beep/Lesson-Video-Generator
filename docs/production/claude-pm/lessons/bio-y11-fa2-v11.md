### 7. why both ends matter
> Both ends matter, and a simple model shows why. Join two bags made of partially permeable membrane with a tube, fill one with sugar solution, and stand both in water. Water moves into the sugary bag, and solution flows along the tube to the other one. But the flow soon dies away, because sugar builds up in the second bag and the difference shrinks. In a plant, the sink keeps taking sugar out. Loading keeps the source end high, and unloading keeps the sink end low. And in this model, much of the water goes round in a loop: in from the xylem at the source, back to the xylem at the sink.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Translocation: movement of sugar (mainly sucrose) in phloem from a source (makes sugar or releases it from storage, e.g. a leaf in light) to a sink (uses or stores sugar, e.g. a root, fruit, young leaf, tuber in summer).
2. At the source (example: an active loader such as potato), companion cells load sugar into the sieve tube by active transport (ATP); some plants load passively. The sap's water concentration falls, so water enters from the xylem by osmosis; the walls push back, so pressure rises.
3. At the sink, sugar is unloaded (respiration, growth, starch); water leaves by osmosis, much of it back to the xylem; pressure stays low.
4. The pressure difference (pressure gradient) drives bulk flow of sap from source to sink. No cell pumps the sap along the tube; the flow lasts only while both ends keep working.
5. Direction follows the source and sink (up or down), not gravity; sources and sinks can change (young leaf → source; tuber: sink in summer, source in spring).

- **Aphids (1, 11a).** Aphids insert their stylets into a single sieve element; sap enters under its own pressure (aphids regulate intake but need little suction); in the stylet experiments (Mittler 1957; Kennedy and Mittler 1953), the aphids were anaesthetised with carbon dioxide and the stylets severed, and sap exuded for hours. Aphid length about 1 to 4 mm (grey tag "about 2 to 3 mm" for a typical species). Correct. No cutting is shown; the plate shows the stylet stub only.
- **Source and sink definitions (2).** A source exports sugar (mature photosynthesising leaves; storage organs when mobilising); a sink imports sugar (roots, fruits, growing tips, young leaves, storage organs when filling). Correct.
- **Sap contents (2, tag).** Mainly sucrose, plus amino acids, hormones, ions and other substances. Correct.
- **Loading example (3), Revision 2.** "Take an active loader, like the potato in the last video": potato is an apoplastic (active) loader; passive loaders exist (PMC3375970) and are named on a screen tag. Consistent with v10 Revision 2.
- **Water entry (3).** Loading raises the solute concentration of the sieve element, lowering its water potential; water enters by osmosis from the adjacent xylem (and surrounding apoplast). The sieve element's plasma membrane is the partially permeable membrane. Correct at the water-concentration level.
- **Pressure (4).** Sieve tube turgor pressures are commonly about 0.6 to 1.5 MPa, and higher in some species (up to about 2 to 3 MPa); a car tyre is about 0.2 to 0.25 MPa above atmospheric. "In some plants, phloem sap is under several times the pressure in a car tyre" is safe (about 3 to 6 times for typical values; more in some). Fallback if the science check prefers no comparison: "phloem sap is under high pressure" (shorter). Revision 2: the science check asked; the fallback is used ("So phloem sap is under high pressure") and the comparison bars are removed (root decision 15). The "walls push back" phrasing is the Year 11 form of hydrostatic pressure in a confined compartment; sieve tubes have thin but strong cellulose walls and are supported by surrounding cells.
- **Sink (5), Revision 2.** Sugar is unloaded (symplastically or apoplastically), used in respiration and growth or stored (for example as starch); the solute concentration in the sieve element at the sink is lower, water leaves, and the pressure is lower. Revision 2 says "in this model water leaves by osmosis, much of it back into the xylem", with a screen tag that water follows a water potential gradient set by solutes and pressure. Correct as a model.
- **Bulk flow (6).** Mass flow of the solution down the hydrostatic pressure gradient; all solutes move with the water at the same speed. "No cell along the way pumps the sap": correct for the driving force (path phloem companion cells retrieve leaked sucrose and maintain the sieve elements, but do not pump the sap along). Correct.
- **Both ends (7).** Münch's model (1930): two osmometers joined by a tube, both in water; water enters the solute-rich bag, solution flows to the other, water leaves it, and the flow declines as the driving difference shrinks. In the plant, unloading at the sink maintains the difference. Revision 2: the narration now says the flow "soon dies away" as the difference shrinks (equilibrium is set by pressure and membrane flux, not by equal concentrations alone), and "in this model, much of the water goes round in a loop". Water circulation between xylem and phloem: correct as a model (Münch circulation).
- **Starch (5).** Starch is insoluble and osmotically almost inactive, so converting sugar to starch keeps the sink's solute concentration low. Correct.
- **Speed (6).** Revision 2: the speed sentence is no longer spoken (used reserve cut); the screen chip keeps it as a scale estimate. Phloem transport is typically about 0.3 to 1.5 m per hour ("often up to about a metre an hour" is safe). Diffusion of sucrose (D about 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m² s⁻¹) over 1 m takes on the order of decades (t ≈ x²/2D ≈ 10⁹ s ≈ 30 years). "far faster than sugar could ever spread by diffusion" is correct.
- **Tracer (10), Revision 2.** The example is labelled illustrative on screen and in speech ("In a typical result"), not attributed to one experiment, and says "with little in the other grown leaves" (not "none"). ¹⁴CO₂ fed to a mature leaf is fixed into sugars that are exported in the phloem to sinks (young leaves, roots, fruits) within hours; other mature leaves import little. Radioactive tracers are used only in research labs (grey tag).
- **Young leaves (9, 10).** Expanding leaves import sugar (sinks) until about a third to half grown, then make the sink-to-source transition and export. "Once a leaf is fully grown, it makes more sugar than it uses, and it becomes a source" simplifies the timing; acceptable. Flag for the science check (fallback "As a leaf grows, it starts making more sugar than it uses", length-neutral).
