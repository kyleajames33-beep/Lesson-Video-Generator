### 2. outer and middle ear
> Let's follow the sound. The outer ear funnels sound waves down the ear canal to the **eardrum**, a thin membrane that vibrates with them. Behind it, in the middle ear, three tiny bones called the **ossicles** pass that vibration along: malleus, incus, then stapes. The stapes pushes on a small window into the inner ear. That window is much smaller than the eardrum, so the pressure is concentrated, and that's what moves the fluid inside.

### 3. the cochlea and hair cells
> In the inner ear, the **cochlea** is a coiled, fluid-filled tube. As the stapes pushes, waves travel through the fluid and make a membrane inside vibrate. High sounds move it most near the start of the coil, and low sounds further along. Sitting on that membrane are **hair cells**, the receptors here. When it moves, their tiny hairs bend, ion channels open, and the cell triggers signals in the **auditory nerve**, which carries them to the brain. So the cochlea is where vibration becomes a nerve signal.

### 4. two kinds of hearing loss
> That gives two main kinds of hearing loss. In **conductive** loss, sound can't get through the outer or middle ear. The canal might be blocked, fluid might fill the middle ear after an infection, the eardrum might be torn, or the stapes might be stuck. The cochlea still works. In **sensorineural** loss, the hair cells or the auditory nerve are damaged, by loud noise, ageing, genes or some infections. Human hair cells don't grow back, so this loss is usually permanent.

### 5. the hearing aid
> Now match a device to the break. A **hearing aid** sits behind or in the ear. But if most hair cells are gone, louder sound has nothing left to stimulate.

### 6. the cochlear implant
> A **cochlear implant** skips the hair cells. A processor behind the ear turns sound into a coded electrical signal. A coil sends it through the skin, by radio, to a receiver under the skin. The receiver sends pulses down a thin array of electrodes threaded along the cochlea. Each electrode stimulates the nerve fibres at its position, so electrodes near the start of the coil signal high pitches. The hair cells are bypassed, but the auditory nerve must still work.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Sound path: outer ear → eardrum → ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) → cochlea, where hair cells turn vibration into signals → auditory nerve → brain.
- Conductive loss: outer or middle ear blocked or damaged; the cochlea works. Sensorineural loss: hair cells or auditory nerve damaged; usually permanent.
- Hearing aid: microphone, amplifier, speaker; makes sound louder along the normal path, so it needs enough working hair cells (it helps when enough usable hearing remains).
- Bone conduction implant: vibration through the skull to the cochlea, skipping the outer and middle ear (conductive or mixed loss, one deaf ear). Cochlear implant: electrodes stimulate the auditory nerve, skipping hair cells (severe to profound sensorineural loss; needs a working nerve). Developed by Graeme Clark's team, Melbourne, 1978.
- An implant gives access to sound, not normal hearing. Match the device to the break, not to how advanced it is.
