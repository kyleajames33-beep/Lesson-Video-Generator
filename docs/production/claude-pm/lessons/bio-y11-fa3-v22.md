## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (3 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Forelimbs (3).** Bat wing, whale flipper and cat foreleg share the pentadactyl plan (humerus; radius and ulna; carpals; metacarpals; phalanges), in the same order. Correct (v09).
- **Thylacine (3, 4, 6).** Skull convergent with canids; mitochondrial and nuclear DNA from museum specimens place it among dasyuromorph marsupials, close to the Dasyuridae (Tasmanian devil, quolls). Correct (v21; site b2).
- **"Thousands of positions" (4).** Phylogenies routinely use thousands of aligned positions or more. Correct.
- **DNA degradation (4).** DNA breaks down after death; the oldest recovered DNA is about 2 million years old (environmental DNA from Greenland, 2022); most fossils are far older and have no recoverable DNA. "Most fossils can't be sequenced": correct.
- **Rates vary (4).** Substitution rates vary among genes and lineages. Correct.
- **Australia's separation (5).** Australia began separating from Antarctica about 85 million years ago, with a deep-water gap by about 35 million years ago (v06's figure). "Tens of millions of years ago": correct.
- **Marsupials and monotremes (5).** About 330 to 340 living marsupial species, roughly two thirds or more in Australia and New Guinea (the rest in the Americas); living monotremes (platypus and echidnas) occur only in Australia and New Guinea. "Most of the world's marsupials and every living monotreme": correct. Science check to confirm the proportion (fallback: "many of the world's marsupials", length-neutral).
- **Biochemistry (6).** All known cellular life stores genetic information in DNA using the bases A, T, C and G, and uses ATP as an energy carrier. Correct. (RNA viruses are not cellular life and are not mentioned.)
- **Selection observed (9).** Antibiotic resistance evolving in bacteria is directly observed natural selection (v18). Correct.
- **Southern beech (11a, 11b).** Nothofagus: south-eastern Australia (including Tasmania), New Zealand, New Guinea, New Caledonia, southern South America; fossil pollen and leaves in Antarctica. Seeds are small nuts, poorly dispersed (mostly by gravity and wind over short distances), and do not survive long in sea water. Molecular dating suggests some trans-Tasman dispersal (Knapp and others, 2005); the model answer says "largely" and "not a likely explanation for the whole distribution" accordingly.
