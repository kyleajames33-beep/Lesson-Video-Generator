- **Site corrections and additions (recorded here only):** (1) The site says "view against a white background". White precipitates (AgCl, BaSO4, CaCO3) are hard to see on white; the video says view over a **black** background as well as a white one (common school practice for well plates). (2) The site's design reason for using nitrates and sodium salts is implicit; the video makes it the central validity idea: partners that always dissolve, so a solid can only be the pair being tested. (3) The site's PbCl2 / PbI2 warm-water analysis question is not used: at 0.1 mol/L stock (0.05 mol/L in the well) lead chloride may or may not precipitate (author note b), and warming needs a test tube in a bath, not a well plate. The "slightly soluble" idea is carried by calcium sulfate instead, which the site names. (4) Composition analysis (the syllabus "predict and analyse the composition ... when two ionic solutions are mixed") is made practical: test the clear liquid above a precipitate with a drop of each reagent to find which ion was in excess. This is new to P03; C13 taught the prediction ("spectators plus any excess").
- **Main teaching move:** the investigation is a test of predictions. Design for validity (always-soluble partners, a check of each stock alone), predict every cell, mix microscale, record carefully, read patterns into rules with evidence, evaluate every mismatch, then analyse a mixture's composition by testing its liquid. Validity, reliability and accuracy are named on this investigation. Risk assessment with the real hazards.

- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Concentrations: with equal drops of 0.1 mol/L solutions, each ion is about 0.05 mol/L in the well. Ion products at 0.05 mol/L against literature Ksp (25 C, for the science check only; never shown): CaSO4 2.5 x 10^-3 against 4.9 x 10^-5; Ca(OH)2 1.25 x 10^-4 against 5.0 x 10^-6; Ag2SO4 1.25 x 10^-4 against 1.2 x 10^-5; PbCl2 1.25 x 10^-4 against 1.7 x 10^-5. All exceed Ksp on paper, but supersaturation, ion pairing and activity effects mean these salts often form slowly, faintly or not at all at this concentration; solubility-based estimates (for example PbCl2 about 1.0 g per 100 mL at 20 C, about 0.036 mol/L, and Ag2SO4 about 0.8 g per 100 mL) put PbCl2 and Ag2SO4 close to the edge. Hence "faint cloud or none" on screen for these four and the spoken "if anything" for the two sulfates. (b) Lead chloride is therefore not used as a spoken example. (c) Ba(OH)2: solubility about 3.9 g per 100 mL (octahydrate basis) at 20 C, about 0.12 mol/L, so 0.05 mol/L stays clear; carbon dioxide from the air can make a faint BaCO3 cloud over time (not spoken). (d) Pb(OH)2 dissolves in excess NaOH (plumbite); with 3 + 3 drops it stays as a white solid; not spoken. (e) Silver with hydroxide gives brown Ag2O (AgOH is not isolated); silver carbonate is pale yellow and darkens in light. (f) Composition test (scene 8, A1): with lead in excess, the clear liquid holds Pb2+, so a drop of KI gives more PbI2; with iodide in excess, a drop of lead nitrate gives more PbI2. A trace of the other ion always remains (a Ksp question, C14); "left over" means a usable amount. With a large excess of iodide, PbI2 can redissolve as iodo complexes; at drop scale this is not seen and is not mentioned. (g) Silver chloride darkens in light (photodecomposition); records should be taken promptly; not spoken. (h) Pronunciation: "lead" as the metal (rhymes with "bed"); "lead nitrate", "lead iodide"; "NAGSAG" as "NAG-sag"; "nought point one". (i) Validity, reliability, accuracy follow common NESA usage (see P01 author note h).

Main question: *How do you set up a solubility investigation so a solid can only mean one thing, and how do you turn a grid of results into rules and test your predictions?*
Entry knowledge (C13, C11 and Year 11): NAGSAG with exceptions, the swap-partners method, precipitates and spectators, "no precipitate" not "no reaction", what stays dissolved after mixing (C13); dissolved ions (C11); variables and risk assessments (Year 11).
Stops after: explaining why the cations are supplied as nitrates and the anions as sodium salts; planning the microscale matrix with predictions first, a check of each stock alone, a clean dropper technique and a black background; naming the independent, dependent and controlled variables and why concentration matters; stating the expected results and tracing each to a rule; writing a rule as a general statement with evidence and exceptions, and separating untested rules; classifying a mismatch as a wrong rule or a technique problem; testing the liquid above a precipitate to find the ion in excess; defining validity, reliability and accuracy for this investigation; writing a risk assessment for lead, silver, barium and hydroxide solutions. No Ksp, no Qsp, no conduct.
Handoff: Ksp, a number for how slightly soluble a compound is (route chem-m5-c14).

### 1. hook (a grid of evidence)
> Twenty-five tiny wells. Five positive ions down the side, five negative ions across the top, and a few drops of each in every well. Some wells stay **clear**. Some go **cloudy**: white, yellow, even brown. This grid is the evidence the solubility rules come from. So how do you set it up so the results can be trusted, and how do you turn a grid of cloudy wells into **rules**?

Visual: heading "Where do the rules come from? Your own results." writes on. The painted well plate (`p03-well-plate-v1.png`, Codex item 1) sits centre on a coded black card; the 5 x 5 block is outlined in pen with the row and column ion labels writing on. On "clear" a few wells fill with coded clear liquid; on "cloudy" others bloom with coded solids in their real colours (white, bright yellow, pale yellow, brown) in the matrix pattern of the Decisions table. On "rules" a small rules card (C13 NAGSAG, faint) slides in at the right with a pen arrow from the grid to it ("evidence → rules"). Hold about 1 second.

### 2. the key design choice: partners that always dissolve
> The clever part is the choice of solutions. Every positive ion comes as a **nitrate**: sodium, calcium, barium, lead and silver nitrate. Every negative ion comes as a **sodium** salt: sodium chloride, iodide, sulfate, carbonate and hydroxide. Nitrates and sodium compounds always dissolve. So when two solutions meet in a well, the only pair that could form a solid is the **pair you're testing**. And before you start, check each solution on its own. It should be clear.

Visual: ten painted dropper bottles (`p03-dropper-bottle-v1.png`, Codex item 2) line up, five down the left and five across the top, with coded labels "NaNO3", "Ca(NO3)2", "Ba(NO3)2", "Pb(NO3)2", "AgNO3" and "NaCl", "NaI", "Na2SO4", "Na2CO3", "NaOH", all "0.1 mol/L". On "nitrate" the NO3- part of each left label lights grey; on "sodium" the Na+ part of each top label lights grey. On "always dissolve" a tag "N and G of NAGSAG: always soluble" writes (C13 rules card rows flash). On "pair you're testing" the C13 swap cross plays small for one well (Pb(NO3)2 + NaI): "Na+ NO3-" ticks green and greys out (spectators), "Pb2+ I-" lights amber: "the only possible solid". On "on its own" each bottle gets one coded drop in a separate check well with a little water from the wash bottle (`m6-wash-bottle-v1.png`); all stay clear, each with a teal tick (tag "stock check: clear on its own"). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 3. the method: predict first, then mix
> Draw a five by five grid in your book and **predict** every cell first, using the rules from the last video. Predicting first is what turns your results into a test. Put a clear well plate over the grid. Add three drops of one positive ion solution along each row, then three drops of one negative ion solution down each column. Keep the dropper tip out of the well, so you don't contaminate the bottle. And look at each well over a **black** background as well as a white one, because a thin white cloud is hard to see on white.

Visual: a coded results grid (book page lines) writes on; on "predict" each cell gets a pen prediction mark ("ppt" or "none") filling quickly row by row. The well plate slides over the grid so each well sits on its cell. On "along each row" a dropper bottle tilts over row 1 and coded drops fall into five wells, then the rows fill in turn (fast); on "down each column" the columns fill. On "dropper tip out" a close inset shows the dropper tip held above a well with a gap and a teal tick, beside a crossed inset of a tip touching the liquid (tag "contaminates the bottle"). On "black" the coded black card slides under half the plate: a faint white cloud in the Ca2+/SO4 2- well is invisible over the white tile (`m6-white-tile-v1.png`) half and clear over the black half (tags "white on white: hard to see", "white on black: clear"). A record-sheet tag under the grid: "record: ppt or no ppt, colour, heavy or faint cloud. Write 'no precipitate', not 'no reaction'." Hold about 2 seconds.

### 4. the variables
> The **independent variable** is the pair of ions in each well. The **dependent variable** is what you see: a precipitate or not, its colour, and whether it's a heavy solid or a faint cloud. Keep these **controlled**: every solution at the same concentration, about nought point one moles per litre, three drops of each, the same temperature, and the same time before you record. Concentration matters most, because some compounds only just dissolve, and whether they show a cloud depends on how much is there.

Visual: the variables card writes in the right zone, one row per cue: "Independent (what you change): the cation and anion pair"; "Dependent (what you observe): ppt or not, colour, heavy or faint"; "Controlled (kept the same): 0.1 mol/L stocks, 3 drops + 3 drops, temperature, time before recording". On "Concentration matters most" two coded wells side by side show the same pair (Ca2+ + SO4 2-) with fewer drops (clear) and more drops (faint cloud), tag "same pair, different amounts". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 5. expected results
> Here's what to expect, and you can check each one against NAGSAG. The sodium row stays clear everywhere. The carbonate column gives a white solid with every metal except sodium, and silver's is pale yellow. Silver gives a solid with both halides, white silver chloride and pale yellow silver iodide, and lead gives bright yellow lead iodide. Barium and lead give white sulfates, while calcium and silver sulfate give only a faint cloud, if anything. And with hydroxide, silver gives a brown solid, but barium stays **clear**.

Visual: the wells fill to the expected matrix (Decisions table) as each sentence lands, each group with its rules-card row flashing small in the corner: Na+ row (all clear, "Group 1: soluble"); CO3 2- column (white, Ag+ pale yellow, Na+ clear: "carbonates: insoluble unless Group 1"); Ag+ and Pb2+ with Cl- and I- ("halides: except Ag+, Pb2+"; PbCl2 cell drawn as a faint cloud with a small tag "faint, or none at this concentration", not spoken); SO4 2- column (Ba2+, Pb2+ white; Ca2+, Ag+ faint: "sulfates: except Ba2+, Pb2+; Ca2+, Ag+ slight"); OH- column (Ag+ brown, Pb2+ white, Ca2+ faint, Ba2+ clear: "hydroxides: insoluble, except Group 1 and Ba2+"). The painted tubes `m5x-ppt-agcl-white-v1.png` and `m5x-ppt-pbi2-yellow-v1.png` stand small at the side for the two syllabus solids. A chip "expected, at 0.1 mol/L; your results are the test" sits under the plate. Hold about 2 seconds.

### 6. from results to rules
> Now turn the pattern into rules. Read **along each row** and **down each column**. The sodium row is clear in every well, so sodium compounds are soluble. The carbonate column has a solid everywhere except sodium, so carbonates are insoluble, except with sodium. Write each rule as a general statement, then the **evidence** for it, then any exception you saw. If you didn't test something, like ammonium or acetate, it isn't your conclusion. Note that it came from the reference table.

Visual: on "along each row" a pen bracket runs along the Na+ row; on "down each column" another runs down the CO3 2- column. The rule-with-evidence card writes in the right zone, two filled examples: "Rule: sodium compounds are soluble. Evidence: Na+ row, 5 of 5 clear. Exceptions: none seen." and "Rule: carbonates are insoluble except with Na+. Evidence: CO3 2- column, 4 of 5 solid; Na+ clear." On "ammonium or acetate" a separate grey box writes: "Not tested here: ammonium, acetate, nitrate as a test ion. Source: reference table." with a pen tag "not my conclusion". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 7. testing your predictions
> Then compare your twenty-five predictions with your twenty-five results. Every mismatch is either a **rule you had wrong** or a **technique problem**, and you should say which. If you predicted that barium hydroxide would precipitate, the clear well corrects you: it's an exception. A faint cloud in calcium sulfate is real, because some compounds are **slightly soluble**, a genuine third category. But a faint cloud could also be a dirty well, so **repeat** any doubtful cell in a clean one.

Visual: the predict-then-observe overlay: each cell shows its pen prediction over the well; matches get a small teal tick, mismatches a pen ring. The mismatch sorter writes in the right zone with two bins: "rule wrong" and "technique". On "barium hydroxide" the Ba2+/OH- cell (predicted "ppt", observed clear) is ringed and flies into "rule wrong" with the tag "exception: Ba(OH)2 soluble". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 8. what's left in the mixture (test the liquid)
> The syllabus also asks what's in the mixture afterwards, and you can test that. Mix lead nitrate with potassium iodide, let the yellow solid settle, and take some clear liquid from above it. Add a drop of potassium iodide: if more yellow solid forms, there were still **lead ions** left, so the lead nitrate was in excess. Add a drop of lead nitrate instead: more yellow means **iodide** was left over. The spectators, potassium and nitrate, are there either way.

Visual: the painted settled tube (`m5x-ppt-pbi2-yellow-v1.png`) centre; the C13 equation strip small: "Pb2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) → PbI2(s)". On "take some clear liquid" the graduated pipette (`m5x-graduated-pipette-v1.png`) draws off the clear layer (coded level change) and empties it into two clean wells of the plate, labelled "test A" and "test B". On "a drop of potassium iodide" a dropper bottle labelled "KI" adds a coded drop to test A; on "lead ions" a yellow cloud blooms (tag "more PbI2: Pb2+ was left over, so Pb(NO3)2 in excess"). On "a drop of lead nitrate" the "Pb(NO3)2" bottle adds a drop to test B; on "iodide" the alternative shows (tag "more PbI2: I- was left over, so KI in excess"). A small rule chip: "at most one test goes cloudy: whichever ion is left over". The C13 composition card writes on "spectators": "dissolved: K+, NO3- (spectators), plus the ion in excess". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 9. validity, reliability and accuracy
> The same three words come up in every practical question. It's **valid** because each well tests one pair, with partners that always dissolve, so a solid can only be the pair you're testing. It's **reliable** if repeated wells, and other groups, give the same results. And it's **accurate** when your observations match what's really there: a black background, clean wells and fresh solutions all help.

Visual: three cards write in turn in the right zone, each with its plain question and this investigation's answer: "Validity: does it test what it claims? One pair per well; always-soluble partners; stock check." (pen link to the grey Na+ and NO3- labels); "Reliability: same result on repeats? Repeat doubtful cells; compare groups." (two coded well rows matching); "Accuracy: observations match what's really there? Black background, clean wells, fresh solutions, compare with accepted results." (the black card half pulses). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 10. the risk assessment
> And the risk assessment. **Lead nitrate** is toxic, builds up in the body and can harm an unborn baby. **Silver nitrate** stains skin and can damage eyes. **Barium nitrate** is harmful if swallowed. **Sodium hydroxide** can harm eyes. So wear safety glasses and gloves, use only drops, never pipette by mouth, and wash your hands afterwards. Lead, silver and barium waste goes into a labelled heavy-metal container, never down the sink. And working with drops is itself a control: tiny amounts mean tiny exposure and tiny waste.

Visual: the risk table writes in the right zone, three columns "Hazard / Harm / Control", one row per cue, each hazard cell with its dropper bottle and coded label: lead nitrate: "toxic; builds up in the body; may harm an unborn baby; eye damage" / "drops only, gloves, glasses, wash hands; follow school rules (some schools use a teacher demo or supplied results)"; silver nitrate: "stains skin; damages eyes" / "gloves, glasses"; barium nitrate: "harmful if swallowed" / "no mouth pipetting, wash hands"; sodium hydroxide (and sodium carbonate): "eye damage or irritation" / "glasses". ### 13. feedback 1 (brief)
> **Potassium, nitrate, and leftover silver ions.** Adding chloride made more silver chloride, so silver ions must still have been dissolved: the silver nitrate was in excess. Potassium and nitrate are the spectators. It's not chloride: if chloride were in excess, the silver would be used up, and adding more chloride couldn't make more solid.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why nitrates and sodium salts: they always dissolve, so a solid can only be the tested pair (2). Why check each stock alone: a cloudy stock would fake a result (2). Why predict first: it turns observations into a test of the rules (3). Why the tip stays out: it would carry one solution into the other's bottle (3). Why black: a white cloud is invisible on white (3). Why concentration matters most: some compounds only just dissolve, so the amount decides whether a cloud shows (4, 15). ## Safety facts check (self-check, 18 Oct; science check to confirm)

Every hazard in scene 10 and the notes was checked against supplier safety data sheets (classifications vary a little by supplier and form; the video states only hazards common to them) and the site's safety box (lesson 16, card 3b and the precipitation safety note), which follows school-laboratory practice.

- **Lead(II) nitrate:** Fisher Scientific UK SDS (2021): H272 (oxidiser, may intensify fire), H302 + H332 (harmful if swallowed or inhaled), H318 (causes serious eye damage), H360Df (may damage the unborn child; suspected of damaging fertility), H373 (may cause damage to organs through prolonged or repeated exposure), H400 and H410 (very toxic to aquatic life, long lasting). Other suppliers add H317 or give H372. Learner copy: "toxic, builds up in the body and can harm an unborn baby" (screen adds eye damage). "Builds up in the body" matches the site ("a cumulative poison") and H373 or H372 (repeated exposure). School rules: an informal NSW teacher report says lead nitrate is permitted for Years 7 to 12 under Chemical Safety in Schools; not confirmed from an official source. Learner copy says only "follow school rules (some schools use a teacher demo or supplied results)" (site wording).
- **Silver nitrate:** oxidiser (H272), corrosive as the solid (H314: severe skin burns and eye damage), very toxic to aquatic life (H410) (Trace Sciences and Spectrum SDSs); 0.1 mol/L solutions are less severe but still eye hazards; silver nitrate stains skin dark (site: "stains skin and clothing and is corrosive to eyes"). Learner copy: "stains skin and can damage eyes".
- **Barium nitrate:** oxidiser (H272), harmful (some suppliers: toxic) if swallowed (H302 or H301), harmful if inhaled (H332), eye irritation (H319) (Sigma-Aldrich 202754 classification; Labbox and Loba Chemie SDSs). Learner copy: "harmful if swallowed" (true for every supplier wording).
- **Sodium hydroxide, 0.1 mol/L:** about 0.4 percent, below the GHS corrosive threshold; school practice requires safety glasses for any NaOH. **Sodium carbonate:** eye irritant (H319). Learner copy: "sodium hydroxide can harm eyes"; sodium carbonate on screen only.
- **Calcium nitrate:** oxidiser, harmful if swallowed, serious eye damage for the solid (supplier SDSs); covered by glasses and gloves; not given its own spoken row. Sodium chloride, iodide, sulfate and nitrate: low hazard at 0.1 mol/L.
- **Controls:** glasses and gloves, drops only, never pipette by mouth, wash hands (site); heavy-metal waste container for lead, silver and barium, never the sink (site); microscale reduces quantity handled and waste (standard green-chemistry and school practice).
- **Expected results check:** colours and solubility categories from C13's science check (`../checks/c13-claude-science-check.md`: AgCl white, PbI2 bright yellow, BaSO4 white, AgI pale yellow, Ag2CO3 pale yellow, silver with hydroxide brown Ag2O, CaSO4 and Ag2SO4 slight, Ca(OH)2 slight, Ba(OH)2 soluble) and the site matrix; the concentration-dependence of the four "faint or none" cells is in author note (a).
