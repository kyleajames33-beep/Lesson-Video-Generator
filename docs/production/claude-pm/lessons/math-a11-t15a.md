### 1. hook: one calculator angle, two times
> Here's the clock. On this clock, we measure the hand's angle from twelve, clockwise, the way it turns. After m minutes past the hour, the minute hand has turned six m degrees. But look at ten to the hour. The hand is the mirror image of ten past, and the tip is at exactly the same **height**. The calculator only ever gives one angle. So how do we find every solution of a trig equation in a **domain**?

### 2. sine equals a half: the method
> Start with sine theta equals a half, for theta from zero to three hundred and sixty degrees. On the graph, the line y equals a half crosses the sine curve twice, so expect two **solutions**. On the circle, the height a half is reached at two points. Here's the method. First, the related angle. The related angle is the acute angle between the ray and the x-axis, and an acute angle always has a positive sine. So we find it from the positive value: the inverse sine of a half is thirty **degrees**. Second, the quadrants. Sine is positive, so the point is above the x-axis, in the first or second quadrant. Third, place the angles. In the first quadrant it's thirty degrees itself. In the second, it's a hundred and eighty minus thirty, a hundred and fifty **degrees**. Both are inside the domain, and two matches the graph's count.

### 5. a radian domain and a calculator

### 6. rearrange first
> Sometimes you rearrange first. Two sine x equals one becomes sine x equals a half, so x is pi over six or five pi over six radians, the radian forms of thirty and a hundred and fifty degrees. Here's a sneakier one. Solve sine x equals cosine x, from zero to two pi. We'd like to divide both sides by cosine x, but first check that cosine x equals zero gives no solutions. There, sine x is one or minus one, not zero, so nothing is lost. Dividing gives tangent x equals **one**. The related angle is pi over four, and tangent is positive in the first and third quadrants. So x is pi over four, or pi plus pi over four, which is five pi over four **radians**. On the graph, that's exactly where the sine and cosine curves **cross**.

## Teaching checks
