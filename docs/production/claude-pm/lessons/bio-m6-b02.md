  3. Site text (review card): "a 1 Gy dose raised cancer incidence by 42% rather than 100%: ionising radiation is a mutagen ..., but cells' repair systems, identified as a Class 1 carcinogen risk by IARC in 1979, correct most damage". Main question: *What does a mutagen physically do to DNA, and why doesn't every exposure end in a mutation?*
Entry knowledge: see above. Students start Year 12 and have watched b01. They know a mutation is a change in base sequence that can make a new allele, that DNA has two complementary strands that act as templates, and that copying errors are usually repaired. They have not met how radiation or chemicals damage DNA, what "ionising" means, or why exposure raises risk rather than guaranteeing a change.
Stops after: distinguishing a mutagen (an agent that raises the chance of a DNA change) from a mutation (the change itself); explaining that damage becomes a permanent mutation only if it is copied before it is repaired, so exposure is a risk, not a guarantee; saying that UV, X-rays and gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation; explaining that UV is not ionising and joins neighbouring thymines into a dimer that distorts the DNA, mainly in skin cells; explaining that X-rays and gamma rays are ionising, break DNA strands directly and through free radicals, can cause large changes when breaks rejoin wrongly, and reach deep cells including those that make eggs or sperm; explaining three chemical routes (base analogues, base-modifying chemicals such as those in tobacco smoke, chemicals that slide between bases and shift the reading); explaining naturally occurring mutagens (background radiation from rocks and soil, radon and space; aflatoxin; viruses that insert DNA into a gene or its control region, breaking it or switching it on) and that natural does not mean harmless; applying this to an unseen UV against X-ray comparison (including which cells each affects) and to the UV yeast data, separating survival from mutation.
Handoff: what one changed base can do to a protein (route entry bio-m6-b03), then chromosomal mutations (bio-m6-b04).

### 1. hook (sunlight on skin)
> Every sunny day, ultraviolet light from the Sun damages the DNA in your skin cells, many times over in each exposed cell. Yet almost all of that damage never becomes a mutation. Your cells fix it. So what exactly does UV do to DNA? What do X-rays and some chemicals do differently? And why doesn't damage always become a mutation?

### 2. mutagen and mutation
> Start with two words that sound alike. A **mutagen** is anything that raises the chance of a change in DNA, like UV light, X-rays or certain chemicals. A **mutation** is the change itself, a different base sequence. So UV isn't a mutation. It's a mutagen. Mutations also happen without any mutagen, from rare copying mistakes. A mutagen raises that rate.

### 3. damage, repair or copy
> Here's what has to happen in between. A mutagen damages one spot on the DNA. Repair enzymes usually find the damage and fix it, using the other strand as a guide. But if the cell copies its DNA before the repair happens, the copying enzyme can put the wrong base opposite the damage. When that new strand is copied again, the wrong base gets its own matching partner. Now the change is in both strands. It's permanent, a mutation.

Visual: `RepairRace` on the strand board. On "damages one spot" one base on the top strand gets a small amber damage mark. Two paths branch below it. On "permanent, a mutation" the lower path's final strand rings amber with the label "mutation". Hold one second.

### 4. a risk, not a guarantee
> That's why exposure is a risk, not a guarantee. More exposure means more damage, so more chances that some of it gets copied before it's repaired. A stronger source, or a longer time in it, damages more spots at once, and repair can fall behind. And a mutagen can't choose where it hits, so the change could land in any gene, or in no gene at all.

### 5. electromagnetic radiation and UV
> The first group of mutagens is **electromagnetic radiation**. UV light, X-rays and gamma rays are all part of the same spectrum as visible light, but they carry more energy. The UV in sunlight can't knock electrons off atoms, but it can join two neighbouring thymine bases on the same strand, forming a **thymine dimer**. That kink distorts the DNA, so when it's copied, the wrong base can go in. UV doesn't get far into the body, so it mainly damages skin cells.

### 6. ionising radiation: X-rays and gamma rays
> X-rays and gamma rays carry much more energy. They're **ionising**, which means they can knock electrons off atoms. That can snap the DNA backbone directly, or split water molecules into reactive fragments called **free radicals**, which then attack DNA. Sometimes both strands break. If the cell joins the broken ends back wrongly, a whole piece can be lost or swapped. These rays pass deep into the body, so any cell can be hit, including cells that make eggs or sperm.

### 7. chemical mutagens
> The second group is **chemical mutagens**, and they work in three main ways. Some look so much like a normal base that the cell builds them into new DNA, but later they pair with the wrong partner. That's a **base analogue**. Some, like chemicals in tobacco smoke, attach to a base and change how it pairs. And some slide in between neighbouring base pairs, so the copying enzyme adds or skips a base, which shifts every codon after it.

### 8. naturally occurring mutagens
> The third group is **naturally occurring mutagens**, and natural doesn't mean harmless. **Background radiation**, from rocks and soil, radon gas and space, is ionising, so it can break DNA strands, just like X-rays. If it lands inside a gene, or the region that switches it on, that gene can be broken, or switched on when it shouldn't be.

### 9. one question for any mutagen
> So to explain any mutagen, ask what it physically does to DNA. UV joins neighbouring bases. Ionising radiation breaks strands. Chemicals swap in for bases, change them, or squeeze between them. Some viruses insert new DNA. And the last step is the same for all of them: the damage only becomes a mutation if it's copied before it's repaired.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Mutagen: an agent that increases the chance of a DNA change. Mutation: the change in base sequence itself.
- Damage becomes a mutation only if it is copied before it is repaired, so exposure raises risk; it does not guarantee a mutation.
- Electromagnetic radiation: UV is not ionising; it bonds neighbouring thymines (thymine dimer), kinking DNA (mainly skin cells). X-rays and gamma rays are ionising; they break DNA strands directly and through free radicals, and reach deep cells.
- Chemicals: base analogues (mimic a base, then mispair); base-modifying chemicals (e.g. in tobacco smoke); chemicals that slide between bases (add or skip a base).

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (18 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Alberts, Molecular Biology of the Cell; Griffiths et al.) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **UV damage and repair in skin.** Sunlight UV (mostly UVB for DNA damage) forms cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and 6-4 photoproducts between adjacent pyrimidines on the same strand; thymine-thymine dimers are the most common. Human cells remove them by nucleotide excision repair. Exposed skin cells receive many lesions per sunny day; the narration says "many times over" and gives no number. "Almost all of that damage never becomes a mutation": correct in the sense that repair removes most lesions; science check to confirm the wording.
- **UV not ionising.** UVA and UVB photons (about 3 to 4.4 eV) are below typical ionisation energies of biological molecules (about 10 eV and above); UV is classed as non-ionising radiation (WHO, ARPANSA). Far (extreme) UV can ionise, so Revision 2 says "The UV in sunlight can't knock electrons off atoms". Correct.
- **UV penetration.** UVB is absorbed mostly in the epidermis, UVA reaches the dermis; neither reaches internal organs. "Doesn't get far into the body ... mainly damages skin cells" and the tag "UV reaches only the outer skin": correct (eyes are also exposed; author note).
- **Electromagnetic spectrum.** UV, X-rays and gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation with higher photon energy than visible light (site correction 5). Correct.
- **Ionising radiation.** X-rays and gamma rays ionise atoms; direct ionisation of DNA breaks bonds, and radiolysis of water forms hydroxyl radicals that cause most of the DNA damage from low-LET radiation. Single- and double-strand breaks; misrepair of double-strand breaks (for example by non-homologous end joining) gives deletions and translocations. Correct.
- **Germ-line reach.** X-rays and gamma rays penetrate tissue and can reach gonads. Correct. (Human germ-line effects at atomic-bomb doses have not been detected in survivors' children; site correction 1; not spoken.)
- **Base analogue.** 5-bromouracil resembles thymine, is incorporated opposite A, and in its rarer form pairs with G, giving A-T to G-C transitions. Correct. Screen tag only.
- **Base-modifying chemicals in tobacco smoke.** Benzo[a]pyrene, after metabolic activation to its diol epoxide, binds guanine (bulky adduct), causing mostly G to T changes. Correct. Screen tag only; the narration says "chemicals in tobacco smoke".
- **Intercalating agents.** Acridines (proflavine, acridine orange) and ethidium bromide insert between stacked base pairs and cause single-base insertions or deletions (frameshifts). Correct (site correction 4). Screen tag "acridine dyes".
- **Background radiation.** ARPANSA: natural radioactivity in rocks and soil is the largest natural source of dose in Australia, together with radon (Australian indoor radon averages about 10 Bq/m3, against a world average of about 40) and cosmic rays; the Australian natural dose averages about 1.7 mSv a year. All are ionising. Radon acts mainly through alpha particles, which ionise more densely than X-rays and give clustered breaks, so Revision 2 says "can break DNA strands, just like X-rays" rather than "the same way". Granite is a common higher-uranium rock; the plate is context only.
- **Aflatoxin.** Aflatoxin B1, from *Aspergillus flavus* and *A. parasiticus* on poorly stored peanuts, maize and grains; liver P450 enzymes convert it to the 8,9-epoxide, which binds guanine at N7, causing mainly G to T changes (TP53 codon 249 in liver cancer). Revision 2 narration: "once the liver processes it, attaches to bases". Correct.
- **Inserting viruses.** Hepatitis B virus DNA can integrate into host chromosomes (not part of its normal cycle, but common in chronic infection and found in most HBV-related liver cancers, including insertions at the TERT promoter that switch the gene on); retroviruses insert as part of their cycle; HPV integration in cervical cancer. Revision 2: "that gene can be broken, or switched on when it shouldn't be" (both loss and gain of function). The plate is a generic enveloped virus; the tag names hepatitis B, which is enveloped. Correct.
- **Chemical classes as spoken.** The site's three routes (base modification, base analogue, replication interference) are kept, with intercalation as the replication-interference example. Correct.
- **Attempt 2 data.** Site sample results: 0 s 142; 15 s 98; 30 s 61; 60 s 24; 120 s 5 (mean of three plates). Drops: 44 in 0 to 15 s (2.9 per s), 37 in 15 to 30 s (2.5 per s), 37 in 30 to 60 s (1.2 per s), 19 in 60 to 120 s (0.3 per s); so "dropping fastest at first" is correct. "After two minutes" = 120 s. These are illustrative site values, not a published dataset; the card presents them as a student's results. Survival against mutation: the site's own stated limitation and standard practice (mutagenesis is scored with a selectable or visible marker). Correct.
