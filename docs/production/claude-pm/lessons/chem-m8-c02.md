### 2. measuring by mass
> This is gravimetric analysis, which just means measuring by mass. You turn the ion you want into a solid with a known formula, collect all of it, dry it, and **weigh** it. For that mass to mean something, three things need to be true. Almost all of the ion has to end up in the solid. The solid's formula has to be known. And what you weigh has to be only that solid, clean and **dry**.

Visual: heading: "Gravimetric analysis = measuring by mass". The beaker stays left. A short coded flow writes on the right: "ion in water → solid (known formula) → dry → weigh". On "weigh" `m8-balance-watch-glass-v1.png` appears small at the end of the flow. ### 3. choosing the solid
> So which solid? For sulfate, why barium and not calcium? Both sulfates have a one to one formula, so their K s p values compare directly. Calcium sulfate's is around half a million times bigger, so a lot of the sulfate would stay **dissolved** and never get weighed. Barium sulfate leaves much less dissolved, and adding barium in excess pushes that even lower. That's the common ion effect. Sodium won't work at all, because sodium sulfate is **soluble**. For chloride, the usual choice is silver, making silver **chloride**.

### 4. precipitate and digest
> Here's the method. Dissolve the sample, add dilute hydrochloric acid to remove **carbonate**, then add excess barium chloride solution. A drop more into the clear liquid on top shows whether all the sulfate is **out**. Barium chloride is toxic and silver nitrate stains skin, so wear gloves and glasses, and the residues go in heavy-metal waste. Then **digest** it: keep it warm and let it stand. Small crystals dissolve and bigger ones grow. Bigger crystals don't slip through the filter paper, and they trap fewer other **ions**.

### 5. filter and wash
> Next, filter it through a filter paper you've already dried and weighed. Rinse every bit of solid out of the beaker with distilled water from a wash bottle. Then wash the solid on the paper. Ions like chloride, and the extra barium, are still in the liquid clinging to the crystals. If they stay, they dry onto the solid and add mass that isn't barium **sulfate**.

### 6. dry to constant mass
> Now dry the paper and solid in an oven, and cool it in a desiccator, a sealed container with a drying agent, so it doesn't pick up water from the air while it **cools**. The balance is zeroed with the empty watch glass on it, so each reading is just the paper and the solid. Then weigh it, and repeat. Heat, cool, weigh, again and again, until two weighings in a row **agree**. Then further drying is no longer removing measurable water: that's constant mass. Washing still matters, though, because dry impurities would stay on the **balance** too.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Gravimetric analysis: turn the ion into a solid of known formula, then weigh it. Needs: almost all the ion in the solid; known formula; only that solid weighed, clean and dry.
2. Choose a very insoluble solid: SO₄²⁻ with Ba²⁺ (BaSO₄, Ksp 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁰, not CaSO₄, 4.93 × 10⁻⁵); Cl⁻ with Ag⁺ (AgCl). Excess reagent lowers what stays dissolved (common ion effect).
3. Method: dissolve → acidify (dilute HCl removes carbonate) → add excess reagent → digest (bigger crystals filter better, trap less) → filter (pre-weighed paper) → wash → dry → cool in a desiccator → weigh on the tared watch glass; repeat to constant mass (drying no longer removing water).
4. Calculation: m(solid) by difference → n = m ÷ M → formula ratio → m(ion) → % = m(ion) ÷ m(sample) × 100, or c = n ÷ V. Example: 0.350 g BaSO₄ from 0.500 g → 28.8% sulfate.
5. Errors: extra mass (damp, unwashed) reads high; lost solid (torn paper, incomplete precipitation) reads low. Repeating and averaging don't remove an error that is the same every time.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the mass means anything: the three requirements (2). Why barium, not calcium: far smaller Ksp for the same formula type, so almost no sulfate stays dissolved (3). Why excess reagent: common ion effect, and it ensures all the sulfate precipitates (3, 4). Why digest: bigger crystals filter and trap less (4). Why wash: soluble ions would dry on and add mass (5). Why a desiccator: no water taken up while cooling (6). Why constant mass: when readings agree, drying is no longer removing measurable water; washing, not constant mass, keeps out dry impurities (6). Why acidify: carbonate would add a second barium solid (4). Why tare the watch glass: every reading is then paper plus solid only (6). Why use the ratio: one sulfate per barium sulfate (8). Why each error has a direction: extra mass or lost solid (10). Why repeats don't help: averaging can reduce random scatter, but the same error each time stays in the average (10, feedback 2).
