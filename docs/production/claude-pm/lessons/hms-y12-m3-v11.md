### 2. Medicare: the purpose first
> Start with what **Medicare** is for. It has two aims. Your ability to get necessary medical care shouldn't depend on your ability to pay. And illness shouldn't ruin you financially. It's paid for from general tax plus an income levy. It isn't a fund you pay into and draw out of. It's today's taxes paying for today's care. That's what **universal** means here: covered because you live here and need care, not because you contributed or have a job.

### 4. bulk billing, and the gap nobody announced
> Here's how a cost barrier appears inside a universal scheme. Government sets a fee and pays a **rebate** based on it. If a doctor charges that fee and bills Medicare directly, you pay nothing. That's **bulk billing**. If they charge more, you pay the gap. Say a visit costs ninety dollars and the rebate is forty-two. The gap is forty-eight dollars. Now, the rebate only rises when government decides. If it rises more slowly than the cost of running a practice, the gap grows year after year, with no law ever announcing a fee. And it bites hardest on someone with a low income and a chronic condition who needs a visit every month, because twelve visits means five hundred and seventy-six dollars. Then the chain from last lesson runs again.

### 5. private health insurance, and the argument
> So what does private health insurance add? It pays for treatment as a private patient, which brings your choice of doctor, your choice of timing, and often a private room. Extras cover helps with dental, glasses and physio. Government encourages it with a premium rebate, a tax penalty for high earners without cover, and higher premiums for late joiners. And **community rating** means insurers must charge everyone the same, whatever their health. The case for it: it moves some demand out of public hospitals and brings private money in. The case against: that support goes mostly to people who are better off, and the same money spent on public care could buy more health. Both sides are describing the same fact. Whether it's fair is a value judgement, and saying so is the strong answer.

### 6. the NDIS and My Aged Care: funding follows the person
> Two big programs sit at the edge of Medicare, both funded nationally. The **NDIS**, the National Disability Insurance Scheme, funds supports for people with permanent and significant disability, through a plan built on the person's own goals. It's not a health scheme. It pays for daily living and independence, not treatment. **My Aged Care** is the front door to federally funded aged care: an assessment, then help at home or a place in residential care, aiming to keep people at home longer. Both attach funding to the person, not to a service. That fits support to the person's goals, but hands the work of organising providers to the person or their family. And both meet health at a seam, where people wait in hospital for a funded place.

### 7. access and equity: three gains, three shortfalls
> So what do these arrangements mean for access and equity? Three real gains. Nobody is refused emergency or public hospital care because they can't pay. Medicines stay affordable for life-long conditions. And risk is pooled, so the healthy and the wealthy help pay for the sick and the poor. Three real shortfalls. The services left outside, like dental care, have the steepest gradient. Gaps wear down universal coverage without anyone deciding to. And support for private cover follows income, not need. Which services sit inside the boundary decides who's protected, more than the total spent. And universal coverage doesn't maintain itself.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing. No official figure on screen.
- **Purposes and funding (2).** Medicare funded from general revenue and the Medicare levy (2% of taxable income), with the Medicare Levy Surcharge for higher earners without private hospital cover.
- **Inside (3).** Public patient treatment in public hospitals; Medicare Benefits Schedule rebates for out-of-hospital GP, specialist, pathology and imaging services; the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (co-payment capped, lower for concession card holders; PBS safety net); the Medicare safety nets.
- **Outside (3).** Most adult dental (Child Dental Benefits Schedule and public dental are exceptions); allied health and psychology limited to set sessions under care plans; optical largely outside (eye tests by optometrists are rebated; glasses are not; the video says "glasses"); ambulance not covered by Medicare (free for residents in Queensland and Tasmania); private hospital care.
- **Worked gap (4).** Illustrative: $90 fee, $42 rebate, gap $48; 12 × $48 = $576. Arithmetic checked. The standard GP consultation rebate was about $43 in 2025–26 (illustrative only; not stated as current).
- **Rebate indexation (4, mechanism only).** Indexation freeze 2014 to 2020; bulk-billing incentives raised in November 2023 and extended to all patients from November 2025; no trend narrated.
- **Private insurance (5).** Private health insurance rebate, Medicare Levy Surcharge, Lifetime Health Cover loading (described, not named); community rating under the Private Health Insurance Act; distribution of cover by income.
- **NDIS and My Aged Care (6).** NDIS for permanent and significant disability, reasonable and necessary supports, individual plans; jointly funded by the Commonwealth and states (said "funded nationally"). My Aged Care as the entry point: assessment, then home support (Support at Home since 1 November 2025, which replaced Home Care Packages; site correction 28) or residential care; hospital patients awaiting aged care placement.
- **Gains (7).** Public hospital treatment free regardless of means; PBS affordability; risk pooling through taxation.
