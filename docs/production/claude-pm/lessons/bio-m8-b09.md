### 4. p53: repair or self-destruct
> At that checkpoint, a protein called **p53** responds to DNA damage. It stops the cycle, so repair enzymes can fix the damage. > Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (30 Oct) against standard references. The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing, with source and date.
- **"Huge numbers of new cells" each day (scene 1).** Estimates are of the order of 300 billion cells replaced a day, mostly blood and gut lining (Sender and Milo 2021, *Nature Medicine*). No number is spoken.
- **"A few changes can slip through" each copy (scene 1).** After proofreading and mismatch repair, human DNA replication leaves roughly 1 error per 10^9 to 10^10 bases, which is a few new mutations per cell division across about 6 × 10^9 bases (diploid). Codex to confirm the wording "a few changes can slip through" is fair; fall-back "changes can slip through".
- **Cancer cells divide by mitosis (scene 2).** Correct; cancer cells often divide abnormally (unequal chromosome sharing is common), which is why scene 2 says "the main problem isn't how it divides, but when". Codex to confirm the hedge is enough.
- **Cell cycle and checkpoints (scene 3).** G1, S, G2, M; the G1/S checkpoint checks cell size, growth signals and DNA damage; the G2/M checkpoint checks that replication is complete; the spindle assembly checkpoint holds anaphase until all kinetochores are attached (matches bio-m5-b05a). Correct.
- **p53 (scene 4).** p53 accumulates after DNA damage and halts the cycle at G1/S (through p21) and can trigger apoptosis; it also acts at G2/M. "At that checkpoint" is a simplification; correct at Year 12 level. Revision 2: "responds to DNA damage", not "checks for" (p53 is not the sole sensor; science check S5, NCI TP53).
