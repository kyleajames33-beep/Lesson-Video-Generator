### 1. hook (two brothers)
> Two brothers have the same parents, yet they can look quite different. Each began from one sperm and one egg, and each of those carried just one set of chromosomes, not two. So how does a cell halve its chromosome number, and make gametes that differ from each other?

Visual: a coded family scene (`Plate` with simple figures): a mother and father at the top, each holding a small cell outline labelled "egg" and "sperm"; two brothers below with a few visibly different features (hair colour, height, freckles), drawn as coded silhouettes with simple colour differences. On "began from one sperm and one egg" a sperm and an egg path-draw from the parents and meet in a small cell (the zygote) above each brother. A scribble circle lands on a gamete on "just one set", and the tag "one set: 23 chromosomes" writes on. On "halve its chromosome number" the camera leaves the gamete and pulls back to the cell in a parent's body that makes gametes (a larger cell, tagged "before: 46 chromosomes, two sets"; this is a diploid precursor cell, not a gamete), whose nucleus shows a plum row and a teal row of bars (b05a's 23 and 23). A bracket and a flat arrow then cut away some bars from both rows, leaving a small cell tagged "gamete: 23" with a mix of plum and teal bars (a gamete carries one of each kind, from either parent). A second gamete appears beside it with a different mix of plum and teal bars on "differ from each other", and the heading writes on: "How does meiosis halve the number and make gametes different?" The question mark holds about two seconds. (Siblings are diagrammatic; the tag reads "simplified".)

### 2. why halve
> Suppose sperm and eggs were made by mitosis, like skin cells. Each would carry 46 chromosomes, so a fertilised egg would start with 92, and the next generation with 184. Gametes need one set, 23 chromosomes. A cell makes them with a different kind of division called meiosis: it copies its DNA once, then divides twice.

Visual: a body cell with a "46" ticker; an arrow labelled "mitosis" makes a sperm tagged "46" and an egg tagged "46", which join into a zygote whose ticker climbs to "92" on "92", then a daughter-generation arrow to "184" on "184", with a grey cross over the whole chain and the tag "the number would double every generation". The board wipes to the correct chain: the body cell "46" makes a sperm "23" and an egg "23" that join into a zygote "46", with a tick. On "meiosis" the word writes on in amber hand type over the arrow. On "copies its DNA once, then divides twice" a small three-beat strip draws under the arrow: a copy icon, then two division arrows ("1 copy, 2 divisions") and a tag "1 cell, 4 cells". A one-second hold.

### 4. copy once, then pair up (prophase I)
> Meiosis starts like mitosis: in interphase every chromosome is copied, so each one has two sister chromatids. Then comes the first difference. In prophase one, each chromosome finds its homologous partner, and the two lie side by side, gene against gene. That lets the cell later send one partner of each pair to each end.

Visual: the four bars each duplicate with a soft pulse into long doubled threads joined at a cream disc (b05a's look), alleles copied on both chromatids (so A appears on both plum chromatid threads, and so on), with the tag "copied once: each chromosome has 2 sister chromatids". The stage strip appears along the bottom with a divider chip "meiosis I" and a first chip "1 prophase I", with "same four stage names as mitosis" written small beside it. On "finds its homologous partner" the long teal doubled thread slides across to lie beside the long plum, with matching beads aligned (A beside a, B beside b) and a thin dotted line between the aligned beads; then the short pair does the same (`PairUp`, about three seconds). A bracket under each pair reads "homologous pair, side by side". On "send one partner of each pair to each end" two faint arrows point from the paired bars to the left and right ends of the cell. A grey inset with a flat cross: "mitosis: no pairing". A one-second hold.

### 5a. crossing over: what happens
> While they are paired, a chromatid from one chromosome and a chromatid from its partner break at the same place and swap the matching pieces. These two belong to different chromosomes, so they are called non-sister chromatids. This is crossing over.

Visual: the paired long chromosomes enlarge (camera push). On "a chromatid from one chromosome and a chromatid from its partner" the inner plum chromatid and the inner teal chromatid light in turn with scribble circles. On "break at the same place" a small amber lightning mark appears at the level between the A and B beads on both lit chromatids. On "swap the matching pieces" the two lower pieces slide across (`CrossOver`, about three seconds): the plum chromatid now has a plum top with A and a teal bottom with b; the teal chromatid has a teal top with a and a plum bottom with B; the bead letters travel with the pieces. The outer chromatids stay unchanged. On "non-sister chromatids" a bracket from one lit chromatid to the other reads "non-sister", then a second bracket across the two sister chromatids of the plum chromosome reads "sister". The words "crossing over" write on in amber. No other tags in this scene. A one-second hold.

### 5b. crossing over: what it makes
> It does not make new alleles. It makes new combinations of the alleles already there, so the four long chromatids are now all different, even the sister chromatids.

Visual: the same board. A scribble circle lands on the four long chromatids in turn with tiny tags "A B", "A b", "a B", "a b", and a bracket reads "4 different chromatids". On "does not make new alleles" the four letters pulse and a flat grey cross lands on a ghost bead labelled "new" with the tag "no new allele". A held caption beneath the board carries the side points that were spoken in revision 1: "normal and regulated, not an error", "swapped pieces keep their colour: sister chromatids now differ" and "the short pair can cross over too (not drawn)". ### 6a. metaphase I: the pairs line up

### 6b. metaphase I: chance and independent assortment
> Which partner faces which end is chance, and each pair decides on its own. Here plum long and plum short face left. Another cell could line up with plum long and teal short on the left. This is independent assortment.

Visual: the same board. ### 7. anaphase I and telophase I: the first division
> In anaphase one, the fibres pull the homologous partners apart, but the centromeres do not split, so each chromosome still has two chromatids. Each end now holds one of each kind of chromosome, which is one set. Telophase and cytokinesis finish the first division, and there are two cells, each with half the chromosome number. That is how meiosis one halves it.

Visual: chip "3 anaphase I" lands. Using line-up 1, the plum long chromosome (still doubled, two chromatids at a disc) and the plum short travel left as whole X shapes, the teal long and teal short travel right (about three seconds; the discs stay whole, a scribble circle lands on one disc with the tag "centromere stays whole: chromatids not separated"). On "one of each kind" a bracket on each end reads "long, short: one of each kind". A scribble circle on each end with the tag "one set". The chips "4 telophase I" and a cytokinesis marker land; the cell pinches in two (animal cell). Each new cell is tagged "2 chromosomes (count the centromeres)" with a pen tapping each disc, and a small grey tag "4 chromatids". A bracket pairs the parent (small, ghosted, 4 chromosomes) with the two cells and the tag "half the number: haploid (each chromosome still doubled)". A one-second hold.

### 8. meiosis II: the second division
> Each cell goes straight into the second division. A gamete cannot keep doubled chromosomes, because it would carry twice the DNA of one set, so each cell divides again to share out the two chromatids of each chromosome. In metaphase two, each chromosome lines up on its own. In anaphase two, the centromeres split and the sister chromatids are pulled apart. Because of crossing over, the two chromatids of a chromosome can differ, so the two cells get different chromosomes.

Visual: the divider chip "meiosis II" lands and chips "5 prophase II", "6 metaphase II", "7 anaphase II", "8 telophase II" appear in turn (the strip scrolls). A flat grey tag with a cross on the gap between the divisions: "no copying between the divisions". The left cell enlarges (the cell that received the plum chromosomes in line-up 1). On "each chromosome lines up on its own" its two doubled chromosomes (long, with its two different chromatids, and short) slide into a vertical line, each on its own; a grey inset beside it says "like mitosis, but only one set". On "the centromeres split" each disc pops in two and, on "pulled apart", the sister chromatids travel to opposite ends. A scribble circle on the two long chromatids carrying "A B" and "A b" has the tag "different pieces: the cells get different chromosomes". On "the two cells get different chromosomes" the cell pinches in two. Then the right cell does the same briefly in the background, sped up (about three seconds), landing in four cells at the bottom. A one-second hold.

### 9. four cells, and what crossing over adds
> So one cell has made four haploid cells, and in this cell the letters show that all four differ. Now switch crossing over off. Without it there would be only two kinds, each twice.

### 10. tracking the numbers
> Let's track the numbers in our model cell, which starts with four chromosomes. After copying, it still has four chromosomes, because we count centromeres, but now eight chromatids. After the first division, each of the two cells holds two chromosomes and four chromatids. After the second, each of four cells holds two chromosomes, and none has a sister chromatid, because the copies have been pulled apart. A human cell goes the same way, from 46 chromosomes to 23 in each of four cells.

Visual: the count tracker (`CountTracker`, as in b05a) draws on at the right of a small model cell: four columns, "start", "after copying", "after division 1: each cell", "after division 2: each cell", with a mini cell icon above each (one cell with four bars; one cell with four doubled bars; two cells each with two doubled bars; four cells each with two single bars). Column 2 ticks to 4 on "still has four" with the tag "count centromeres" and eight chromatid chips popping in on "eight chromatids", label "8". Column 3 ticks to 2 on "each of the two cells holds two chromosomes" with the chromatid row "4" on "four chromatids". ### 19. key notes (quiet, about 75 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Meiosis turns one diploid cell (two sets) into four haploid cells (one set each). The DNA is copied once, then the cell divides twice.
- Prophase I: homologous chromosomes pair up, and crossing over swaps matching pieces between non-sister chromatids. Metaphase I: homologous pairs line up in the middle, each facing either way, independently of the other pairs (independent assortment). Both make new combinations of alleles, not new alleles. Only mutation makes new alleles.
- Anaphase I: homologous partners separate and the centromeres do not split. Each cell has half the chromosome number (one set), and each chromosome still has two chromatids.
- Meiosis II: the centromeres split and the sister chromatids separate. Result: four haploid cells, each chromosome undoubled (one chromatid, one DNA molecule).
- Human cell: 46 chromosomes; after copying, 46 chromosomes and 92 chromatids; then 23 in each of two cells; then 23 in each of four. Fertilisation joins two gametes: 23 + 23 = 46.
- With n homologous pairs, assortment alone gives 2^n combinations of whole chromosomes in a gamete (crossing over ignored; human 2^23, over 8 million).
- Mitosis: one division, two cells, same number, identical (barring errors). Meiosis: two divisions, four cells, half the number, usually different.
- Meiosis I splits the homologous pairs. Meiosis II splits the sister chromatids.
