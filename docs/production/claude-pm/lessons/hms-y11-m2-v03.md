### 2. motion, and three laws
> **Linear motion** is the whole body travelling one way. **Angular motion** is rotation about an axis, and every joint action is angular. Most movement is **general motion**, both at once: your limbs rotate at the joints, and that carries your body forward. A body keeps doing what it's doing until a force acts, so a moving player is hard to stop. Force equals mass times acceleration, so to accelerate harder, push harder or carry less. And every action has an equal and opposite reaction. A sprinter pushes back into the blocks, and the ground pushes them forward.

### 3. the body's levers
> The bone is the bar, the joint is the pivot, called the **fulcrum**, the muscle's pull is the **effort**, and the load is the **resistance**. Whatever sits in the middle names the class. Fulcrum in the middle is first class, like nodding your head. Resistance in the middle is second class, like rising onto your toes. Effort in the middle is third class, like a biceps curl, and that's most of the body. Here the biceps pulls five centimetres from the elbow, holding fifty newtons thirty-five centimetres out. It needs seven times that force, three hundred and fifty newtons. What it buys is speed and range at the hand.

### 4. balance, and five factors
> Next, balance. Your **centre of gravity** is the point where your mass is evenly spread. The **line of gravity** drops straight down from it. ### 6. force: make it, aim it, absorb it
> Force raises three questions: make it, aim it, absorb it. To make the most force, use **summation of forces**: body parts in sequence, largest to smallest, each starting as the last one peaks, like legs, hips, trunk, then arm in a throw. To aim it, think about where it's applied. Through the centre gives straight-line motion, and off centre gives spin. And to absorb it safely, spread it over a longer time and a larger area. Bending your knees as you land buys time, using those eccentric contractions. Landing on the whole foot spreads the area.

### 8. safe lifting is a torque problem
> Now the lift. The danger is **torque**, the turning effect of a force, which is the force times its distance from the pivot. Take a ten-kilogram box, about a hundred newtons. Held against your body, twenty centimetres from your lower spine, that's twenty newton metres. At arm's length, sixty centimetres out, it's sixty, three times the turning effect on your lower back. So keep the load close. Bending your knees is what lets you get close, so the torque stays small. Then the gluteus maximus and quadriceps do the work across big synovial joints. Keep your feet apart, so the line of gravity stays inside the base. And step to turn, rather than twist.

### 9. squatting and walking
> The same principles make squatting and walking safer. In a squat, keep the line of gravity over the middle of the foot, and let the knees track over the toes, so the hinge isn't pushed sideways. In walking, the heel lands first and the foot rolls through to the toes, while the ankle, knee and hip bend slightly, spreading the impact over more time.

### 10. efficiency, and specific needs
> Biomechanics also makes movement more **efficient**, which means getting the result for the least energy. For a wheelchair user, the seat and axle position set the angle at which the arm meets the push rim. Set well, the shoulder works in its middle range, the deltoid and triceps push harder each stroke, fewer strokes are needed, and the shoulder takes less repeated strain. A running prosthesis is shaped to store energy on landing and return it at push-off. The principles don't change. Only the body parts available to apply them do.

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Forearm lever (3).** Biceps insertion about 4 to 5 cm from the elbow axis; load in the grip about 30 to 35 cm; 50 N × 35 cm = 350 N × 5 cm = 1,750 N cm (`solveBeam`). Shortening 2 cm at the tendon gives about 14 cm at the grip (ratio 7). Illustrative geometry; reviewer to confirm "about" is fair.
- **Lever classes (3).** First class: atlanto-occipital joint nodding the head; second class: rising onto the toes (the common school model, ball of the foot as fulcrum); third class: elbow flexion. "Most body levers are third class": standard.
- **Lifting torque (8).** 10 kg ≈ 98 N, rounded to "about a hundred newtons"; 100 N × 0.20 m = 20 N m; × 0.60 m = 60 N m; ratio 3. Distances illustrative (horizontal distance from the load to the lumbar spine). The page's hedge kept: no claim that one posture is safe or that lumbar flexion causes injury.

- **Stability factors (4).** As the site table; standard.
