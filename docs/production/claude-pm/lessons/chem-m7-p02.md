- **Choice of samples (route review, recorded):** cyclohexane and cyclohexene, as the route review names them. They also make the layer trap visible (both are less dense than water and do not mix with it), and cyclohexane carries the c02 and c03 point that a ring fits CnH2n without a double bond. - **The layer trap (new teaching, not on the site):** when cyclohexane is shaken with bromine water, the bromine moves into the hydrocarbon layer, which turns orange while the water layer goes pale. Students often record this as "decolourised". The site does not mention it. It is the main practical skill this video adds (scene 5, memory aid, attempt 1), and it is why the control tube is there.
- **Substitution as a teacher demonstration (C7-IQ3-02):** students "investigate" substitution through a teacher demonstration in a fume cupboard: bromine dissolved in cyclohexane stays orange in the dark; under a UV lamp the colour fades slowly and the gas at the mouth turns damp blue litmus red (hydrogen bromide). Cyclohexane has twelve equivalent hydrogens, so one monobromo product forms (bromocyclohexane), which keeps the equation clean. Pure or concentrated bromine is fatal if inhaled (H330), so this is never a student bench activity. Chem-m7-c04b showed the same contrast with hexane in its hook; this video adds the litmus evidence and the equation for the ring.

- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Boiling points: cyclohexane 80.7 °C, cyclohexene 83.0 °C ("about two degrees apart"); densities about 0.78 and 0.81 g/mL, both less dense than water and immiscible with it, so they form the top layer. (b) Amounts: 1.0 mL cyclohexene is about 0.81 g, 0.0099 mol (M = 82.14 g/mol). 2.0 mL of dilute bromine water at a typical school strength of about 0.01 to 0.02 mol/L holds 0.00002 to 0.00004 mol Br₂; even saturated bromine water (about 0.2 mol/L) holds about 0.0004 mol. The alkene is in large excess in every case, so the colour goes completely. (c) Products: with cyclohexene in excess as a separate layer, most bromine moves into the organic layer and reacts there to give 1,2-dibromocyclohexane (trans); in the water layer some 2-bromocyclohexan-1-ol (a bromo-alcohol) can also form. The HSC equation is C₆H₁₀ + Br₂ → C₆H₁₀Br₂; the narration says "each of its carbons gains a bromine atom" (chem-m7-c04a author note d). (d) Partition: bromine's distribution between a hydrocarbon and water strongly favours the hydrocarbon (a ratio of the order of 20 to 30 for alkanes and tetrachloromethane), so with 1.0 mL of cyclohexane and 2.0 mL of water about nine tenths of the bromine moves into the top layer: "most of it moves up". The water layer stays pale yellow. (e) Light: at room temperature in normal indoor light, cyclohexane and bromine water show no visible change within the few minutes of the test; in bright sunlight a slow substitution can begin in the organic layer. Hence "normal room light, away from sunlight" and "read at once". (f) Demonstration: a few drops of bromine solution in cyclohexane in a clear glass tube, in a fume cupboard; under a UV lamp the colour fades over minutes and HBr is given off, which turns damp blue litmus red and fumes in moist air (c04b author note c). All twelve hydrogens of cyclohexane are equivalent, so the monobromo product is bromocyclohexane only (further substitution is possible with excess bromine; not mentioned). (g) Alkynes: electrophilic addition of bromine to an alkyne is usually slower than to a comparable alkene; the narration says "usually more slowly". (h) Other compounds that decolourise bromine water: phenols (substitution, often with a white precipitate), aldehydes (oxidation), some other reducing agents; not named in narration ("some other kinds of compounds"), named on a grey screen tag "e.g. phenols, some aldehydes". (i) Significant figures: the volumes are measured with a graduated pipette, written 1.0 mL and 2.0 mL; no calculation is made. (j) Pronunciation: "cyclohexane" SY-clo-HEX-ane; "cyclohexene" SY-clo-HEX-een; "one, two-dibromocyclohexane" as "one two di-BROH-mo-SY-clo-hex-ane"; "bromocyclohexane"; "pent-one-ene"; "cyclopentane"; "one-bromoheptane"; "H Br" as letters; "C five H ten" as letters and numbers.

### 1. hook (one colour vanishes, one only seems to)
> Here are two clear liquids, cyclohexane and **cyclohexene**. Both have a ring of six carbons, and they boil about two degrees apart. Shake each with orange bromine water. In one tube, the colour **vanishes**. In the other, the water goes pale too, but look closer: the orange has moved into a **layer** on top. So how do you run this test fairly, and what does the result really tell you?

### 2. what the colour change means

By the CLP mixture rules from bromine's classification, a saturated solution (about 3.4 % by mass, about 0.2 mol/L) would be at least a skin irritant and seriously damaging to the eyes, and harmful if inhaled; the dilute solutions most schools use (about 0.01 to 0.02 mol/L, about 0.2 to 0.3 %) fall below most generic cut-offs, but still release bromine vapour that irritates the eyes, skin and lungs. Learner copy: "gives off bromine vapour, which irritates the eyes and lungs"; the table adds "skin" and the footer points to the supplier SDS. No claim that it is harmless, and no claim that dilute bromine water is "corrosive and toxic" (site correction 3). Science check to confirm the saturated-solution mixture classification and a current supplier SDS for a school-strength solution.
- **Products:** 1,2-dibromocyclohexane and bromocyclohexane are brominated organic liquids, irritants on supplier SDSs (skin and eye irritation); learner copy says only "the products contain bromine" and sends them to halogenated organic waste. Science check to confirm the supplier H-statements before any are printed on screen (none are printed in this revision).
- **Hydrogen bromide (demonstration):** a corrosive, acidic gas (anhydrous HBr: H314, H335); formed in small amounts inside the fume cupboard; the litmus test is done there.
- **UV lamp:** UV radiation damages the eyes and skin; the lamp is shielded and never looked at directly; teacher operated.
- **Stoppered volatile liquids:** vapour pressure can push out a stopper or spray on release; shake gently and release pointing away from people, inside the fume cupboard.
- **Disposal:** all residues (brominated products, unreacted hydrocarbons, bromine water layers) into the labelled organic waste container, the halogenated one where the school separates them; never the sink (cyclohexane H410, cyclohexene H411; flammable vapour in drains).
- **Eye protection:** safety glasses or goggles; gloves (nitrile) for bromine water splashes; follow the school's risk assessment.
