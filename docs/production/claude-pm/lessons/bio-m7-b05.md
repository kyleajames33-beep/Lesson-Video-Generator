### 6. the firebreak
> The boldest response is the **hypersensitive response**. Cells at and around the infection kill themselves, fast, on purpose. ### 7. the evidence: two banksias
> So what happens in real banksias, and does timing matter? In a 1998 study, researchers infected banksia seedlings with Phytophthora's swimming spores and watched under a microscope. In scarlet banksia, from Western Australia, root tips were dead within a day, and the damage reached the seedling's stem by day five. Callose was laid down late. > Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (22 Oct) against the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Boot-cleaning stations and track closures.** Hygiene infrastructure (boot-cleaning stations, track and vehicle restrictions, especially when soils are wet) is used in WA's south-west parks and reserves to limit *P. cinnamomi* spread (DBCA dieback management; Dieback Working Group; site L08 dieback box). Correct.
- **About 40 per cent susceptible.** About 2,300 of about 5,710 described plant species of the South West Botanical Province are susceptible (about 40 per cent; Shearer, Crane and Cochrane 2004, *Australian Journal of Botany* 52: 435 to 443; DBCA). The narration says "about 40 per cent of the region's native plant species are susceptible", not "killed" (site error 2). Science check to confirm the citation.
- **Oomycete.** *P. cinnamomi* is an oomycete (Stramenopiles), with cell walls mainly of cellulose and beta-glucans, motile biflagellate zoospores released from sporangia in wet soil; zoospores are attracted (chemotaxis) to root exudates, encyst behind the root tip (zone of elongation), germinate and penetrate; hyphae colonise cortex and vascular tissue, killing fine roots and causing root rot; above-ground symptoms (chlorosis, wilting, dieback) follow from root loss. Hemibiotrophic (a biotrophic phase in living cells, then necrotrophic). Spread by water and by movement of infested soil on footwear, vehicles and machinery. Correct as narrated.
- **Origin.** Considered introduced to Australia (probably post-European settlement in the south-west), with a suspected origin in the region of Papua New Guinea, Taiwan or Southeast Asia (threat abatement plan background, 2018); debated for eastern Australia, where Shepherd (1975), Pratt and Heather (1973) and Arentz (2017) argued for a much longer presence, and greater tolerance in some eastern species has been read as a sign of longer local exposure. Revision 2 narration hedges the premise: "this pathogen probably arrived from overseas, so neither banksia evolved alongside it for long" (science check finding 1). Author note: origin debated for eastern Australia (TAP 2018 background; Arentz 2017).
- **Root barriers and entry (corrected in Revision 2, science check finding 3).** In the young root (the zone of elongation, where zoospores encyst) the epidermis is thin-walled; suberin lamellae and lignin develop in the exodermis and endodermis as the root matures, and periderm (bark) forms on older roots and stems. Narration: "As a root ages, its outer cell walls are toughened with lignin and other waterproof materials, and older roots and stems grow bark"; the young part "has thin walls and no bark". *Phytophthora* germ tubes typically enter between epidermal cells along the anticlinal walls and grow between cells, later forming haustoria in cortical cells; direct penetration of a cell also occurs, so the narration says "usually pushes in where two cells meet" (Hardham, ANU; Hardham and Blackman 2018).
- **Induced defences.** Callose (beta-1,3-glucan) papillae deposited between the cell wall and plasma membrane at penetration sites; lignification of nearby walls; phenolics and phytoalexins (antimicrobial compounds synthesised after attack). "A plug made of a sugar chain, on the inside of its wall": correct. Phytoalexins "poison or slow the pathogen": correct.
- **Hypersensitive response.** Rapid, localised programmed cell death at the infection site, usually triggered when R proteins recognise pathogen effectors; restricts pathogens that need living cells (biotrophs, hemibiotrophs in their biotrophic phase). "*Phytophthora* feeds on living cells at first": correct (hemibiotroph). "A signal can also travel through the plant and prepare distant tissue": systemic acquired resistance (salicylic acid dependent) in many plants; not shown specifically for *Banksia*, so the narration does not claim it for banksia.
- **Tynan et al. 1998.** As quoted in Decisions (Crossref abstract). The narration's "root tips were dead within a day" (within 24 h), "the damage reached the seedling's stem by day five" (hypocotyls by day 5), "callose was laid down late" (callose deposition delayed in *B. coccinea*), "root damage only started on day three, and by day nine it rarely reached halfway up the root" (*B. serrata* and *B. spinulosa* var. *collina*), and "in hairpin banksia the threads took longer to get in" (penetration delayed in *B. spinulosa* var. *collina*): match the abstract. "The microscope work used seedlings a few weeks old" (2 to 3 weeks old, in vitro). The paper's tolerance ranking also used 10-month seedlings inoculated with colonised millet or zoospores: *B. coccinea* moderately susceptible (not the most susceptible; *B. hookeriana* and *B. ashbyi* were); *B. serrata* tolerant. Calling scarlet banksia the one that "dies" is fair for the in vitro test. **Revision 2 claims only what the abstract reports:** timing of necrosis, delayed callose in *B. coccinea*, delayed penetration in *B. spinulosa* var. *collina*. The abstract reports nothing about lignin, phytoalexins or the hypersensitive response in banksias and draws no "timing decides" conclusion, so scene 7 asks "what happens in real banksias", scene 8 says "Both species laid down callose. The clearest difference is timing", the row ladders are gone, hairpin banksia's delayed entry is not attributed to any named barrier, and note 4 says "Timing differed". Common names: *B. coccinea* scarlet banksia (south-west WA); *B. serrata* old man banksia (east coast, including NSW); *B. spinulosa* hairpin banksia (*collina* variety, eastern Australia). The science check should read the full paper if available to confirm nothing in the results contradicts the abstract's summary.

- **Phosphite.** Phosphite (phosphonate) is applied as a foliar spray or stem injection in WA conservation management; it suppresses *P. cinnamomi* directly and enhances host defence responses; it does not eradicate the pathogen from soil.
