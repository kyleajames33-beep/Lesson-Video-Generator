### 2. the energy systems set the rules
> Recall from Year 11: the faster an energy system, the less it can supply in total. Anaerobic training isn't just hard training. It stresses one chosen pathway, and that depends on two numbers: how long each effort lasts, and how long the recovery is. Up to about ten seconds of maximal work, the ATP-PC system dominates, and stored phosphocreatine runs low. It takes minutes to refill, so a long rest keeps the next effort maximal. From about ten seconds to two minutes, anaerobic glycolysis dominates, and hydrogen ions build up and interfere with contraction. Here the recovery is deliberately incomplete, so the next effort starts already disturbed. Beyond two minutes, the aerobic system takes over. So the rest is part of the prescription. Change it, and you change which system you train.

### 3. anaerobic interval, HIIT and SIT
> These three look alike, but they're different prescriptions. **Anaerobic interval training** uses near-maximal efforts of about ten to ninety seconds, resting three to five times as long. It trains glycolytic capacity and tolerance of that disturbance, for events like the eight hundred metres. **HIIT**, high intensity interval training, uses efforts of about thirty seconds to four minutes, with only one to one or one to two, so recovery stays incomplete. That keeps oxygen demand high, so it trains anaerobic capacity and aerobic power together, for team sports like hockey. **SIT**, sprint interval training, uses all-out efforts of six to thirty seconds with about one to eight or longer, so recovery is nearly complete and every effort stays maximal. It trains peak power, for rugby or any repeated-sprint sport. So the first athlete did HIIT, the second did SIT. Read the ratio, and you can name the method.

### 4. plyometrics: train the transition
> **Plyometrics**, like bounding and depth jumps, work through the **stretch-shortening cycle**. A muscle that's stretched quickly under load, the eccentric action you met in Year 11, then shortens straight away, produces more force than shortening alone. The tendon stores elastic energy as it stretches and returns it, and the quick stretch triggers a reflex that boosts muscle activation. Both need a fast switch. The pause between landing and take-off is the **amortisation** phase, and if it's long, the stored energy is lost as heat. So prescribe them by ground contacts, early in a session, with full rest, on a surface with some give. Tired plyometrics are ineffective and risky. And they come after a strength base and good landing control.

### 5. resistance training: one exercise, four prescriptions
> Resistance training means working muscles against a load, and the exercise matters far less than how it's loaded. Take one back squat, with a one-rep max of a hundred kilograms. For maximal strength, lift eighty-five per cent or more, for one to five reps, resting three to five minutes. For **hypertrophy**, which means muscle growth, lift sixty-five to eighty-five per cent, six to twelve reps, resting about a minute. For power, lift thirty to sixty per cent, as fast as possible, for three to six reps, with long rests. Long rest protects the quality of each lift, so it suits strength and power. Short rest builds fatigue, so it suits growth and endurance. So describe a resistance session by load, reps, sets and rest, not by naming the exercise.

### 6. assessing relevance: the demand-profile routine
> The verb is assess, so listing methods earns little. Use three steps. First, describe the sport's **demand profile**: how long a decisive effort lasts, how often it's repeated, and how much recovery there is. Second, choose the method whose work and rest match that profile. Third, name the **trade-off**, because every method costs something. A rugby winger makes repeated five-to-fifteen-second sprints with long gaps, so SIT plus resistance, but it's tiring, so keep it away from match day. An eight-hundred-metre runner needs anaerobic intervals, which are brutal, so limit how often. And our track cyclist? Under forty seconds all-out, so SIT and heavy, fast lifting. Overall, relevance is high, but it depends on the sport and the athlete. Beginners and growing athletes don't start here, and every method relies on an aerobic base to recover.

- **Stretch-shortening cycle (4).** Elastic energy stored in the tendon and returned; the stretch reflex increasing activation; a long amortisation phase dissipates stored energy as heat. Illustrative contact times 0.18 s and 0.45 s.
