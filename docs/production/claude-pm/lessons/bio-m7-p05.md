## Decisions

- **Main teaching move:** a puzzle. Three antibiotic discs on one lawn: A clears a 25 mm zone, B 15 mm, C nothing. Is A the best medicine, and is C useless? Neither follows. The video explains why a zone forms (the drug diffuses and thins), builds the fair test, then returns to the puzzle with the honest reading (scenes 9, 10): the plate compares how well each drug stopped this bacterium on agar under these conditions; zone size also depends on dose and diffusion; C may target a part this bacterium lacks; and treating a person needs the drug to reach the infection safely, which only clinical evidence shows. The general move students keep (shared with p02): claim only what the plate measured.
- **The investigation.** A school disc-diffusion comparison. Aim: compare how well three antibiotics stop one harmless bacterium growing on agar. Organism: a supplied risk group 1 teaching culture (not named in learner copy; author note: school suppliers commonly provide *Escherichia coli* K-12, *Micrococcus luteus* or *Bacillus subtilis*; Codex to confirm against NSW school guidance). IV: the antibiotic in the disc (commercial discs A, B and C, each with a fixed dose printed by the supplier). DV: zone of inhibition diameter (mm), measured through the base across the disc's centre, two diameters at right angles averaged; "no zone" recorded as the disc diameter, 6 mm. Controlled: culture and volume spread (0.1 mL, one suspension), agar batch and depth (same volume poured per plate), disc size (6 mm) and supplier, disc spacing (at least 2 cm apart and from the edge), incubation 28 °C (within 25 to 30 °C) for 48 h, inverted, measuring method. Negative control: a blank sterile disc dipped in sterile water. Replicates: three plates, each carrying all four discs, rotated so each disc sits in a different position on each plate (the painted zones plate is shown rotated 0°, 90° and 180°, uniform rotation only). Positive control: not used as a separate plate, because disc A's zone shows the lawn and conditions allow inhibition to be seen and the lawn itself shows the agar supports growth (author note; the learning review may ask for one).
- **Constructed data (labelled "example data" on screen):** A 24, 26, 25 mm (mean 25); B 15, 14, 16 mm (mean 15); C 6, 6, 6 mm (no zone); blank 6, 6, 6 mm. Checked under "Numbers to verify".
- **Why zone size is not a ranking of medicines (scene 9; author note for the science check).** Diameter depends on the drug's activity against the organism, the disc content (dose), the drug's diffusion through agar (molecular size, solubility, charge), agar depth and composition, inoculum density, and incubation time and temperature. Clinical laboratories therefore compare each drug's zone with that drug's own published breakpoints for that organism (CLSI or EUCAST tables on standardised Mueller-Hinton agar), never one drug's zone with another's. Vancomycin, a large glycopeptide, diffuses slowly and gives small zones against susceptible organisms; the narration says "a large molecule spreads slowly and can make a small zone even when it works well" and names no drug. C's absent zone: an antibiotic with no target in the organism (for example a drug acting on a wall structure that the strain lacks, or one that cannot cross a Gram-negative outer membrane) gives no zone; the narration says "might mean this bacterium simply lacks the part C targets", which recalls b11's targets and stays conditional. Screen tag: "clinical labs compare each drug with its own cut-off, on standard agar".
- **Plate is not a patient (scene 10).** Reaching infected tissue at an effective concentration, safety at that dose, the actual pathogen (not a teaching strain), and evidence from clinical trials (b11 scene 10) are recalled in two sentences, then the narrow conclusion. This is the route's stop.

> A student put four paper discs on a plate spread with bacteria. Two days later, around disc A, a clear zone twenty five millimetres across. Around disc B, fifteen. Around disc C, **nothing**. ### 2. what a zone is
> Each disc holds a measured dose of an antibiotic. On the agar, the drug **diffuses** outwards, so it's strongest near the disc and weaker further out. Where it's strong enough to stop the bacteria growing, the agar stays clear. That clear ring is the **zone of inhibition**. At its edge, the drug has become too dilute, and beyond it the bacteria grow into a cloudy layer.

### 9. what zone size depends on
> So, on this agar, A stopped this bacterium more than B did. But zone size doesn't depend only on the drug's power. And C's blank result might mean this bacterium simply lacks the part C **targets**.

### 10. a plate isn't a patient
> A plate also isn't a patient.
