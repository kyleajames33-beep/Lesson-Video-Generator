### 1. hook (two metres in a tennis ball)
> In the copying video we saw billions of letters packed into a nucleus. How? If you could unwind the DNA in just one of your cells, you would have a thread about two metres long. It sits in a nucleus about six thousandths of a millimetre across. Blow that nucleus up to the size of a tennis ball, and the thread would be more than ten kilometres long. So how does a cell store DNA this long and still use it? And does a bacterium, which has no nucleus at all, do it differently?

Visual: the opening line recalls B4 over a quick flash of the helix and the tag "billions of letters". A coded human cell (or painted animal cell plate, if available) centred with a scribble circle on the nucleus. A plum thread draws out of the nucleus and keeps drawing across the full width of the screen with the tag "about 2 metres" on "two metres". On "six thousandths of a millimetre" a tiny ring around the nucleus carries the tag "nucleus: about 6 micrometres (0.006 mm)". On "Blow that nucleus up" the camera zooms and the nucleus morphs into a tennis ball (painted or coded) with the tag "enlarged about 11,000 times"; the thread beside it is drawn running off the edge of the screen and then, in a quick zoom-out strip, past a row of landmarks until a tag reads "more than 10 km (a long walk)". The question mark holds about two seconds.

### 2. two kinds of cell
> Every cell stores its genes in DNA, the same double helix you met earlier. What differs is where the DNA is kept and what shape it takes. A eukaryote, like an animal or plant cell, keeps its DNA inside a nucleus, an inner compartment with a membrane around it. A prokaryote has no nucleus. Bacteria are the prokaryotes we will use as our example.

Visual: the split board holds. The helix (`Helix`) draws on in the centre on "the same double helix" and the tag "same four bases, A, T, C, G" writes on; the helix then fades and the same DNA appears in each cell, drawn in again as a plum thread (the helix does not split or copy, so it cannot be read as replication), tagged "the same DNA in both cells", with the plum thread colour fixed from here on. On "where the DNA is kept" scribble circles land on the interior of each cell in turn. ### 3. the bacterium: a ring in the nucleoid
> Start with a bacterium. In most bacteria the main DNA is one long molecule whose two ends are joined, so it forms a ring, which we call a circular chromosome. With no nucleus around it, the ring lies in one region of the cell called the nucleoid, a word that means nucleus-like. The nucleoid has no membrane. It is simply the part of the cell where the DNA sits.

Visual: the camera pushes into the bacterium (painted rod-shaped plate with the DNA left out; the DNA is coded over it). The long plum thread draws as a line across the cell with its two ends marked by amber dots; on "whose two ends are joined" the ends travel together and fuse into a ring with a click, and the tag "circular chromosome: a ring" writes on. On "lies in one region" the ring settles into a pale patch of the cytoplasm and a scribble circle lands on the patch; on "nucleoid" the word writes on in amber with the gloss "nucleus-like" (the same word family as "eukaryote: true nucleus", shown as a pair). The right half of the board dims to about 30 per cent. On "has no membrane" a grey cross lands on a ghost dashed ring and the tag "no membrane around it" writes on. A one-second hold.

### 4. not floating loose: coiling
> This ring is far longer than the cell. Stretched out, it would be about one and a half millimetres, several hundred times the length of the cell. So it is not floating loose. Enzymes twist the ring, the way twisting a loop of rubber band makes it coil up on itself, and proteins grip the coiled ring at many points to hold it compact. That packs the long ring into the nucleoid without any membrane.

Visual: the right half stays dimmed (about 30 per cent). The ring (still in the bacterium) swells out to a giant loop that is drawn crossing the cell wall and running far outside the screen's cell; a `ScaleBar` beside it reads "ring about 1.5 mm; cell about 2 to 4 micrometres: several hundred times (about 400 to 800)". This is the only ratio on screen in this scene; the human comparison is made in scene 6. The narration and screen use the same value, 1.5 mm. On "Enzymes twist the ring" a small hand-drawn rubber-band loop appears beside the cell (tag "analogy: twisting a loop of rubber band") and twists; the cell's ring then twists in the same way and folds into coils (`Coil`), and small sand-coloured blobs (proteins) snap onto the coils at many points and grip them, with the tag "held compact by proteins". It is one picture: a twisted ring held at points, with no separate loops drawn. The coiled ring fits the nucleoid patch. A flat grey cross on a tangled-spaghetti ghost carries the tag "not floating loose". A one-second hold.

### 5. plasmids
> Many bacteria also carry plasmids: small, separate rings of DNA, usually much smaller than the main chromosome. The cell copies a plasmid separately from the main chromosome, and a plasmid often carries extra genes, such as one that makes the bacterium resistant to an antibiotic. You saw bacteria pass DNA to each other in conjugation. Plasmids are often what they pass on.

Visual: two or three small plum rings appear in the bacterium, outside the nucleoid patch; a scribble circle lands on them with the tag "plasmid: small, separate ring". A grey screen tag at the bottom: "plasmids are also used as tools in biotechnology, later". A one-second hold.

### 6. the eukaryotic cell: linear chromosomes in a nucleus
> Now a eukaryotic cell. Its DNA is inside the nucleus, and it is not one ring. It is split into several chromosomes, and each is one DNA molecule with two ends, so we call them linear. In a human body cell you counted 46 in mitosis. Together they hold those two metres of DNA, about six billion pairs of bases. That is why the packing matters so much here.

Visual: the camera moves to the right cell, a coded cell cutaway (or painted plate) with the nucleus enlarged on the board. Plum rods (long and short, drawn as single threads with a pair of amber dots at each end) fill the nucleus; on "one ring" a ghost ring flashes grey and fades. On "several chromosomes" the rods are counted by a pen tap (a ticker runs to 46 for a human cell, with a model-nucleus fall back of 8 rods shown, tag "model: 8 shown; human: 46"). On "one DNA molecule with two ends" one rod is lit and its two ends flash with the gloss "linear: two ends". On "those two metres" the rods straighten end to end into the long thread from the hook with the tag "all 46 together: about 2 metres". On "that is why the packing matters" a screen line writes on beside the thread and the nucleus ring: "2 m of DNA in a nucleus about 6 micrometres across" (this is where the human comparison lives; no bacterial bar and no extra ratio). The tag "about 6 billion base pairs (pairs of bases)" matches the spoken "about six billion pairs of bases". The left half of the board is dimmed. A one-second hold.

### 7a. why the thread winds
> A nucleus holds that much DNA because the thread is wound and folded. Loose, two metres of thread would be a tangle that could neither fit nor be found when needed. The first level of packing is that each chromosome's DNA wraps around groups of proteins called histones, like thread round a spool. DNA carries a negative charge and histones carry a positive one, and opposite charges attract, so the thread clings to them.

Visual: the camera zooms into one rod; the left half of the board is dimmed. A painted spool and thread plate (sewing spool with thread, tagged "analogy: thread on a spool") appears beside it for one beat and fades. On "wraps around groups of proteins called histones" sand-coloured discs (`Spool`) appear along the plum thread and the thread winds round each one in turn (`WindOnSpools`, about three seconds), the thread between the discs staying straight. Small "-" signs on the thread and "+" signs on the discs pulse on "negative charge" and "positive one", with the tag "opposite charges attract". A grey screen tag at the lower right: "histone: a packaging protein". A one-second hold.

### 7b. nucleosome and chromatin
> One spool of DNA and histones is called a nucleosome. The whole beaded thread is chromatin, and the cell folds it further.

Visual: the hand-drawn spool plate fades out as the wound discs from 7a stay on screen. On "nucleosome" a scribble circle lands on one wound disc with the word writing on in amber and the gloss "DNA wound on histones"; on "chromatin" a bracket along the whole beaded thread writes "chromatin"; on "folds it further" the beaded thread folds into a looser coil that is much shorter than the thread, with the tag "wound on histones, then folded further". Winding on histones alone shortens the DNA only about six to seven times, so the screen does not claim it fits the nucleus by itself. A one-second hold.

### 8a. loosen to read a gene
> Winding creates a problem, because a gene can only be read, meaning the cell uses its instructions, when the cell can reach it. So the winding is adjustable. Chemical tags on the histones let the cell loosen the thread where it needs to read a gene, and tighten it elsewhere. Some tags weaken a histone's positive charge, so it grips the DNA less tightly.

Visual: the beaded thread loosens and tightens in a short loop (`WindOnSpools` reversing) while a pen taps a stretch of thread marked with a small amber gene bead; on "loosen the thread where it needs to read a gene" the stretch around the gene opens (the discs slide apart, the thread straightens around the bead) with a reader icon (a magnifying glass) reaching in; the tag "gene reachable". On "tighten it elsewhere" the rest of the beaded thread contracts. On "weaken a histone's positive charge" the "+" sign on one histone fades and the thread around it loosens, with the tag "weaker + : looser grip". A grey screen tag: "histone tag: a small chemical group added to a histone", and an amber gloss on the gene bead: "read a gene = use its instructions (next video)". The handoff to B6b (how a gene is read) is made here. A one-second hold.

### 8b. tight for division
> Before a cell divides, the chromatin is wound far more tightly, into the short, thick chromosomes you saw in mitosis. That shortens it thousands of times, so the chromosomes can be moved without tangling.

### 9. DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts
> A eukaryotic cell keeps a little DNA outside the nucleus too. Mitochondria, where cells release energy from food, each hold many copies of a small ring of DNA, and so do chloroplasts, where plants carry out photosynthesis. Why a ring? Mitochondria and chloroplasts came from bacteria that began living inside larger cells, and they still carry a bacterium's kind of DNA. In our cells, these rings are extra to the 46 chromosomes in the nucleus.

Visual, one idea per beat: (1) the right cell switches to a cutaway of an animal cell (painted plate; nucleus empty of chromosomes) with oval mitochondria outside the nucleus; a zoom into one mitochondrion shows a small plum ring inside it, then a few more copies fade in beside it with the tag "many copies (one ring drawn for clarity)". (4) The animal cell returns on screen before "In our cells", and on "extra to the 46" the nucleus's 46-ticker holds in the corner with a plum ring added and the tag "46 is the nucleus count only". The mitochondrial figure (about 16,600 base pairs, 37 genes) is optional screen-only context and is not spoken. A one-second hold.

### 10. side by side
> Put it side by side. Bacterial DNA is usually one main ring in the nucleoid with no membrane, coiled and held by proteins, plus plasmids. Eukaryotic DNA is several linear chromosomes in a nucleus, wound on histones as chromatin, plus small rings in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Both are made of the same double helix with the same four bases. The difference is the arrangement.

Visual: `CompareTable` builds on the fixed split board (column headings "typical prokaryote (e.g. a bacterium)" and "typical eukaryote"), one row at a time on the cue words: row 1 "nucleus" (none / present), row 2 "main DNA" (one ring / several linear chromosomes), row 3 "where" (nucleoid, no membrane / inside the nucleus), row 4 "packaging" (in bacteria: coiled, held by proteins, not on histones / wound on histones: chromatin), row 5 "extra DNA" (plasmids / rings in mitochondria and chloroplasts). Each cell fills with a tiny plum icon (a ring, rods, a coiled ring, a beaded thread) as its words are spoken. The row "same double helix, same four bases" sits beneath as a bar the whole table rests on. Both halves of the board return to full strength. A one-second hold.

### 11. memory

## Memory aid and assumptions

Aids: **the Greek roots** ("pro" before, "eu" true, "karyon" kernel or nut: prokaryote is before a nucleus, eukaryote has a true nucleus) and **"bacteria: ring; us: lines in a nucleus, but our mitochondria and chloroplasts still keep a ring"**. Both are built from the bank's two insights for this topic ("Bacterial DNA has no nucleus around it" and "Mitochondrial DNA looks bacterial for a reason", `module5-memory-aids-2026-10-11.md`, section "DNA in prokaryotes and eukaryotes"). The bank is a draft; the roots and the ring-or-line aid are authored here and checked below.
Assumptions checked:
- Greek roots. "Pro" means before, "eu" means well or good and "karyon" means kernel or nut. "Nucleus" is a Latin word (from nux, nut) that means kernel in the same way; it is a parallel word, not derived from karyon. "True" is a popular gloss for "eu" used here as a memory hook (the dictionary sense is "good"), so "before a nucleus" and "true nucleus" are memory translations rather than strict etymology (Claude science check, finding 6). It is a memory aid for the definition (what the cell lacks or has), not a claim about history, although prokaryotes did appear earlier on Earth. It holds for both prokaryote groups (bacteria and archaea), because it is about the nucleus only. It does not predict the DNA shape or histones, which is why the second aid exists. In scene 11 it is paired with the scene 3 gloss "nucleoid = nucleus-like" so the student sees one word family.
- Ring or line. True for "most bacteria" (one circular chromosome) and for the eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes (linear). It fails for the rare bacteria with a linear chromosome (some Borrelia and Streptomyces species) or more than one chromosome (Vibrio cholerae has two); the learner copy says "most bacteria" and "usually" for exactly this. It also does not cover plasmids (small rings in bacteria; also seen in yeast), so the aid is about the main chromosome. It says nothing about archaea, so the claim that bacteria are not wound on histones is not repeated for all prokaryotes.
- Tennis ball. The scale assumes a nucleus of about 6 micrometres (typical nuclei are 5 to 10 micrometres) and a diploid human genome of about 2 m. With 5 to 10 micrometres the enlarged thread is about 13 to 27 km, so "more than ten kilometres" is safe at either end. The thread would be about 22 micrometres thick at that scale (DNA is about 2 nanometres wide), which is thinner than a human hair, so the copy does not say "a hair".
- Spool and rubber band: see Decisions, analogy mapping.
