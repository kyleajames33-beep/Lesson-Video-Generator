### 1. hook (two metres in a tennis ball)
> In the copying video we saw billions of letters packed into a nucleus. How? If you could unwind the DNA in just one of your cells, you would have a thread about two metres long. It sits in a nucleus about six thousandths of a millimetre across. Blow that nucleus up to the size of a tennis ball, and the thread would be more than ten kilometres long. So how does a cell store DNA this long and still use it? And does a bacterium, which has no nucleus at all, do it differently?

### 2. two kinds of cell
> Every cell stores its genes in DNA, the same double helix you met earlier. What differs is where the DNA is kept and what shape it takes. A eukaryote, like an animal or plant cell, keeps its DNA inside a nucleus, an inner compartment with a membrane around it. A prokaryote has no nucleus. Bacteria are the prokaryotes we will use as our example.

### 3. the bacterium: a ring in the nucleoid
> Start with a bacterium. In most bacteria the main DNA is one long molecule whose two ends are joined, so it forms a ring, which we call a circular chromosome. With no nucleus around it, the ring lies in one region of the cell called the nucleoid, a word that means nucleus-like. The nucleoid has no membrane. It is simply the part of the cell where the DNA sits.

Visual: the camera pushes into the bacterium (painted rod-shaped plate with the DNA left out; the DNA is coded over it). The long plum thread draws as a line across the cell with its two ends marked by amber dots; on "whose two ends are joined" the ends travel together and fuse into a ring with a click, and the tag "circular chromosome: a ring" writes on. On "lies in one region" the ring settles into a pale patch of the cytoplasm and a scribble circle lands on the patch; on "nucleoid" the word writes on in amber with the gloss "nucleus-like" (the same word family as "eukaryote: true nucleus", shown as a pair). The right half of the board dims to about 30 per cent. On "has no membrane" a grey cross lands on a ghost dashed ring and the tag "no membrane around it" writes on. A one-second hold.

### 4. not floating loose: coiling
> This ring is far longer than the cell. Stretched out, it would be about one and a half millimetres, several hundred times the length of the cell. So it is not floating loose. Enzymes twist the ring, the way twisting a loop of rubber band makes it coil up on itself, and proteins grip the coiled ring at many points to hold it compact. That packs the long ring into the nucleoid without any membrane.

Visual: the right half stays dimmed (about 30 per cent). The ring (still in the bacterium) swells out to a giant loop that is drawn crossing the cell wall and running far outside the screen's cell; a `ScaleBar` beside it reads "ring about 1.5 mm; cell about 2 to 4 micrometres: several hundred times (about 400 to 800)". ### 5. plasmids

The cell copies a plasmid separately from the main chromosome, and a plasmid often carries extra genes, such as one that makes the bacterium resistant to an antibiotic. You saw bacteria pass DNA to each other in conjugation. Plasmids are often what they pass on.

### 6. the eukaryotic cell: linear chromosomes in a nucleus
> Now a eukaryotic cell. Its DNA is inside the nucleus, and it is not one ring. It is split into several chromosomes, and each is one DNA molecule with two ends, so we call them linear. In a human body cell you counted 46 in mitosis. Together they hold those two metres of DNA, about six billion pairs of bases. That is why the packing matters so much here.

Visual: the camera moves to the right cell, a coded cell cutaway (or painted plate) with the nucleus enlarged on the board. ### 7a. why the thread winds
> A nucleus holds that much DNA because the thread is wound and folded. Loose, two metres of thread would be a tangle that could neither fit nor be found when needed. The first level of packing is that each chromosome's DNA wraps around groups of proteins called histones, like thread round a spool. DNA carries a negative charge and histones carry a positive one, and opposite charges attract, so the thread clings to them.

Visual: the camera zooms into one rod; the left half of the board is dimmed. A painted spool and thread plate (sewing spool with thread, tagged "analogy: thread on a spool") appears beside it for one beat and fades. On "wraps around groups of proteins called histones" sand-coloured discs (`Spool`) appear along the plum thread and the thread winds round each one in turn (`WindOnSpools`, about three seconds), the thread between the discs staying straight. Small "-" signs on the thread and "+" signs on the discs pulse on "negative charge" and "positive one", with the tag "opposite charges attract". A grey screen tag at the lower right: "histone: a packaging protein". A one-second hold.

### 7b. nucleosome and chromatin
> One spool of DNA and histones is called a nucleosome. The whole beaded thread is chromatin, and the cell folds it further.

Visual: the hand-drawn spool plate fades out as the wound discs from 7a stay on screen. ### 8a. loosen to read a gene
> Winding creates a problem, because a gene can only be read, meaning the cell uses its instructions, when the cell can reach it. So the winding is adjustable. Chemical tags on the histones let the cell loosen the thread where it needs to read a gene, and tighten it elsewhere. Some tags weaken a histone's positive charge, so it grips the DNA less tightly.

Visual: the beaded thread loosens and tightens in a short loop (`WindOnSpools` reversing) while a pen taps a stretch of thread marked with a small amber gene bead; on "loosen the thread where it needs to read a gene" the stretch around the gene opens (the discs slide apart, the thread straightens around the bead) with a reader icon (a magnifying glass) reaching in; the tag "gene reachable". ### 8b. tight for division

### 9. DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts
> A eukaryotic cell keeps a little DNA outside the nucleus too. Mitochondria, where cells release energy from food, each hold many copies of a small ring of DNA, and so do chloroplasts, where plants carry out photosynthesis. Why a ring? Mitochondria and chloroplasts came from bacteria that began living inside larger cells, and they still carry a bacterium's kind of DNA. In our cells, these rings are extra to the 46 chromosomes in the nucleus.

### 10. side by side
> Put it side by side. Bacterial DNA is usually one main ring in the nucleoid with no membrane, coiled and held by proteins, plus plasmids. Eukaryotic DNA is several linear chromosomes in a nucleus, wound on histones as chromatin, plus small rings in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Both are made of the same double helix with the same four bases. The difference is the arrangement.

Visual: `CompareTable` builds on the fixed split board (column headings "typical prokaryote (e.g. a bacterium)" and "typical eukaryote"), one row at a time on the cue words: row 1 "nucleus" (none / present), row 2 "main DNA" (one ring / several linear chromosomes), row 3 "where" (nucleoid, no membrane / inside the nucleus), row 4 "packaging" (in bacteria: coiled, held by proteins, not on histones / wound on histones: chromatin), row 5 "extra DNA" (plasmids / rings in mitochondria and chloroplasts). Each cell fills with a tiny plum icon (a ring, rods, a coiled ring, a beaded thread) as its words are spoken. The row "same double helix, same four bases" sits beneath as a bar the whole table rests on. Both halves of the board return to full strength. A one-second hold.

### 10b. writing the comparison

Each row of the table can become a sentence like that. Try the packaging row: bacterial DNA is coiled and held by proteins, whereas eukaryotic DNA is wound on histones as chromatin.

### 11. memory
> Two ways to keep it straight. Karyon is Greek for a kernel, here the nucleus. Pro means before, so a prokaryote is from before the nucleus. Eu means good or well, so a eukaryote has a proper nucleus, which biologists often call a true nucleus. For shapes: bacteria use a ring, we use lines in a nucleus, but our mitochondria and chloroplasts keep a ring from their bacterial past.

Visual: two big handwritten amber lines write on, one at a time, under small icons: "pro-karyote: before a kernel (no nucleus)" with a bare bacterium doodle, "eu-karyote: eu = good, a proper (true) nucleus" with a nucleus doodle; underlines under "pro", "eu" and "karyon". The earlier "nucleoid = nucleus-like" gloss slides in beside them as the same word family. No table rows are repeated. Then a second pair of lines: "bacteria: ring" and "us: lines, in a nucleus" with the ring and the rods, and "ring kept in mitochondria" with a small mitochondrion. A half-second hold.

### 15. key notes (quiet, about 75 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- All cells store genes in DNA (same double helix, four bases). The difference is where it is kept and how it is arranged.
- A plasmid is a small ring of DNA, copied separately from the main chromosome, that often carries genes such as antibiotic resistance. Because it is separate, it can carry an added gene (genetic technology, later).

## Memory aid and assumptions

Aids: **the Greek roots** ("pro" before, "eu" good or well, "karyon" kernel or nut: prokaryote is from before the nucleus, eukaryote has a proper nucleus, often called a true nucleus) and **"bacteria: ring; us: lines in a nucleus, but our mitochondria and chloroplasts still keep a ring"**. Both are built from the bank's two insights for this topic ("Bacterial DNA has no nucleus around it" and "Mitochondrial DNA looks bacterial for a reason", `module5-memory-aids-2026-10-11.md`, section "DNA in prokaryotes and eukaryotes"). The bank is a draft; the roots and the ring-or-line aid are authored here and checked below.
Assumptions checked:
- Greek roots. "Pro" means before, "eu" means well or good and "karyon" means kernel or nut. "Nucleus" is a Latin word (from nux, nut) that means kernel in the same way; it is a parallel word, not derived from karyon. Since the Codex check (15 Oct) the narration says "eu means good or well" (the dictionary sense) and gives "true nucleus" only as the usual biology description, not as the meaning of eu; "from before the nucleus" is a memory translation rather than strict etymology (Claude science check, finding 6; `../checks/b6a-check.md`, finding 1). It is a memory aid for the definition (what the cell lacks or has), not a claim about history, although prokaryotes did appear earlier on Earth. It holds for both prokaryote groups (bacteria and archaea), because it is about the nucleus only. It does not predict the DNA shape or histones, which is why the second aid exists. In scene 11 it is paired with the scene 3 gloss "nucleoid = nucleus-like" so the student sees one word family.
- Ring or line. True for "most bacteria" (one circular chromosome) and for the eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes (linear). It fails for the rare bacteria with a linear chromosome (some Borrelia and Streptomyces species) or more than one chromosome (Vibrio cholerae has two); the learner copy says "most bacteria" and "usually" for exactly this. It also does not cover plasmids (small rings in bacteria; also seen in yeast), so the aid is about the main chromosome. It says nothing about archaea, so the claim that bacteria are not wound on histones is not repeated for all prokaryotes.
- Tennis ball. The scale assumes a nucleus of about 6 micrometres (typical nuclei are 5 to 10 micrometres) and a diploid human genome of about 2 m. With 5 to 10 micrometres the enlarged thread is about 13 to 27 km, so "more than ten kilometres" is safe at either end. The thread would be about 22 micrometres thick at that scale (DNA is about 2 nanometres wide), which is thinner than a human hair, so the copy does not say "a hair".
- Spool and rubber band: see Decisions, analogy mapping.
