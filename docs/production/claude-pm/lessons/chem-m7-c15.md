### 2. why a monomer needs two reactive ends

### 3. building PET, a polyester
> That's how PET is made. Its monomers are ethane one two diol, and benzene one four dicarboxylic acid, a benzene ring with a carboxyl group on **opposite** sides. At each link, the acid loses O H and the alcohol loses H. They leave as **water**, and an ester link forms. Building a polymer this way, losing a small molecule at every link, is called condensation polymerisation. With ester links all along its chain, PET is a **polyester**.

### 7. addition against condensation
> So how do the two families compare? An addition monomer needs a C double bond C, and a condensation monomer needs a reactive group at each end. Addition releases **nothing**, so the repeat unit has the same formula as its monomer. Condensation loses a small molecule at every link, here water. For PET and nylon, made from these pairs, the repeat unit is one of each monomer minus two waters. And look at the chain itself. Addition gives an all carbon backbone, while condensation puts ester or amide **links** in it. Water can split those links again, the reverse reaction, called hydrolysis. It's usually slow without acid, base or an enzyme, but a carbon backbone has no such **weak point**.

### 8. nylon: hydrogen bonds between chains
> Now the properties. Polyethylene's chains are held only by dispersion forces. But nylon has an N H and a C double bond O in every amide link. The N H on one chain forms a **hydrogen bond** with the C double bond O on the next chain, all along their length. Those hydrogen bonds hold the chains together much more strongly, so nylon is tough, and it melts at about two hundred and sixty five degrees, far above HDPE's **hundred and thirty**. That suits strong ropes and fishing line. Heat it enough, and the chains slide, so it can still be remoulded. The amide links inside each chain don't **break**.

### 9. PET: stiff rings and polar links
> PET's repeat unit has no O H or N H to donate hydrogen bonds, so these chains don't hydrogen bond to each other. But its benzene rings are flat and stiff, so the chains can't twist and **bend** easily, and its polar ester links add dipole dipole attraction between chains. So PET is strong and stiff, and it melts at about two hundred and sixty degrees. Blown into a **bottle**, it's tough and clear. Spun into thin fibres, the same polymer becomes **polyester** clothing.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Condensation polymerisation: monomers with a reactive group at each end join, and a small molecule (usually water) is lost at every link. Diol + dicarboxylic acid → polyester (ester links –COO–); diamine + dicarboxylic acid → polyamide (amide links –CONH–).
2. Monomers and repeat unit. PET: ethane-1,2-diol and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid give [–O–CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–CO–]ₙ. Nylon 6,6: hexane-1,6-diamine and hexanedioic acid give [–NH–(CH₂)₆–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–]ₙ. Each link releases one water.
3. Repeat unit (diol or diamine plus diacid): one of each monomer, minus the water at each end; two links per repeat unit; brackets, continuation bonds, n. Monomers: add the water back (OH to the C=O side, H to the O or N side); count the C=O carbons.
4. Addition against condensation: C=C against two reactive ends; no by-product against water; repeat unit = monomer against (for these pairs) one of each monomer − 2 H₂O; C–C backbone against ester or amide links (which water can split: hydrolysis, usually slow).
5. Nylon: N–H···O=C hydrogen bonds between chains: tough, melts about 265 °C (ropes, fishing line). PET: stiff rings, polar ester links: strong, stiff, melts about 260 °C (bottles, polyester fibres). Both are thermoplastics; melting does not break the chain links.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a condensation monomer needs two reactive ends: after each link, a reactive group remains at each end (2). Why water forms: the acid's OH and the alcohol's or amine's H leave together (3, 5). Why the repeat unit is one of each monomer minus two waters: each unit has a link at each end, and each link lost one water (4). Why the units join: the bracket ends are complementary (4). Why adding water recovers the monomers: it reverses the link (6). Why the acid is hexanedioic, not butanedioic: the two C=O carbons belong to the acid (6). Why the repeat-unit formula differs: water leaves (7). Why condensation links can be hydrolysed and a C–C backbone cannot: only the ester or amide link has a place for water to split (7). Why nylon is tough and high melting: hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O on neighbouring chains (8). Why nylon still remoulds: only the forces between chains are overcome (8). Why PET is stiff and high melting without hydrogen bonds: stiff rings and polar ester links (9).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** addition produces water (7, crossed grey chip); one ester link per PET repeat unit (4, said positively); water stays in the chain (feedback 1, crossed); only the CH₂ groups count when naming the acid (6, crossed ghost; feedback 2, crossed); melting nylon breaks its amide links (8, said positively); a monomer with one reactive group can polymerise (2, ethyl ethanoate stops).
- **One idea at a time:** one model per scene (one join; one PET build; one repeat unit; one nylon build; one recovery; one comparison board built row by row; one chain box with one change at a time: hydrogen bonds, then heat; then rings, then dipoles).
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen HSC-style tasks with every condition visible (both monomers by name and formula and both demands in A1; the repeat unit, the HDPE comparison and the "forces between chains" demand in A2), 10 seconds each with the invitation to pause, nothing answer-bearing. One crossed mistake each; feedback lengths under "Narration length estimate".
- **Avoided double teaching:** esterification, the ester group, condensation and the cut line are c10 callbacks; the amide link is a c11 callback; repeat-unit notation, the chain view and thermoplastic behaviour are c14 callbacks; hydrogen bonding is a c05 callback; dipole–dipole is a c08b callback.
- **Dotpoint coverage:** `C7-IQ6-02`: model (repeat units drawn from monomers and back; the chain view) and compare the structure, properties and uses of nylon (nylon 6,6; nylon 6,10 in attempt 2) and polyesters (PET; PBS in attempt 1; poly(ethylene adipate) in scene 6), compared with the addition polymers of `C7-IQ6-01` (scene 7). Mixed practice of both dotpoints is `chem-m7-g04`.
- **Out of scope by design:** acid chloride routes; conditions and catalysts; natural polymers; Kevlar and other extension polymers; environmental lifecycle; thermosets.
