- **Area from the circumference.** The pizza cut into 16 slices, alternated tip-up and tip-down, makes a near-rectangle whose length is half the circumference (πr) and whose height is r. Rectangle area: πr × r = πr². This uses a05a's base × height.

### 1. hook: the crust on a slice
> This pizza is thirty centimetres across, cut into eight equal slices. Look at one slice. It has two straight edges, where the knife went in to the centre, and one curved edge, the crust. How long is that crust? And how much pizza is in the slice? You can't lay a ruler along a curve, and you can't count squares in a round shape very easily. So how do we find the perimeter and area of circles, slices of circles, and shapes with curved **edges**?

### 2. pi: diameters around a circle
> A few words first. The radius goes from the centre to the edge. The diameter goes all the way across, through the centre, so it's two radii. And the perimeter of a circle has its own name, the circumference. Now roll the pizza along a line for one full turn, and mark how far it went. That's the circumference. Lay diameters along it. One, two, three, and a little bit **more**. Every circle does exactly the same, big or small, and that number, three point one four one five nine and so on, is called pi. So the circumference is pi times the diameter. Since a diameter is two radii, that's also two pi r. For our pizza, pi times thirty is ninety-four point two five **centimetres** all the way around.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): the pizza (420 px) with labels "radius" (blue, centre to edge) and "diameter" (blue, across); grey chips "radius: centre to edge", "diameter: two radii", "circumference: the perimeter of a circle". ### 3. area: slices into a rectangle
> For the area, cut the whole pizza into thin slices, and lay them out side by side, one pointing up, the next pointing down. The more slices, the more the shape looks like a **rectangle**. Its height is one radius. Its length is half the crust, so half of two pi r, which is pi r. And a rectangle's area is length times height, pi r times r. That's pi r **squared**. Our pizza's radius is half of thirty, fifteen centimetres. Pi times fifteen squared is seven hundred and six point eight six square **centimetres**. Two things to watch. Halve the diameter first. Using thirty in the formula gives four times the area, because the thirty gets **squared**. And use the pi key on your calculator, rounding only at the very end. Three point one four instead gives seven hundred and six point five, which is already **off**.

### 4. sectors: a fraction of the circle
> Now the slice. A slice of a circle between two radii is called a sector, and its curved edge is called an arc. Eight equal slices means each one is an eighth of the circle. Its angle at the centre is forty-five degrees, and forty-five over three sixty is one eighth. So the crust is one eighth of the circumference, eleven point seven eight **centimetres**. And the slice's area is one eighth of the whole area, eighty-eight point three six square **centimetres**. We call the angle at the centre theta, a Greek letter. Watch the wheel. Whatever the angle, it's the same fraction of the circle, theta over three sixty, for the arc and for the area. Careful with the slice's perimeter, though. It has three edges, the crust and two straight cuts. Eleven point seven eight plus fifteen plus fifteen is forty-one point seven eight **centimetres**. Writing just the crust is the **slip**.

### 5. curved composites: a running track
> Curved edges show up in composite shapes too. The inside edge of a standard running track is two straights, each eighty-four point three nine metres long, joined by a semicircle at each end. Each semicircle has a radius of thirty-six point five metres, so a diameter of seventy-three. Plan first. Perimeter equals two straights plus two semicircles. Two semicircles of the same size make one full circle, so add one circumference. That's a hundred and sixty-eight point seven eight plus pi times seventy-three, three hundred and ninety-eight point one two metres around the **edge**. A lap is called four hundred metres because it's measured a little way out from that edge. The ends of the rectangle are inside the shape, so they don't count. For the area inside, it's the rectangle plus the two semicircles, which again make one circle. Eighty-four point three nine times seventy-three, plus pi times thirty-six point five squared, is ten thousand three hundred and forty-five point eight six square **metres**, just over a hectare.

Pi times three squared, minus pi times two squared, is pi times five, fifteen point seven one square **metres**. It's the same add or subtract thinking as last time, now with **circles**.
