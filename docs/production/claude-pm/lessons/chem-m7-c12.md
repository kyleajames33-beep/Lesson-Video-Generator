Syllabus dotpoint (as printed):

"investigate the structure and action of soaps and detergents"

This video covers the structure of soaps and of anionic, cationic and non-ionic detergents, how soap is made from fats (saponification, as the source of the structure), the cleaning action (micelles and emulsification), and behaviour in hard water. The hands-on comparison is chem-m7-p06.

**Boundary:** this video starts by locating the hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tail, and stops at explaining micelles and comparing soaps and detergents in hard water. It teaches: oil and water don't mix because water molecules hydrogen bond to each other and oil molecules (mostly long hydrocarbon chains, so largely non-polar) can't join in, so the water keeps its hydrogen bonds and squeezes the oil out into a layer (a short recall of c03's hexane layer); a soap is the sodium (or potassium) salt of a long-chain carboxylic acid (sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻Na⁺), named "-oate" in its systematic name, sodium octadecanoate (c09); the long acid barely mixes with water, but in the salt one end is a charged ion; its long hydrocarbon tail is non-polar, attracts oil by dispersion forces and is hydrophobic (avoids water); its carboxylate head is ionic, attracts polar water molecules by ion–dipole attraction (and accepts hydrogen bonds) and is hydrophilic (water loving); soap is made by saponification: fats and oils are triglycerides, triple esters of glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) with three long carboxylic acids; heating with concentrated sodium hydroxide hydrolyses all three ester links, giving glycerol and three soap molecules; NaOH is a reactant (three per fat molecule), not a catalyst; the basic mixture takes the acid's proton, leaving a carboxylate ion, which can't re-form the ester with glycerol, so the reaction goes essentially to completion (contrast with c10's reversible esterification); in water, soap's tails are squeezed out like oil, so soap gathers at the surface with tails out of the water, and above a certain concentration it also clusters into micelles (tails inward, heads outward); cleaning: tails dissolve into the grease by dispersion forces, heads stay in the water, agitation breaks the grease into small droplets each wrapped in soap (grease in the core, heads outward), the droplets' negative surfaces repel each other, so they don't easily join back together, and they stay dispersed as an emulsion that the rinse carries away; the grease is emulsified, not dissolved in the water; hard water contains dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions (often from rocks such as limestone); two soap anions and one Ca²⁺ form insoluble calcium stearate, the scum (2C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻(aq) + Ca²⁺(aq) → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Ca(s)), using up soap, so there's less froth and more soap is needed; synthetic detergents keep the tail-and-head plan with different heads: anionic (sulfonate –SO₃⁻ or sulfate –OSO₃⁻; e.g. sodium dodecyl sulfate) which still attract Ca²⁺, but whose calcium and magnesium salts are much more soluble and stay dissolved, so far less scum forms; cationic (a positive nitrogen head, R–N(CH₃)₃⁺, like c11's ammonium ions; fabric softeners, some antiseptics); non-ionic (an uncharged polar chain that hydrogen bonds with water, so Ca²⁺ has no charge to bind); the comparison: both clean by micelles; soap from renewable fats and oils, readily biodegradable, forms scum in hard water; detergents work in hard and soft water, many made from petrochemicals (some from plant oils). **Not here:** surface tension and the pepper demonstration (site hook; author note b); the critical micelle concentration as a number (author note c); acid-catalysed ester hydrolysis (c10 recall only); why potassium soaps are softer (author note d); builders, enzymes and other laundry additives (author note e); phospholipid membranes (Biology); detailed environmental lifecycle and the history of branched detergents (author note f); calculations; the hands-on soap and detergent comparison and its risk table (chem-m7-p06).

### 3. soap: a particle with two ends

### 4. where soap comes from: saponification
> Where does soap come from? Fats and oils are triglycerides. Each one is glycerol, a small molecule with three O H groups, joined to three long acid chains by three **ester** links. Heat a fat with concentrated sodium hydroxide, and the hydroxide ions break all three ester links. You get glycerol, and three soap particles. That's **saponification**, or soap making. The sodium hydroxide is used up, three for every fat molecule, so it's a reactant, not a catalyst. The strongly basic mixture also takes each acid's **proton**, leaving a carboxylate ion. A carboxylate ion can't re-form the ester with glycerol, so unlike esterification, it goes essentially to **completion**.

### 6. how soap lifts grease

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Soap: the sodium (or potassium) salt of a long-chain carboxylic acid, e.g. sodium stearate C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻Na⁺. Tail: long hydrocarbon chain, non-polar, attracts oil by dispersion (hydrophobic). Head: COO⁻, ionic, attracts water by ion–dipole (hydrophilic).
2. Saponification: fat (triglyceride, a triple ester) + 3NaOH → glycerol + 3 soap, heated. C₃H₅(OOCC₁₇H₃₅)₃ + 3NaOH → C₃H₅(OH)₃ + 3C₁₇H₃₅COONa. NaOH is a reactant; goes to completion (the base takes the acid's proton, and the carboxylate can't re-form the ester).
3. Cleaning: tails dissolve into grease (dispersion), heads stay in water; agitation gives soap-wrapped droplets (micelles), tails in, heads out; negative surfaces repel, so the droplets stay dispersed (an emulsion) and rinse away. Grease is emulsified, not dissolved in the water.
4. Hard water (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺): 2C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻(aq) + Ca²⁺(aq) → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Ca(s), scum. Soap is used up: less froth, more soap needed.
5. Detergents: same tail, different head. Anionic (–SO₃⁻ or –OSO₃⁻): Ca²⁺ is still attracted, but its salts are much more soluble and stay dissolved, so little scum. Cationic (positive N head): fabric softeners. Non-ionic (uncharged polar chain): nothing for Ca²⁺ to bind.
6. Soap: renewable, biodegrades readily, scums in hard water. Detergents: work in hard water, many from petrochemicals.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why oil and water separate: water keeps its hydrogen bonds and squeezes out largely non-polar molecules (2, a c03 recall). Why soap dissolves in water at all, although the long acid barely does: in the salt one end is a charged ion, and that head attracts water by ion–dipole attraction, not merely "it contains sodium" (3). Why the tail goes into oil: non-polar chains attract by dispersion (3, 6). Why soap is a salt: saponification hydrolyses the fat's ester links in base, leaving carboxylate ions with Na⁺ (4). Why NaOH is a reactant: three are used up per fat (4). Why saponification goes to completion, unlike esterification: the base takes the acid's proton, and the carboxylate ion can't re-form the ester (4). Why soap lines the surface and forms micelles: the tails are squeezed out of water like oil, while the heads stay in (5). Why the grease leaves the fabric and stays away: tails in grease, heads in water, droplets with negative surfaces repel each other, so they don't easily join (6). Why it is an emulsion, not a solution: the grease stays as oil droplets (6). Why soap forms scum in hard water: two carboxylate ions and one Ca²⁺ make an insoluble, neutral salt (7). Why there is less froth: soap in scum can't act (7). Why detergents work in hard water: Ca²⁺ is still attracted to an anionic head, but the calcium salt is much more soluble and stays dissolved; non-ionic heads carry no charge to bind (8).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** water repels oil (2); soap dissolves because of sodium (3); NaOH is a catalyst (4, crossed); tails point into water (5, 6); soap dissolves grease in water (6, crossed; feedback 1, crossed); calcium is not attracted to detergent heads (8, said positively); emulsions never separate (1, 6, softened); one soap ion per Ca²⁺ (7; feedback 2, crossed); detergents are stronger soaps (9, crossed).
