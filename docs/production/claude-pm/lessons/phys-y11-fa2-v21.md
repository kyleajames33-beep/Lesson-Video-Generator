- **The cause made visible (owner quality bar; `WavefrontRefract` with v18's construction).** One wavefront AB meets the boundary at A; B travels BC = v₁t in air to C while the wavelet from A grows to AD = v₂t in glass; the refracted wavefront CD is tangent to it. Triangle ABC (right angle at B) and triangle ACD (right angle at D) share AC. The angle at A between AB and the boundary is θ₁; the angle at C between CD and the boundary is θ₂ (v18: a wavefront's angle to the surface equals the ray's angle to the normal; recalled in a clause). So sin θ₁ = BC/AC and sin θ₂ = AD/AC; dividing, AC cancels: sin θ₁/sin θ₂ = v₁/v₂. Said in plain sentences: "opposite side over the long side" for sine (Stage 5 trigonometry, route entry), no "hypotenuse", no "Huygens" (as v18). The reflection version (v18) had equal sides and so equal angles; here the sides are unequal, so the angles are unequal, said as the contrast.
- **From speeds to indices (v19's interface ratio).** sin θ₁/sin θ₂ = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁, so n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. "Snell's law" named once as the common name (site key term).

### 2. one wavefront crosses
> Further away. Water slows the light less, so it bends less. But by exactly how much? Watch one wavefront cross into glass. Its near end reaches the boundary first and starts moving more slowly. Its far end still has this distance to go in air. In the same time, the light from the near end travels a shorter distance in the glass, because it's slower. So the new wavefront is turned, and the ray turns towards the **normal**.

### 3. the sines and the speeds
> Look at the two triangles. They share this long side along the boundary. Like the mirror, the angle between a wavefront and the boundary equals the ray's angle to the normal. In the top triangle, the distance in air is opposite the angle of incidence, so the sine of that angle is the distance in air over the long side. In the bottom triangle, the sine of the angle of refraction is the distance in glass over the same long side. Divide one sine by the other, and the long side cancels. The ratio of the sines equals the ratio of the **speeds**.

### 4. the law of refraction, and the table
> Last time, we found that the ratio of the speeds is the ratio of the indices, flipped. So sine theta one over sine theta two equals n two over n one. Cross-multiply, and n one sine theta one equals n two sine theta two. That's the law of refraction, often called Snell's law. Now test it on our table. Sine forty over sine twenty-five is one point five two. Sixty and thirty-five give one point five one. Seventy and thirty-nine give one point four nine. Every row gives about one point five, the index of the glass. The angles weren't proportional, but their sines **are**.

### 5. reading the law
> So n times sine theta stays the same on both sides of the boundary. If the index goes up, the sine has to go down, so the angle gets smaller, and the ray bends towards the normal. Each index pairs with the angle in its own medium, and medium one is where the light starts. Along the normal, theta one is zero, so theta two is zero too, and there's no bend. And it's the sines that balance, not the angles, so n one theta one equals n two theta two is **wrong**.

### 6. worked example: air into water
> Task: light passes from air into water at forty degrees to the normal. Find the angle of refraction. In air, n one is one point zero zero, and theta one is forty degrees. In water, n two is one point three three. Rearrange the law: sine theta two equals n one sine theta one, over n two. That's zero point six four three, over one point three three. So sine theta two is zero point four eight **three**. Take the inverse sine, and theta two is twenty-eight point nine **degrees**. That's further from the normal than the twenty-five in glass, just as we **predicted**.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Law of refraction (Snell's law): n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Angles from the normal. Medium 1 is where the light comes from; each n pairs with the angle in its own medium.
2. Why: in the same time, the two ends of a wavefront travel distances in the ratio of the speeds, so sin θ₁ / sin θ₂ = v₁ / v₂ = n₂ / n₁.
3. To find θ₂: sin θ₂ = n₁ sin θ₁ / n₂, then θ₂ = sin⁻¹(...). Higher n₂: smaller θ₂ (towards the normal). Lower n₂: larger θ₂ (away). θ₁ = 0: no bend.
4. The sines balance, not the angles: n₁θ₁ = n₂θ₂ is wrong.
5. Ray diagram: boundary with media and n; dashed normal; incident ray (arrow in, θ₁); θ₂ from the law; refracted ray (arrow out, θ₂); optional faint reflected ray at θ₁.
