### 1. hook: lead you can't see
> Last time, two clear solutions, silver nitrate and sodium chloride, made a white solid. Water engineers use the same trick on a bigger problem. Old lead pipes can leave lead ions dissolved in tap water. They're poisonous, and the water still looks perfectly **clear**. Add the right solution, and the lead comes out as a solid you can filter off. But not every pair of clear solutions makes a **solid**. So how could you know which ones will?

Visual: opens on m3-v02's final frame; the "Next" chip fades and the pattern strips stay small. On "perfectly clear" the AgCl pair slides left and a clear sample bottle (`y11c-sample-bottle-clear-v1.png`, requested in m2-v14) appears with the tag "tap water from old lead pipes" and a lens showing a few amber "Pb²⁺" dots among water (rose tag "poisonous, invisible"). On "makes a solid" two more tubes appear: `m5x-no-ppt-clear-v1.png` (tag "two clear solutions: still clear") beside the white AgCl tube; a pen "?" between them.

### 2. what's really in each bottle
> Start with what's in each bottle. A dissolved ionic compound isn't whole units floating around. It's separate ions, each surrounded by water. Silver nitrate solution is silver ions and nitrate ions. Sodium chloride solution is sodium ions and chloride ions. Mix them, and you have **four kinds of ions** in one tube. The only new thing that can happen is that ions find **new partners**.

Visual: the board clears to the two dropper bottles with an ion lens above each: amber Ag⁺ and violet NO₃⁻; amber Na⁺ and violet Cl⁻; each ion in a pale blue water shell. On "four kinds of ions" the two lenses slide together and merge (tag "4 kinds of ion"). On "new partners" pen arcs link Ag⁺ to Cl⁻ and Na⁺ to NO₃⁻ (tags "new pair?").

### 3. the precipitate and the spectators
> Silver ions and chloride ions attract each other more strongly than the water can keep them apart, so they lock together into solid silver chloride. A solid that forms when two solutions are mixed is a **precipitate**, and this is a precipitation reaction. Sodium and nitrate ions stay dissolved, exactly as they were. They take no part, so they're called **spectator ions**. They're in the tube, but they're not in the reaction.

Visual: in the merged lens, Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ shed their shells, lock into a small white lattice and sink (m2-v15's ion lock); `m5x-ppt-agcl-white-v1.png` pulses. On "precipitate" gloss chips "precipitate: a solid that forms when two solutions are mixed" and "precipitation reaction". On "spectator ions" Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ keep swimming and get grey "spectator" tags; gloss chip "spectator ions: present, unchanged, take no part". A composition card writes: "solid: AgCl" and "still dissolved: Na⁺, NO₃⁻".

### 4. finding the pattern by experiment
> Which pairs lock together was found by experiment, and you can see the pattern on a small scale. Put one drop of each of two solutions into a well of a spotting plate, and record which pairs go cloudy. Wear safety glasses, use tiny volumes, and put the waste in the container your teacher labels. Silver nitrate stains skin, and lead and barium salts are toxic, so those are teacher demonstrations. Now look down the **results**. None of the solids contain sodium, potassium or **nitrate**.

Visual: `p03-well-plate-white-tile-v1.png` fills the right two thirds; a coded 3 × 3 block of wells fills drop by drop (rows "AgNO₃", "CuSO₄", "CaCl₂"; columns "NaCl", "Na₂CO₃", "NaOH"): white, pale yellow, brown; clear, blue-green, pale blue; clear, white, faint white (author note b). A safety strip: `m5x-safety-glasses-v1.png` small, "tiny volumes; waste to the labelled container; AgNO₃ stains skin; Pb²⁺ and Ba²⁺ salts: teacher demonstration". On "results" a results grid writes beside the plate with "cloudy" and "clear". On "potassium or nitrate" a pen ring circles the grid's formulas: every Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ stays in solution (tag "never in the solid").

### 5. the solubility rules (NAGSAG)
> Patterns like that are summed up as the solubility rules. A handy way to remember the soluble groups is **NAGSAG**. Nitrates, ammonium and Group 1 compounds are almost always soluble. Sulfates are soluble, except with barium, lead and **calcium**. Acetates are soluble. And Group 17, the halides chloride, bromide and iodide, are soluble except with **silver and lead**. Everything else, like carbonates and hydroxides, is mostly insoluble, unless it's paired with Group 1 or **ammonium**.

Visual: the rules card (from C13, Year 11 wording) replaces the plate; gloss chips "soluble: dissolves", "insoluble: barely dissolves". On "NAGSAG" the six letters stack down the left; rows write on their words: "N: nitrates (NO₃⁻)", "A: ammonium (NH₄⁺)", "G: Group 1 (Na⁺, K⁺)", bracket "almost always soluble". On "calcium" the S row: "S: sulfates (SO₄²⁻): soluble, except Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺ (insoluble), Ca²⁺ (slightly); also Sr²⁺; Ag⁺ slightly". "A: acetates (CH₃COO⁻): soluble". On "silver and lead" the G row: "G: Group 17 halides (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻): soluble, except Ag⁺, Pb²⁺" (footnote "PbCl₂ slightly soluble"). On "Group 1 or ammonium" a separate box: "Mostly insoluble: carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides, sulfides, unless with Group 1 or NH₄⁺ (also Ba(OH)₂ soluble)". Tag: "a first prediction from experiments". Hold about 3 seconds (reading hold).

### 6. the method, and barium sulfate
> So here's the method. List the ions, swap partners, and **check both** new pairs against the rules. Try barium chloride and sodium sulfate. Swapping gives barium sulfate and sodium chloride. Sodium chloride is Group 1, so it's soluble. Barium sulfate is a sulfate with barium, one of the exceptions, so it's the white **precipitate**. Balanced, barium chloride plus sodium sulfate gives barium sulfate solid, plus two sodium chloride, **aqueous**.

Visual: the card shrinks to the corner. Swap cross: tiles "Ba²⁺ Cl⁻" and "Na⁺ SO₄²⁻"; cations cross over (pen X). On "check both" a tick box appears under each new pair; a chip "checked one product only" fades in and is crossed. NaCl ticks green ("Group 1: soluble"); on "precipitate" BaSO₄ flags amber ("sulfate with Ba²⁺: insoluble"); `m5x-ppt-baso4-white-v1.png` stands in the left column (grey tag "teacher demonstration"). On "aqueous" the molecular line writes: "BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)" (margin "molecular").

### 7. the full ionic equation
> That equation hides what's really in the tube. So write every aqueous ionic compound as its separate ions. On the left, a barium ion, two chloride ions, two sodium ions and a sulfate ion. On the right, sodium chloride splits too, but barium sulfate is a solid, so it stays **whole**. That's the full ionic equation. Never split the precipitate, because in a solid the ions aren't free.

Visual: the token flight: each (aq) formula splits and its ions fly down to line 2: "Ba²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq)" (margin "full ionic"); gloss chip "full ionic equation: every aqueous ionic compound as separate ions". On "whole" BaSO₄(s) drops straight down unsplit with a lock icon (tag "(s): never split").

### 8. cancel the spectators
> Now find the ions that are exactly the same on both sides: two sodium ions and two chloride ions. They're the spectators, so cross them **out**. What's left is the net ionic equation: barium ion plus sulfate ion gives barium sulfate solid. Check the charges. Plus two and minus two make zero, matching the neutral **solid**. That one line is the only change that actually happened.

Visual: pen links join matching pairs in line 2 (2Na⁺ to 2Na⁺, 2Cl⁻ to 2Cl⁻); on "out" each pair is struck grey on both sides. Line 3 writes: "Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)" (margin "net ionic"); gloss chip "net ionic equation: only the ions that change". On "the neutral solid" the charge tally writes: "(+2) + (−2) = 0 → 0". The ion lens returns small: Ba²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ lock and sink, grey Na⁺ and Cl⁻ swim on.

### 9. lead iodide: charges set the twos
> Here's one where the charges matter. Mix lead nitrate and potassium iodide. Potassium nitrate is soluble, but lead with a halide is an exception, so lead iodide is a bright yellow **precipitate**. A lead ion is plus two and iodide is minus one, so the formula is P b I **two**. That's why the equation needs two potassium iodides, and why the net ionic equation is lead ion plus two iodide ions gives lead iodide **solid**.

Visual: the stack clears. Swap cross "Pb²⁺ NO₃⁻" and "K⁺ I⁻" to "Pb²⁺ I⁻" and "K⁺ NO₃⁻"; KNO₃ ticks; on "precipitate" PbI₂ flags amber and `m5x-ppt-pbi2-yellow-v1.png` stands in the left column (grey tag "lead salts: toxic, teacher demonstration, heavy-metal waste"). On "P b I two" charge tiles "+2" and two "−1" balance; a chip "PbI" fades in and is crossed. Lines write quickly: "Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)"; "Pb²⁺(aq) + 2NO₃⁻(aq) + 2K⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2K⁺(aq) + 2NO₃⁻(aq)" with 2K⁺ and 2NO₃⁻ struck grey. On "solid" line 3: "Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s)", tally "(+2) + 2(−1) = 0".

### 10. prediction: no precipitate (in-scene countdown, 3 seconds)
> Quick prediction. Sodium sulfate and potassium nitrate. Will a solid **form**? Swapping gives sodium nitrate and potassium sulfate, and both are Group 1 compounds, so both are **soluble**. Nothing forms. Every ion is a spectator, so there's no net ionic **equation**. Write no precipitate.

Visual: the swap cross with "Na⁺ SO₄²⁻" and "K⁺ NO₃⁻"; chips "solid" and "no solid". On "solid form" a 3 s `Countdown` ring runs (in-scene; narration resumes after it). On "equation" the lens shows all four ions with grey spectator tags (tag "all still there") and line 3 shows an empty bracket "none: all spectators".

### 11. back to the lead

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Dissolved ionic compounds are separate ions. Mixing two solutions: ions swap partners; an insoluble new pair is the precipitate. Ions that stay dissolved and unchanged are spectator ions.
2. NAGSAG (soluble): nitrates, ammonium, Group 1, sulfates (not Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺; Ca²⁺ slightly), acetates, halides (not Ag⁺, Pb²⁺). Mostly insoluble: carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides, sulfides, unless with Group 1 or NH₄⁺.
3. Molecular → full ionic (split only (aq) ionic compounds; never the solid) → net ionic (cross out spectators; charges balance).
4. Example: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s). If both possible products are soluble: no precipitate.
