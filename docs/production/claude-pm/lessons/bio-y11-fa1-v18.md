### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Enzyme: a biological catalyst (almost always a protein) that speeds up a specific reaction without being used up. Example: catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide, 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
- The substrate binds at the active site; the reaction happens; the products are released; the active site is free again, so a little enzyme converts a lot of substrate.
- Activation energy: the energy needed to start a reaction. Enzymes provide a route with a lower activation energy (substrate held in position, bonds strained), so many more molecules react each second. Products and overall energy are unchanged.
- Cells can't speed reactions by heating (proteins would stop working); enzymes let reactions run fast at about 37 °C.
- Specific: each enzyme acts on one substrate, because of its active site's shape. So the cell controls each reaction separately (more enzyme, or blocking it).

Memory: a bottle opener, not a bottle. A lower hill, not a harder push.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (2 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Peroxide equation (scene 1, notes).** 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. H: 4 = 4. O: 4 = 2 + 2. Correct.
- **"so slowly that you'd hardly notice in months" (scene 1).** Commercial hydrogen peroxide (3 to 6 per cent) loses only a few per cent of its strength over months at room temperature (stabilisers added). Correct.
- **"millions of times faster" (scene 1).** The demonstration alone: months (about 10⁷ s) against seconds is about a million-fold. Catalase's enhancement over the uncatalysed reaction is far larger. "millions" is safe. Flag against the route's "thousands" (Open decision 1).
- **Catalase in liver; peroxide as a harmful by-product (scene 2).** Liver cells are rich in catalase (in peroxisomes); hydrogen peroxide is produced by several oxidase reactions and is a reactive, damaging compound. Correct.
- **"Almost all enzymes are proteins" (scene 2).** Ribozymes (catalytic RNA) are the exception (grey tag). Correct.
- **Products and foam (scene 3).** Oxygen gas forms the foam. Correct.
- **Turnover (scene 4).** Catalase turnover is up to about 10⁷ per second (often quoted as up to about 40 million). "millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules every second": correct.
- **Activation energy and particle energies (scenes 5, 6).** At a given temperature, molecules have a spread of energies; only those at or above the activation energy react on collision; lowering the activation energy raises the fraction that can react. Enzymes hold substrates in position and strain bonds; the overall energy change and products are unchanged. Correct. ("hit hard enough" and "bumping into each other" are a collision-theory simplification; acceptable.)
- **Heating and proteins (scene 7).** High temperatures disrupt protein shape (denaturation, named in v20). Starch hydrolysis in the laboratory uses dilute acid and heating; salivary and pancreatic amylase digest starch within minutes at about 37 °C. Correct.
- **Specificity (scene 8).** Each enzyme acts on one substrate or a small group of closely related substrates; catalase does not hydrolyse starch; amylase does. "thousands of different enzymes": a human cell expresses thousands of enzyme types. Correct.
- **Control (scene 9).** Cells regulate pathways by changing the amount of an enzyme (gene expression) or its activity (for example by inhibition); respiration rate rises with ATP demand. Correct at this level.
- **Attempt 1 plausibility.** Potato tissue contains catalase and foams in hydrogen peroxide (less than liver). Foaming stopping when the substrate is exhausted and restarting with fresh substrate is the standard result. Correct.
