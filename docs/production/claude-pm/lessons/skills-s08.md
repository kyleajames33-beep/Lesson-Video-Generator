## Decisions

- **Main question:** *When a question is worth six to nine marks, how do you plan in about a minute, cover every demand with one idea per paragraph, use the stimulus as evidence, and end with a judgement that earns the top band?* Heading on screen: "Long answers: plan it, build it, judge it".
- **Owner brief served:** "Students know the content but don't know how to write the answer." The research pack (`../research/exam-skills/common-faults.md`) found three faults in the long questions above all: answering a nearby question (12 of 12 NESA feedback reports, Chemistry and Biology 2020 to 2025), leaving the stimulus unused or copying it (12 of 12), and a judgement without adequate reasons (7 of 12). `what-markers-reward.md` shows the band differences: for Biology 2022 Q27 (evaluate, 6 marks) the top band needed "detailed use of the stimulus" and a well-informed judgement; for Biology 2023 Q30 (assess) the top band needed a comprehensive explanation using the graph and a judgement, while the next band accepted only some reference to the evidence. The video teaches one plan (demands, verdict, one line per paragraph with a number), one paragraph build (PEEL) and one ending rule (verdict first and last, with its condition).
- **Why the worked question is vaccination in a fictional region:** it sits in Biology Module 7 content students have just met (b09, b10), it needs both an explanation and a judgement (analyse and assess), it has a real stimulus with values to select, and its fair judgement needs a condition (the outbreak itself made some people immune, and the graph shows correlation), which is exactly the "judgement with a condition" move. Region R and its data are fictional and labelled "example data". Near-match check below.
- **Worked question (card text):** "Region R introduced a two-dose measles vaccine many years ago. Measles spreads by droplets and is highly infectious. Two doses protect about 97% of people who receive them. To stop measles spreading, about 95% of a population needs to be immune. Several rural clinics closed in 2013. A school catch-up vaccination program began in 2018. | Year | 2010 | 2012 | 2014 | 2016 | 2018 | 2020 | 2022 | Two-dose coverage (%) | 96 | 95 | 91 | 87 | 89 | 94 | 95 | Measles cases | 12 | 8 | 40 | 310 | 180 | 25 | 6 | Analyse the data to explain the changes in measles cases in Region R from 2010 to 2022. In your answer, refer to immunity in individuals and in the population. Assess the effectiveness of the catch-up program. (7 marks)" The table is also drawn as a coded dual-axis line graph (coverage line, navy; cases columns, terracotta) beside the card, with "example data".
- **Model answer (screen, built in scenes 6 to 9; HSCScience illustrative):** Opening judgement: "The catch-up program was probably effective: after it began, coverage returned to 95% and cases fell from 180 to 6, although immunity from the 2016 outbreak probably also helped." P1 (individual): "Each dose presents measles antigens without causing disease, so the immune system makes memory B and T cells. On later exposure, the secondary response is fast and strong enough to stop the infection, so a vaccinated person is very unlikely to catch or pass on measles." P2 (population): "When about 95% of people are immune, an infected person rarely meets someone susceptible, so chains of transmission die out. This matches 2010 to 2012, when coverage was 95 to 96% and cases stayed low (12 and 8)." P3 (the outbreak): "After the clinics closed, coverage fell from 95% to 87% by 2016. More people were susceptible, so transmission chains continued, and cases rose from 8 to 310." P4 (the program, with a limit): "After the 2018 program began, coverage rose from 89% to 95%, back to the herd immunity level, and cases fell from 180 to 6. This supports the program's effectiveness. However, the data show a correlation, and people infected in 2016 became immune, so part of the fall probably came from the outbreak itself." Closing: "Overall, the program was probably the main reason cases fell, because cases dropped as coverage returned to the 95% threshold."
- **Illustrative marking guide (screen; HSCScience illustrative, following the band language in recent NESA guidelines, not quoted from any one question):** 6 to 7: explains the changes using individual and population immunity, with detailed use of the data; makes a supported judgement about the program. 4 to 5: sound explanation with some use of the data; a judgement. 2 to 3: describes the trend or explains vaccination in general. 1: relevant information. Labelled "HSCScience marking guide (illustrative)".

### 1. hook
> The longest questions, worth six to nine marks, can decide a band. In six years of NESA's marking feedback for Chemistry and Biology, every report flagged answers that left the stimulus unused, and more than half flagged judgements without reasons. So here's how to plan a long answer in a minute, build it one idea per paragraph, use the **stimulus**, and finish with the **judgement**.

### 2. how long answers are marked
> Long answers usually aren't marked by ticking separate points. A marker reads the whole answer and places it in a **band**. In recent guidelines, the top band for this kind of question asks for detailed use of the stimulus and a well-supported judgement. So the target isn't more words. It's every demand covered, with **evidence**, in an order a marker can follow.

- The band meter draws at the right: a 0 to 7 scale shaded in four bands "6 to 7", "4 to 5", "2 to 3", "1". ### 4. take it apart
> Step one: take the question apart, as in the first skills video. There are two verbs. **Analyse** the data to explain the change in cases, and **assess** the catch-up program. There's a constraint: refer to immunity in individuals and in the **population**. And there's the stimulus, which you're expected to use. That's four demands, so write four small ticks in the margin, and tick each one as you cover it.

### 6. build a paragraph: PEEL

With fewer people immune, an infected person met more susceptible people, so chains of transmission kept going, and cases rose from eight to three hundred and ten. So the fall in coverage explains the **outbreak**.

### 8. use the stimulus, don't copy it
> Using the stimulus means more than copying it. A weak answer says cases went up, then down. A strong one picks the values that matter, compares them, and says what they **mean**: coverage dropped below the ninety five per cent needed, and that's when cases climbed. Quote the number, then interpret it.

A judgement with its **condition** is stronger than a bare yes.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Long answers (6 to 9 marks) are usually marked in bands, read as a whole. Top band: every demand, detailed use of the stimulus, a supported judgement.
2. Unpack: every verb, every constraint ("refer to", "with reference to"), the stimulus. Tick each demand in the margin as you cover it.

## Memory aid check

- **"Verdict first, PEEL each paragraph, verdict last."** PEEL (point, evidence, explain, link) is a common Australian school writing scaffold, not a NESA rule. Assumptions and limits: it fits explain, analyse, assess, evaluate and justify answers where each paragraph makes one claim from evidence; "verdict first and last" applies only when the verb asks for a judgement (assess, evaluate, justify, and analyse when it asks for an implication); for explain-only or describe questions, the first line states the answer to the question instead. A calculation or a compare answer uses its own structure (s05, s03). Scene 9 introduces "verdict first and last" with "Assess asks for a judgement", and key note 6 names the judgement verbs, so the limit is in learner copy.
- **"Quote the number, then say what it means."** Restates s06's "direction, data, change" for a stimulus inside a long answer. Assumption: the stimulus is quantitative; for a text stimulus, "quote the detail, then say what it means" (not needed here).
