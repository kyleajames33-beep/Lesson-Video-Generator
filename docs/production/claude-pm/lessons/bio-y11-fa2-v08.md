## Decisions

- **Main teaching move: make the record talk, then question it.** Three moves in order: process (decide what to do with the odd count, calculate means), reveal (plot against light intensity, read the steep part and the plateau, compare with the prediction, then a contrasted dataset that shows which factor capped the plateau), and question (did light cause it? what do bubbles actually measure?). The video never re-shows the set-up (route).
- **Processing (scene 2).** The 47 is left out of the mean, because the note records a cause unrelated to the plant's rate (a second stream from a leaf), and the recount 33 is used; the 47 stays in the record with its note. One mean is worked aloud (20 cm: 25, 27 and 26, mean 26); the others fill quietly (40 cm 8; 30 cm 14; 15 cm 33; 10 cm 35). The small spread in each row returns as evidence of reliability in scene 8.
- **Distance to light intensity (scene 3; site card 3 and the Predict walk-through's 1/d²).** Plotting rate against distance gives a falling curve that hides the prediction. Halving the distance roughly multiplies the light by four, because the light spreads out as it travels; relative light intensity = (10 ÷ d)² × 100 for a small lamp (grey tag only). The 3 s prediction beat: "halve the distance: does the light double?" Author note: the inverse square rule holds for a small (point-like) lamp; the heat shield absorbs a little light equally at every distance.
- **Reading the graph (scene 4).** On the steep part light is limiting; on the plateau another condition is. The data match the prediction, so they support the model; "support", not "prove" (site card 3; the Evaluate walk-through's "plausible but not established").
- **The contrasted dataset (scene 5; route "a contrasted condition-change dataset").** The same pondweed and distances, with three times as much sodium hydrogen carbonate (1.5% instead of 0.5%), so more carbon dioxide. Means only, illustrative: 8, 15, 30, 44, 58. The two curves almost match at low light (light limits both) and part at high light, where the higher-carbon-dioxide curve climbs to a higher plateau: so on the first plateau, carbon dioxide was the limiting factor. v06 showed this as a model; here it is read from data (v06 cited).
- **Causation (scene 6).** The temperature column reads 21 °C at every distance, so the heat shield worked and light was the only condition that changed between rows; between the two datasets, only the sodium hydrogen carbonate changed. Without that column, the rise could be heat (v07's hidden suspect). "A pattern on its own doesn't show a cause; a controlled change does."
- **Measurement limits: counts, volume, net and gross (scene 7; route stop and site flag).** Bubble counts: sizes vary and fast streams merge, so counts can miss some oxygen at high rates; gas volume avoids both. Dissolving: some oxygen dissolves before bubbles form, which matters most when the rate is low. Net and gross: the plant respires all the time, so bubbles measure net production (oxygen left over); gross production (all the oxygen made) is net plus what respiration used. In very dim light the plant can make oxygen and still release no bubbles. "Compensation point" is not named (author note).
- **The optimum from data (scene 5b; route stop "explain plateaus/optima").** A second contrasted dataset changes temperature (lamp close, plenty of carbon dioxide): means rise to a peak at 25 °C, then fall. The cause is v06's (enzymes faster, then denaturing), read here from data; the rule taught is to read the shape: levelling off means another factor has taken over; rising then falling means damage. Attempt 1 then tests how precisely an optimum can be located, which teaching does not cover.
- **Reliability and improvement (scene 8).** Repeats within 2 bubbles per minute of each other: consistent, so reliable. Improvements: gas volume instead of counts, and more distances near the bend.
- **Memory aid.** "Bubbles show what's left over." (Claude's; scene 7 after net and gross are explained, the decision rule and the notes; checked below.) It turns v07's phrase "the oxygen left over" into a rule for reading any oxygen data.
- **Site alignment (L06, kept in substance).** Card 3's "If the data match the prediction, they support the model" (4); the 1/d² step (3); the Evaluate walk-through's reliability-validity separation, the bubble-size criticism, the gas-volume improvement and the bounded judgement (6 to 8, 9b); vocabulary "Reliability ... improved by repeats and averaging" (2, 8); short answer 2's evaluation language (9a, 9b). ### 7. what the bubbles really measure
> One more question: what did we actually measure? Bubbles vary in size, and in a fast stream they merge, so counts can miss some oxygen. Gas volume avoids that. Some oxygen also dissolves in the water before it forms a bubble. And the plant respires all the time, using some of the oxygen it makes. So bubbles show net production, the oxygen left over. Gross production, all the oxygen made, is net plus what respiration used. In very dim light, a plant can make oxygen and still release no bubbles at **all**. Bubbles show what's left over.

### 9b. feedback 1 (brief)
> The mean is twenty-six. Thirty degrees gives the highest mean, but the readings are ten degrees apart, so the real optimum could be anywhere from about twenty to forty. > Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Process: leave a count out of the mean only with a recorded reason (keep it in the record); use the recount. Mean = total ÷ number of counts (20 cm: 78 ÷ 3 = 26 bubbles per min).
