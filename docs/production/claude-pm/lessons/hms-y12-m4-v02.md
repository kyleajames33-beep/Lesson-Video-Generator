### 2. what makes a test defensible
> In Year 11 you met two checks on any measurement: validity, whether it measures what it claims to, and reliability, whether it gives the same result again when the athlete hasn't changed. Testing adds two more. Specificity: does the test match this sport's demands? Timing a rower over a forty-metre sprint fails that. Objectivity: would two testers score it the same? A coach judging technique by eye fails that. A vertical jump is a valid test of leg power, and an invalid test of endurance, however carefully you run it. Reliability is bought with **protocol**, the fixed conditions, equipment and scoring rules. Same surface, shoes, time of day, warm-up and scorer. Each one you hold constant removes a rival explanation. That's why an unreliable test is worse than no test. It produces a number that looks like evidence.

### 3. choose the test from the limiter
> Testing starts from a question, not a test. Which quality is limiting this athlete right now? Name it, and the test follows. Aerobic capacity, a shuttle run. Strength, a one-rep max, the heaviest load lifted once. Power, a vertical jump. Speed, a timed sprint. The syllabus names two tests for a reason. The **Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test** isn't a continuous run. Shuttles alternate with short active rests, so it measures what team-sport players need: repeated efforts with incomplete recovery. The **Wingate test** is a thirty-second, all-out anaerobic sprint on a bike against a fixed resistance. It reports peak power, mean power, and how far power drops by the end. Say a cyclist peaks at a thousand watts and finishes at six hundred. The drop is four hundred, divided by a thousand, so a forty per cent **fatigue index**.

### 4. recreational participants and elite athletes
> The syllabus names two groups, and they want different things from testing. For a recreational participant, testing is about health, motivation and a safe starting point. The measures are simple: resting heart rate, blood pressure, waist size, and a **submaximal** aerobic estimate, where the person never works flat out. It only needs to catch a meaningful health change, every few months. For an elite athlete, testing diagnoses performance and informs selection. The measures are sport-specific, precise enough to catch a change smaller than normal day-to-day variation, and repeated through the season. And the risks differ. A maximal test can put a beginner off exercise, while for an elite athlete the cost is lost training time. So recreational testing supports health and participation, and elite testing earns its place only if it changes a decision.

### 5. four jobs: baseline, limiter, target, re-test
> Now, how assessment builds the program. It does four separate jobs. First, it sets the **baseline**, the first measurement that every later one is compared with. Intensity is set as a share of something measured, like the maximum heart rate from Year 11, or a one-rep max. Without a baseline, it's a guess. Second, it finds the **limiter**, the component holding performance back. Third, it makes the goal specific, a target score on a test the athlete has already done. Fourth, it closes the loop. Re-testing with the same protocol is the only evidence the block worked. A seventeen-year-old club netballer has a strong Yo-Yo score, an average vertical jump, and a big difference between legs on a single-leg hop. So the block puts lower-body power first, adds single-leg work, keeps aerobic work at maintenance, and re-tests both jumps, on the same surface, in the same shoes.

### 6. what a result doesn't tell you
> A result has four limits. Motivation is part of the score: trying harder the second time raises it with no change in fitness. Protocol drifts. Surface, shoes, heat, time of day, and who's counting all move a score. **Ecological validity**, how well a test copies the real sport, is limited. A bike in a lab has no tackles, decisions or changes of direction. And a test measures a component, not an athlete. Now back to the coach's beep test. Learning the test, trying harder, a different court or counter, and simply growing up all compete with the training. The fix isn't to stop testing. Standardise the protocol, judge change against normal variation, and read the results beside what the coach sees in games.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing. No official figures.
- **Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test (3).** Shuttles (2 × 20 m) at increasing speeds separated by short active recovery (10 s in the standard protocol); it assesses the ability to repeat intense efforts with incomplete recovery; suited to intermittent team sports.
- **Wingate test (3).** A 30 s all-out cycle-ergometer sprint against a fixed resistance set from body mass; reports peak power, mean power and a fatigue index (percentage drop from peak to the end or the lowest value). Illustrative arithmetic: (1000 − 600) ÷ 1000 = 0.40 = 40%. Checked.
- **One-repetition maximum (3).** The heaviest load lifted once with correct technique; often predicted from a submaximal set.
- **Recreational and elite (4).** The site's six-row table: submaximal estimates preferred for beginners; elite testing precise enough to detect changes smaller than day-to-day variation.
- **Four limits (6).** Motivation, protocol drift, ecological validity, components not athletes, and growth and maturation in a young squad (the revisit card).
- **Halfback battery (8c).** Site values (85th, 40th, 35th, 30th, 70th); limiter and re-tests as the site's indicative answer.
