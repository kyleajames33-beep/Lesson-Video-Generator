### 7. key-notes (reuse take: key-notes; quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. For the alkene and acid examples practised here, start by finding the backbone (the atoms through the brackets). All C–C, from an alkene: addition. Ester (–COO–) or amide (–CONH–) links in the backbone: condensation, and when the question states the reaction, use it. Side groups do not count.
2. Addition, drawing: only the two C=C carbons join the backbone; everything else hangs off. A section of three units has three side groups and six backbone carbons. Recovering: restore the C=C; add no H.
3. Condensation, recovering: find every link; add water back at each (OH to the C=O side, H to the O or N side). Each monomer has a reactive group at both ends (one molecule can carry both, as lactic acid does).
4. Properties: compare what holds the chains together. N–H on the chains: hydrogen bonds to C=O on the next chain, much higher melting point. Melting overcomes forces between chains; it does not break the links in the chain. In an answer, name the property, give its structural reason and link it to the use.

## Practice questions and model answers

1. (Q1.) But-1-ene, CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃, forms an addition polymer that is used to make hot-water pipes. Draw a section of this polymer showing three repeat units. Circle one repeat unit.
   Model: –CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₃)–, in full structural form with a continuation bond at each end; one –CH₂–CH(CH₂CH₃)– circled. Backbone six carbons, three ethyl side groups, only single bonds. Crossed mistake: "–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–" (the ethyl group put into the backbone).
2. (Q2.) A section of a polymer used in carpet fibres is shown (–O–CH₂CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–CH₂CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–CO–, drawn). (a) Identify the type of polymer. (b) Draw and name the monomers used to make this polymer.
   Model: (a) The backbone contains ester links (–COO–), so it is a polyester, a condensation polymer. (b) Adding water back at each ester link (OH to the C=O carbon, H to the O): propane-1,3-diol, HO–CH₂CH₂CH₂–OH, and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (terephthalic acid), HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH, drawn in full. Crossed mistake: "HO–CH₂CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–COOH" (one link left uncut).
3. (Q3.) The repeat units of two polymers are shown: X, [–CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–]ₙ; Y, [–O–CH(CH₃)–CO–]ₙ (drawn). (a) Classify each polymer as an addition polymer or a condensation polymer. Justify each classification. (b) Draw the monomer of each polymer. (Y card line: Y was made directly from a hydroxy acid, with water released.)
   Model: (a) X: addition. Its backbone is two carbons joined by a C–C bond; the ester group is in a side chain, not a link in the backbone. Y: condensation (a polyester). Its backbone contains an ester link, –O–C(=O)–, and the question states that it formed by direct reaction of a hydroxy acid with water released. (b) X: CH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃ (methyl 2-methylpropenoate), formed by restoring the C=C with no extra H. Y: CH₃CH(OH)COOH (2-hydroxypropanoic acid, lactic acid), formed by adding water back at the ester link; one monomer carries both the –OH and –COOH. Crossed mistake: "X has an ester group, so X is a polyester."
4. (Q4.) Nylon 6,6, [–NH–(CH₂)₆–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–]ₙ, melts at about 265 °C. Polyester P, [–O–(CH₂)₆–O–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–]ₙ, melts at about 60 °C. Explain the difference in the melting points of these two polymers, with reference to their structures.
   Model: Both have the same carbon skeleton ((CH₂)₆, (CH₂)₄ and two C=O per repeat unit), so the comparison is of their links. Nylon 6,6 has N–H groups in its amide links, which form hydrogen bonds with C=O groups on neighbouring chains. P has C=O groups but no H bonded to O or N, so its chains are held only by dipole–dipole and dispersion forces. Melting requires the chains to separate and move past each other; overcoming nylon's hydrogen bonds needs much more energy, so hydrogen bonding is an important structural reason nylon melts at a much higher temperature. (Packing also matters; this does not account for the size of the difference.) Crossed mistake: "Amide bonds are stronger than ester bonds, so they take more energy to break when it melts."
