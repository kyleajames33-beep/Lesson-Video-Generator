## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "Why does light spread after passing an edge or narrow opening?" The answer: light is a wave; every point on a wavefront sends out wavelets (3); at an edge or gap, the wavelets at the boundary have no neighbours to keep the front straight, so they spread into the shadow (4); the effect is large only when the opening is comparable to the wavelength (5).

- **Water first (Year 11 bridge).** The ripple-tank plate gives a familiar visible case; then the same geometry with light.
- **Huygens' construction (site lesson 10 Figure 1).** Shown on an open wavefront first (stays straight), then at a gap and at an edge. The phrase "the line touching the front of every wavelet" is used rather than "envelope".

A 0.1 mm slit is 200 wavelengths of 500 nm light and gives a visibly spread pattern.

### 2. waves through a gap
> You can see the same thing with water. In a ripple tank, straight waves roll towards a barrier with a gap in it. Beyond a wide gap, the waves carry on mostly straight, bending only a little at the **edges**. Make the gap about one wavelength wide, and the waves spread out in semicircles, right round into the regions behind the **barrier**. This spreading is called diffraction. And light does it too, because light is a wave.

### 3. every point is a source
> Why do waves spread like that? In the 1600s, Christiaan Huygens had an idea. Treat every point on a wavefront as a tiny source, sending out its own little circular wave, called a **wavelet**. A moment later, the new wavefront is the line touching the front of every wavelet. On a wide, open wavefront, each wavelet has neighbours on both sides, so the sideways parts fill in, and the front just moves straight **ahead**.

Visual: board change to the Huygens stage: a straight vertical teal wavefront as a row of dots. On "tiny source" each dot pulses; on "wavelet" small teal circles grow from every dot. On "ahead" the construction repeats a few steps; the front advances straight; tag "open front: stays straight".

### 4. at an edge or a gap
> Now put a barrier in the way, with a gap. Only the points in the gap can send wavelets **on**. In the middle of the gap, the wavelets still have neighbours, so that part of the front stays straight. But at each edge, there's nothing beyond to fill in. Those wavelets spread round, into the shadow behind the **barrier**. A single edge does the same thing. Make the gap narrower, and more of the front is near an edge. In a very narrow gap, the whole front behaves like one wavelet, and it spreads out like a **semicircle**.

### 5. why shadows usually look sharp
> So why don't we see light bending round doorways? It's about size. Spreading is strong only when the gap is not much bigger than the wavelength. Visible light has a wavelength of about five hundred nanometres, so a doorway is about two million wavelengths **wide**. The spreading is far too small to notice. Sound has wavelengths around a metre, which is why you hear someone round a corner but can't see **them**. To see light diffract, you need a slit about a tenth of a millimetre **wide**.

### 6. the pattern on the screen

Up close, it has fine bright and dark **fringes**.

### 7. Newton or Huygens?
> In the late sixteen hundreds, there were two models of light. Isaac Newton said light is a stream of tiny particles, which he called corpuscles, flying in straight lines. Huygens said light is a wave. Both could explain reflection, so reflection couldn't decide between them. But they disagreed on two things. Straight-line particles should cast perfectly sharp shadows, and never spread through a gap. And to bend towards the normal on entering water, Newton's particles had to be pulled in, so they'd speed **up**. Huygens' waves had to slow **down**. For about a century, most scientists followed Newton. His reputation was enormous, and with light's tiny wavelength, its spreading was easy to **miss**.

### 8. the evidence decides
> Then the evidence came in. Light was shown to spread into shadows, which only waves **do**. In 1850, Foucault put water in his spinning-mirror apparatus, and found light travels slower in water, just as the wave model **predicted**. And there was a famous surprise. Siméon Poisson pointed out that the wave theory predicted a bright spot right in the centre of the shadow of a round **disc**. He thought that was absurd. François Arago looked, and found the spot. In each case, the two models predicted different things, and the wave prediction **won**. The strongest evidence of all, interference, is next.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Diffraction: waves spread as they pass an edge or through a gap.
2. Huygens' principle: every point on a wavefront is a source of wavelets; the new wavefront is the line touching them all.
3. At an edge, the wavelets have no neighbours beyond, so the wave curves into the geometric shadow.
4. Spreading is large when the gap is comparable to λ. Narrower slit or longer λ: wider pattern (wide bright centre, fainter side bands, dark gaps from cancellation).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, 3 marks).** "Laser light passes through a single narrow slit onto a screen. When the slit is made narrower, the central bright band on the screen becomes wider. Use Huygens' principle to explain this observation."
Model answer: By Huygens' principle every point on the wavefront within the slit acts as a source of secondary wavelets, and the new wavefront is the line tangent to them (1 mark). At the edges of the slit there are no neighbouring wavelets beyond the edge, so the wave spreads into the geometric shadow (1 mark). A narrower slit makes the transmitted wavefront more like a single wavelet (the slit width is closer to the wavelength), so the light spreads through a larger angle and the central band widens (1 mark). Classic mistake (crossed): "less light gets through, so the band is narrower".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, 3 marks).** "Newton proposed that light is a stream of particles; Huygens proposed that light is a wave. Explain how (a) measurements of the speed of light in water and (b) observations of the diffraction of light provided evidence to distinguish between these models."
Model answer: (a) Newton's model explained refraction towards the normal by an attraction that speeds the particles up in water, whereas Huygens' model requires light to slow down; Foucault (1850) measured light to be slower in water, supporting the wave model (1.5 marks). (b) Particles travelling in straight lines would produce sharp shadows and no spreading through narrow gaps; light is observed to spread into the geometric shadow, as Huygens' wavelets predict, supporting the wave model (1.5 marks). Classic mistake (crossed): citing reflection, which both models explain.
