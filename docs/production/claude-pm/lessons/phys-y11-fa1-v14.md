## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route, root wording).** "How do net force and mass together set an object's acceleration?" The answer, built in order: with the mass fixed, doubling the net force doubles the gain in velocity each second (2); with the net force fixed, doubling the mass halves it, because more inertia means the same force changes the velocity less (3); together, a = F_net/m, which defines the newton and fixes the direction of a (4); so the net force, not any single force, goes in (5); read backwards, an acceleration gives the net force, which is not the driving force (6); and the same law explains why heavy and light objects fall together, giving W = mg (7).

### 4. putting it together
> Put the two together, and you get Newton's second law. The acceleration equals the net force divided by the mass. As it's usually written, the net force equals mass times acceleration. Half a newton on one kilogram gives half a metre per second squared. On two kilograms, a quarter. This also defines the unit of force. One newton is the net force that gives one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared. And because force and acceleration are vectors, a nonzero acceleration points in the direction of the net **force**.

### 5. worked example: net force first
> Now a box on a real floor, where friction matters. A four point oh kilogram box is pushed with thirty newtons, while friction of six point oh newtons opposes it. Find its acceleration. The law needs the net force, so find that first. Friction acts against the push, so the net force is thirty minus six point oh, twenty four newtons **forward**. Then the acceleration is twenty four divided by four point oh, which is six point oh metres per second squared, **forward**. Divide the thirty newton push by the mass instead, and you get seven point five. That's too big, because it ignores the friction holding the box **back**.

### 6. working backwards
> The law works backwards too. A twelve hundred kilogram car speeds up steadily from rest to twenty five metres per second in ten seconds. But that's the net force, not the driving force. Air resistance and friction push back too, so the driving force must be more than three thousand **newtons**.

### 7. why heavy and light objects fall together
> Here's a puzzle the law solves. Drop a heavy steel ball and a light one together, and they land together. But the heavy one has twice the mass, so why doesn't it accelerate less? Because gravity pulls it twice as hard. When an object falls freely, with air resistance tiny, its weight is the only force, so its weight is the net force. Every falling object accelerates at g, about nine point eight metres per second squared, so its weight is its mass times g. That's W equals m g. Twice the mass, twice the weight, the same acceleration. Mass is in kilograms, and weight is a force in newtons. A two point oh kilogram crate weighs nineteen point six **newtons**.
