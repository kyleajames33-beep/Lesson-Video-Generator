## Decisions

- **Why a model, and why this one (route start).** The route allows "a safe observational or secondary-data method". Measuring classmates' skin temperature is a human-participant activity (school procedure, consent, and it would need people on screen); testing on live animals is neither practical nor ethical for a school. Route review change 6 chose a model. A model is first-hand, fully controllable, needs no participant and carries only low, well-known hazards (warm water, glassware, spills). The design says so in one sentence (scene 1) and names the trade-off honestly in scene 10: a model can test heat loss through a covering, not regulation. If root wants secondary data as well, see Open decisions.
- **Main teaching move: a model can lose heat, but it can't regulate.** The flask tests one structural trend from b02 (insulation slows heat loss) under fair conditions, and the video ends by naming exactly what a flask lacks (metabolic heat and a feedback loop). The general move students keep, shared with p02 ("claim only what you measured"): **claim only what the model can show.** Memory line: **"The flask can lose heat, but it can't fight back."**
- **The investigation.** Aim: find out how an insulating layer affects the rate of heat loss from a model body. Model: a 250 mL conical flask holding 150 mL of warm water stands in for the body; an even layer of cotton wool held by two rubber bands stands in for fur (both trap still air). IV: insulation (wrapped or bare). DV: temperature drop in 20 minutes (°C), from readings every 4 minutes. Controlled: flask type and size, volume (150 mL, measured with a measuring cylinder), starting temperature (timing starts when each probe reads 45.0 °C), the same card lid with a probe hole (similar evaporation), the same place on the bench, away from windows, heaters and sunlight, same room on the same day. Control: the bare flask (the comparison without insulation). Room temperature recorded (21 °C in the example; screen only). Measurement: a temperature probe and data logger reading to 0.1 °C through each lid (a spirit thermometer read at eye level is the alternative, grey tag). Repeats: three trials per condition, from the same start. Wrapping thickness kept even and dry (author note: wet cotton wool conducts heat and evaporates water, which would add a second variable; one grey tag in scene 10).
- **The prediction beat (scene 5; the stop's "evaluate control variables").** Before any data, students predict whether water at 60 °C or at 45 °C loses heat faster in a 21 °C room. The answer (the hotter one, because the difference from the air is bigger) is the cause behind the most important control (same starting temperature), the reason the curves flatten (scene 8), and the core of attempt 2. A 2 s thinking hold with a "Predict" chip, not a countdown (it is a quick check, not a practice question). b02's "heat flows from hotter to cooler" is recalled, not re-taught.
- **Constructed data (labelled "example data" on screen).** Trial 1, every 4 minutes from 0 to 20: bare 45.0, 41.9, 39.1, 36.8, 34.7, 32.9 °C (drop 12.1); wrapped 45.0, 43.6, 42.3, 41.0, 39.9, 38.8 °C (drop 6.2). Generated from Newton's law of cooling with room 21 °C, k = 0.035 per minute (bare) and 0.015 per minute (wrapped), rounded to 0.1 °C. Repeat drops: bare 12.1, 11.7, 12.2 (mean 12.0, spread 0.5); wrapped 6.2, 6.5, 5.9 (mean 6.2, spread 0.6); gap 5.8; ratio about 1.9 ("roughly halved"). Average rates: 12.1 ÷ 20 ≈ 0.6 °C per minute; 6.2 ÷ 20 ≈ 0.3 °C per minute. Checked under "Numbers to verify".
- **Graphing (scene 7; the route stop's "graph response data").** Time on the horizontal axis (the student chose when to read it), temperature on the vertical axis, both labelled with units, each reading plotted as a point, a smooth curve through each set (cooling data curve; no straight line of best fit), a key. Built with m8-shared's coded `TAxes`, `TLine` and `THLine` (room temperature line). The table's unit lives in its column heading.
- **What the model cannot show (scene 10; the stop's evaluation).** The flask makes no heat and has no receptors, control centre or effectors, so it cannot regulate; a real endotherm keeps producing metabolic heat and its feedback loop changes blood flow, shivering and behaviour (b01, b02 recalled in one sentence). Real fur can be raised or flattened (piloerection; the site's own card) and can get wet. Narrow conclusion: "an insulating layer slowed heat loss from this model."
- **VRA (scene 11), in s07's words.** Validity: "does it measure what it was meant to?" Supported by the controls for heat loss through a covering (same flask, volume, start and place); not valid as a test of how an animal regulates its temperature. Reliability: "do repeats agree?" Yes, within about half a degree, against a gap of nearly six. Accuracy: "how close to the true value?" A probe tip touching the glass reads cooler than the water; keep the tip in the middle of the water; more repeats cannot fix that (s07's "repeats help reliability, but they can't fix an error that pushes every trial the same way", said in this video's own words).
- **Risk table (scene 12).** Three spoken rows, each hazard, harm and a control for this practical: warm water (scalds: the teacher prepares it no hotter than about 50 °C; flasks stand on the bench, not held); glassware (cuts: check for chips; report breakages; do not pick up broken glass by hand); spills (slips, wet electrical equipment: mop up at once; keep the logger and leads dry). Teacher-note rows, not shown: the kettle or urn used to prepare the water is teacher-only; spirit-filled thermometers only, never mercury, and never push a glass thermometer through a bung (the card lid has a loose hole); cotton wool (low hazard; keep away from heat sources). Shown table: only the row being spoken (p02's rule); earlier rows collapse to one-word grey chips. **Safety choice:** the start temperature is 45 °C, near the 45 °C delivery limit Australian standards set for hand basins in schools, and the preparation limit is "about 50 °C". If the school's procedure sets a lower limit, the same method works from 40 °C (smaller drops; same pattern). Codex to verify (Safety facts check).
- **Site alignment (L02, kept in substance).** "Fur, feathers and blubber all work by trapping a layer that conducts heat poorly, either still air or lipid, between the core and the environment. This slows heat transfer in both directions" (scene 2, said plainly; scene 9's reason); "Heat exchange happens across a surface, so the ratio of surface area to volume matters. Animals in cold climates tend to be more compact and rounded" (attempt 1 and its feedback); "Arrector pili muscles contract and raise the hairs ... traps a thicker layer of still air" (scene 10's "real fur can be raised"); the page's learning goal "How insulation and surface area affect heat transfer" (the two model variables). Not used: the cooling effectors, the kangaroo, the flying fox case and heat stroke (b02 taught them); the logged site errors (60 to 80 per cent, "only route", Penrith, 40 °C) are not touched by this video.

### 2. how insulation works, and the model
> Recall that heat flows from a warmer body to cooler air. Fur traps a layer of still **air**, and still air passes heat on slowly, so heat leaves more slowly. A **model** is a simpler object that stands in for the real thing. Here, a flask of warm water stands in for the body, and a layer of cotton **wool** stands in for fur, because it traps still air too.

### 9. repeats, means and the conclusion
> Repeat the trial three times, starting from forty five each time. The bare flask's drops average **twelve**, and the wrapped flask's average six point **two**. ## Safety facts check (self-check, 31 Oct; science check to confirm)

- **Warm water.** Scald time falls steeply with temperature: commonly cited adult full-thickness burn times are about 5 minutes at 49 °C, about 30 seconds at 54 °C and about 5 seconds at 60 °C (shorter for children). Australian plumbing standards (AS/NZS 3500.4 and state rules) limit heated water at personal hygiene outlets to 50 °C in homes and 45 °C in schools, early childhood and aged care settings. Design choice: the teacher prepares water no hotter than about 50 °C; timing starts at 45 °C; flasks stand on the bench and are not held. Learner copy: "Warm water can scald, so your teacher prepares it no hotter than about fifty degrees, and the flasks stay on the bench." - **Glassware.** Conical flasks and beakers: check for chips and cracks; stand on the bench away from the edge; report breakages; the teacher clears broken glass with a dustpan, not by hand. Learner copy as scene 12.
- **Electrical equipment and spills.** Low-voltage probes and a data logger (or laptop) near water: keep the logger and leads dry, mop spills at once, keep mains-powered devices away from the water (tagged and tested, per school procedure).
- **Teacher notes (not in learner copy).** The kettle or urn used to heat the water is teacher-only, on a stable surface, cord clear. Spirit-filled thermometers only, never mercury; never push a glass thermometer through a rubber bung (the card lid has a loose hole). Cotton wool: low hazard; keep it dry and away from heat sources. Codex to confirm against NSW DoE science safety guidance and a school risk assessment for heating water.
