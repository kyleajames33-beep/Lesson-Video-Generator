### 1. hook: fours and sixes
> Here are three x plus two y equals sixteen, and three x minus y equals seven, and both have exactly three x. Hold that thought, and look at this cricket scoreboard. A batter hit eleven boundaries, some fours and some sixes, worth fifty-two runs **altogether**. How many fours, and how many sixes? With f fours and s sixes, f plus s equals eleven, and four f plus six s equals fifty-**two**. Neither equation has a letter on its own. There's a method that gets rid of one letter in a single step, by adding or subtracting whole equations. Getting rid of a letter is called eliminating it, so this is elimination. So how does elimination work, and when do we add instead of **subtract**?

### 2. subtract when the signs match
> Start with the pair that both have three x. Stack them, one under the other, with matching terms lined up. Equal amounts taken from equal amounts leave equal amounts, so we can take the second equation away from the first, every term. Three x take away three x is zero, so x is **gone**. Two y, take away minus y, is two y plus y, which is three **y**. Careful there. Taking away a minus is like cancelling a debt. If someone takes away a ten dollar **debt**, you're ten dollars better off, so taking away minus y adds y. And sixteen take away seven is nine. So three y equals nine, and y equals **three**. Now swap y equals three into either equation. Three x minus three equals seven, so three x equals ten, and x equals ten **thirds**. Keep it as a fraction. If you round it to three point three, the check won't come out exactly. Check in the first equation. Three times ten thirds is ten, plus six is **sixteen**. Writing two y, take away minus y, as just y, is the classic **slip**.

### 3. add when the signs are opposite
> Now try two x plus y equals eleven, and three x minus y equals four. This time the y terms match, but one is plus y and one is minus y. Subtracting would give two y, which doesn't help. So **add** the equations instead. Plus y and minus y make zero, so y disappears. Two x plus three x is five x, and eleven plus four is fifteen. So five x equals fifteen, and x equals **three**. Then two times three, plus y, is eleven, so y equals **five**. Check in the second equation: nine take away five is four. Here's the rule. Same signs, subtract. Opposite signs, **add**. Either way, you're choosing whatever makes the matching terms add up to zero.

### 5. which method?
> So now you have three ways in. Which one fits? Look at the equations before you start. If both start with y equals, like y equals two x plus three, and y equals nine minus x, set them **equal**. If one letter is already alone, like y equals three x minus one, and two x plus y equals nine, **substitute**. And if the same letter has the same number in front, like five x plus two y equals twenty-four, and five x minus three y equals four, eliminate. Here the signs match, so **subtract**. Any method gives the same answer in the end. The best one is just the one with the fewest steps.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 7; pack question 2, original, HSC style, 2 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "Solve the simultaneous equations 2x + 3y = 19 and 2x − y = 3 by elimination."
Model answer: subtract the second from the first: 3y − (−y) = 4y = 16, so y = 4. Then 2x − 4 = 3, 2x = 7 and x = 3.5 (or 7/2). Check: 2(3.5) + 3(4) = 7 + 12 = 19 ✓. Classic mistake (crossed): "2y = 16, y = 8".

**Attempt 2 (scene 9; original, HSC style, 2 marks suggested).** "Solve the simultaneous equations x + y = 9 and 2x + 5y = 30."
Model answer: multiply the first by 2: 2x + 2y = 18. Subtract from the second: 3y = 12, so y = 4. Then x = 9 − 4 = 5. Check: 2(5) + 5(4) = 30 ✓. Classic mistake (crossed): "2x + 2y = 9".
