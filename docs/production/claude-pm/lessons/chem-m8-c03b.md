Ammonia: AgCl dissolves in dilute (about 2 mol/L) NH₃; AgBr dissolves (at least partly) in concentrated NH₃; AgI is essentially insoluble even in concentrated NH₃. "Doesn't dissolve, even in concentrated" is said for AgI; AgBr in concentrated ammonia can dissolve only partly in some school conditions, so the video says "needs concentrated ammonia". AgCl darkens in light (site; not used).

In acid: PO₄³⁻ + H⁺ → HPO₄²⁻, then H₂PO₄⁻; silver hydrogen phosphates do not precipitate under these conditions. Many phosphate salts are sodium hydrogen phosphates at near-neutral pH; Ag₃PO₄ still precipitates from neutral HPO₄²⁻ solutions as silver removes phosphate. Alternative: ammonium molybdate in HNO₃, warmed, gives a yellow precipitate (ammonium phosphomolybdate); not used. Barium phosphate is white and dissolves in acid, which is a second reason acid is added before barium (not said; carbonate is the example).

Confirmation with neutral FeCl₃: deep red-brown colour from the basic iron(III) acetate complex ([Fe₃O(CH₃COO)₆]⁺); on boiling a red-brown precipitate forms (not mentioned). The FeCl₃ must be neutral (in acid the acetate is protonated). Esterification with ethanol and concentrated H₂SO₄ (fruity smell, site) not used; it uses concentrated acid and belongs with Module 7.
  (e) Hydroxide: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃(g) + H₂O(l), gentle warming; NH₃ turns damp red litmus blue. Carbonate and phosphate solutions are basic (CO₃²⁻ and PO₄³⁻ are bases) and can release some ammonia on warming with ammonium salts, which is why the video tests hydroxide last, after ruling them out. Acetate solutions are only very weakly basic (pH about 8 to 9 for 0.1 mol/L sodium acetate) and may turn red litmus faintly blue; not mentioned; the acetate test comes earlier.
  (f) Sulfate: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s), white, insoluble in dilute acid (Ksp 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁰, data sheet). Acidify with dilute HCl or HNO₃; then BaCl₂ or Ba(NO₃)₂.
  (g) Mixtures: tests use separate portions; the phosphate test needs a neutral portion, so it can't follow an acidified halide test in the same portion. If a sample may hold carbonate and sulfate together, acidifying removes the carbonate before barium is added.
  (h) Hook closure: copper(II) sulfate solution: no fizz, no smell, white BaSO₄ with acidified barium, no silver precipitate in acid, no ammonia. Copper(II) ions would also react with ammonia in later confirmation steps (deep blue), and with hydroxide; the closure uses only the barium result.
  (i) Pronunciations: "acetate" as "ASS-uh-tate"; "ethanoic" as "eth-uh-NO-ick"; "effervescence" not spoken; "cream" for AgBr.
  (j) Hazards (not said in this video beyond "waft, don't sniff"; p03 carries the risk table): silver nitrate stains and is corrosive; barium salts are toxic; concentrated ammonia fumes; ammonia gas irritant; heavy-metal waste to labelled containers.

### 1. hook (copper what?)
> Back to the prep room. A pale blue precipitate, a deep blue complex and a blue-green flame told us one bottle holds copper ions. But copper what? Copper chloride, copper sulfate and copper acetate all dissolve, and all give the same copper results. The cation's only half the answer. Now we need the anion, and the list has eight: chloride, bromide, iodide, hydroxide, acetate, carbonate, sulfate and **phosphate**.

### 2. step 1: dilute acid, and the fizz
> The plan's the same as for cations: fresh portions, and reagents that react with only a few ions. Start with dilute acid. Carbonate ions take hydrogen ions and break down into carbon dioxide and water, so the tube **fizzes**. Bubble the gas through limewater, and it turns milky as calcium carbonate **forms**. Fizzing that turns limewater milky points to carbonate. Not every anion test makes a precipitate.

### 3. acetate: no fizz, a smell
> Acetate takes hydrogen ions too, but it doesn't make a gas. It becomes ethanoic acid, the acid in vinegar. So there's no fizz, but if you warm the tube gently, you'll notice a vinegar **smell**. Waft it towards you, don't sniff straight from the tube. To confirm, add neutral iron three chloride to a fresh portion. Acetate ions bond around the iron ions and form a complex with a deep red-brown **colour**.

### 4. why acidify first, and with which acid
> The next tests use barium ions and silver ions. But carbonate forms solids with both, so it could fake a positive result. The fix is to acidify first. The acid turns any carbonate into carbon dioxide, so it can't **precipitate**. Here's the catch: which acid? For the sulfate test, hydrochloric acid is fine, since chloride doesn't precipitate with barium. But in the halide test, it would add chloride, the very ion you're testing for. So use dilute **nitric** acid. Nitrates are all soluble, so nitrate can't fake a result.

Visual: a mini inset: CO₃²⁻ dots meet Ba²⁺ and lock as white tiles; tag "BaCO₃: white" and a red "false positive?" chip; then Ag⁺ with CO₃²⁻ as pale yellow tiles, "Ag₂CO₃". On "precipitate" orange H⁺ dots turn the CO₃²⁻ dots into rising CO₂ bubbles; the tiles never form; chip "acidify first: carbonate leaves as CO₂" (bright). Two acid bottles as coded chips: "dilute HCl" and "dilute HNO₃". On "halide" the HCl chip draws a green Cl⁻ dot into the silver inset, a white AgCl tile forms on its own, and the chip is crossed (caption "adds Cl⁻: false positive"). On "nitric" the HNO₃ chip glows; tag "nitrates all soluble (NAGSAG)". The other chips dim.

### 5. step 2: sulfate with barium
> Now sulfate. Acidify a fresh portion, then add barium chloride or barium nitrate solution. A white precipitate of barium **sulfate** means sulfate. It stays put in the acid, where barium carbonate would have dissolved. It's the same white solid you weighed in gravimetric analysis, so this reaction both detects sulfate and measures it.

Visual: an amber arrow writes: "dilute HNO₃ (or HCl), then Ba²⁺". ### 6. step 3: the halides with silver
> For the halides, acidify a fresh portion with nitric acid, then add silver nitrate. The halides are the exception in the solubility rules when they meet silver, so silver locks onto any halide present. Each halide gives a silver solid, and the colour deepens as you go down the group. Silver chloride is **white**. Silver bromide is **cream**. Silver iodide is pale **yellow**.

Visual: a grey-silver arrow writes: "dilute HNO₃, then AgNO₃ (Ag⁺)". A grey chip "c13: halides insoluble with Ag⁺" writes on "exception". ### 7. ammonia confirms
> Cream and pale yellow can look alike in a test tube, so confirm with ammonia. Ammonia molecules bond to silver ions and form a complex, which pulls the solid back into solution. The less soluble the silver halide, the harder that is. Silver chloride dissolves in dilute ammonia. Silver bromide needs **concentrated** ammonia. And silver iodide stays put, even in concentrated **ammonia**.

Visual: the ammonia ladder: the three tubes stay; a violet arrow "dilute NH₃" drops into all three; AgCl cross-fades to `m5x-no-ppt-clear-v1.png`, the others stay; the complex wrap inset shows NH₃ around Ag⁺ (c03a). ### 8. phosphate: a yellow that isn't iodide
> Phosphate gives a yellow silver solid too, silver phosphate. So how do you keep it apart from iodide? Test phosphate in a neutral solution, not an acidified one. In acid, phosphate ions pick up hydrogen ions and stop precipitating with silver, which is also why acidifying keeps phosphate out of the halide test. So add silver nitrate to a neutral portion, and a yellow precipitate suggests **phosphate**. Then add dilute nitric acid. Silver phosphate **dissolves**, but silver iodide doesn't.

### 9. hydroxide: last, and why
> Last, hydroxide. A hydroxide solution is basic, so it turns red litmus blue. But carbonate and phosphate solutions are basic too, so rule those out first. Then warm a portion with an ammonium salt, like ammonium chloride. Hydroxide ions take a hydrogen ion from each ammonium ion and release ammonia gas. Hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube, and it turns **blue**.

Visual: a blue arrow writes: "warm with NH₄⁺ salt". `m6-litmus-red-v1.png` small first, with a chip "basic: CO₃²⁻, PO₄³⁻ too" (grey) and dotted lines to the carbonate and phosphate boxes, which glow faintly ("ruled out first"). ### 10. the anion flowchart
> Here's the full anion flowchart. Dilute acid first: a fizz for carbonate, a vinegar smell on warming for acetate. Then acidified barium for sulfate, and acidified silver for the halides, with ammonia to split them. Then neutral silver for phosphate, and the ammonium test for hydroxide, last. Each test uses a fresh portion, so one reagent can't spoil the **next**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why carbonate fizzes: it takes hydrogen ions and breaks down to CO₂ (2). Why limewater goes milky: calcium carbonate forms (2).
