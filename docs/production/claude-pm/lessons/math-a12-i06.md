### 1. hook (what do the rectangles add up to?)
> Here's cos x, from zero to pi over two, with eight thin rectangles standing against it. Let's give the curve a meaning. Water flows into a tank, and cos x is the flow rate, in litres per minute, x minutes after a tap starts to close. At the start, it's one litre per **minute**. By pi over two minutes, about one and a half, the tap is **shut**. So how much water went in? The rate keeps changing, so we can't just multiply rate by time. But adding up thin rectangles gives an estimate, and refining them makes it better.

### 2. one strip: rate times width
> Take one thin strip of time, with width delta x. Delta x just means a small change in x, here a short stretch of time. Over that short time, the rate hardly changes. Call the rate f of x. So the water that flows in is about rate times time. On the graph, that's the rectangle's height times its **width**, its area. Litres per minute, times minutes, gives **litres**. So each rectangle's area estimates the water that flowed in during its strip. That's the change in the quantity over one small step.

### 3. add the strips: an estimate of total change
> Now add up all the strips. This sign, a capital sigma, means add them all up. The sum of f of x times delta x, from a to b, estimates the total change from a to **b**. With four strips, using the heights at their left ends, the total is about one point one eight litres. The rate falls across every strip, and each rectangle uses the rate at its start, so each one reaches above the curve. That's an **overestimate**. The height can come from any x in the strip's base. Use the right ends, and every rectangle sits under the curve. That gives about zero point seven nine, an underestimate. The true amount is somewhere in **between**.

### 4. thinner strips
> So make the strips thinner. Delta x is the whole interval, b minus a, split into n equal strips. So delta x equals b minus a, over **n**. Double n to eight, and the left sum drops to about one point one. Sixteen strips gives about one point zero five, and thirty-two gives about one point zero **two**. Meanwhile, the right sums climb towards the same place. Here's why. In a thinner strip, the rate changes less, so the gap between each rectangle and the curve **shrinks**. Both estimates close in on one litre. A later video shows why it's exactly one.

Visual: right column: "Δx = (b − a)/n" (equation strip). On "n" the givens group "a = 0, b = π/2, n = 4" with "Δx = π/8". ### 5. the region, and continuity
> Every estimate so far is an area. So the total change is the area of this region, between the curve and the x-axis, from a to b. That works when the curve stays on or above the axis, like this one. This curve is also continuous on the interval. Informally, that means you can draw it from one end to the other without taking the pen off the **paper**. ### 6. trapezia
> Rectangles have flat tops, but the curve slopes. So join the top points with straight lines instead. Each strip becomes a trapezium, a shape with two parallel sides. With just four trapezia, the estimate is about zero point nine eight seven. That's closer than thirty-two **rectangles**. Here the curve bends downwards, so each straight top sits just under it, and the trapezia slightly underestimate.

### 15. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. If f(x) is a rate, one strip's area f(x) × Δx is about the change over that strip. Σ f(x)Δx, from a to b, estimates the total change.
2. Δx = (b − a)/n. A rectangle's height is f(x) at any x in its base. For a falling rate, left heights overestimate and right heights underestimate.
3. More strips (n bigger, Δx → 0) give better estimates. A trapezium's area is the average of its left and right rectangles; on smooth curves like these, trapezia improve fastest.
4. For a continuous f (drawn without lifting the pen) with f(x) ≥ 0, the estimates approximate the area between y = f(x) and the x-axis from a to b.

A tap's flow rate keeps changing, so how much water goes in? Each thin rectangle under a rate graph is rate times width, a small piece of change, and adding them estimates the total change. See why left and right heights bracket the answer for a falling rate, why thinner strips close in, and why a trapezium (the average of the two rectangles) does better. Then compare rectangles and trapezia on one example as the number of strips doubles. Two exam-style questions at the end.

Mathematics Advanced 11-12 (2024 syllabus), Year 12, integral calculus.
