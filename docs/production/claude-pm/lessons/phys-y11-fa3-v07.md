- **Author notes (not learner copy):**
  (a) Charge on the inner faces: for an isolated pair connected to a supply, the charge sits on the facing surfaces (field outside ideal plates is zero). Plates are idealised as wide compared with the gap.
  (b) Uniformity: each infinite sheet gives a field σ/2ε₀ independent of distance, because as distance grows the patch contributions weaken as 1/r² but their perpendicular components grow and more area contributes at a given angle; the two sheets add between the plates (σ/ε₀) and cancel outside. For finite plates this holds well when the plates are much wider than the gap.
  (c) E = V/d: E = −dV/dx; in a uniform field V changes linearly across the gap, so E = V/d. The "voltmeter probe" is idealised (a real probe distorts the field; a flame probe or a resistive-paper model is used in practice). The video does not derive E = V/d from W = qV (v09, v10).
  (d) Area: at fixed V and d, Q = CV with C = ε₀A/d, so Q ∝ A and σ = Q/A is unchanged, so E = σ/ε₀ is unchanged. Capacitance is not named.
  (e) Units: V m⁻¹ = (J C⁻¹) m⁻¹ = (N m C⁻¹) m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹.
  (f) Breakdown: dry air at sea level about 3 × 10⁶ V m⁻¹ (v03 author note f); for millimetre gaps the real breakdown field is somewhat higher (Paschen), so "about" and "starts to spark" are kept; 5.0 × 10³ ÷ 3.0 × 10⁶ = 1.67 × 10⁻³ m.
  (g) Semolina demonstration: castor oil (insulating) in a glass dish with copper strip electrodes, a few kV from an EHT supply, done by a teacher. The grains are polarised and align with E.
  (h) The ramp analogy: the push along a smooth ramp is mg sin θ, and sin θ grows with height over length; the analogy compares steepness only, not energy.

### 5. why the field is uniform
> So why doesn't this field fade? Put a positive test charge near the top plate. The charge right above pushes it straight down. Most of the plate's charge is off to the sides, so it pushes partly sideways, and the left and right pushes cancel. Move the test charge further down. Each push is weaker, because the charge is further away, but it points more nearly straight down. Those effects balance, so the total push stays the same. And the negative plate's pull adds to it, all the way **across**.

### 6. the edges

So the field is uniform in the middle, and that's the region we use for **calculations**.

### 7. what sets the strength: voltage and gap
> So what sets its strength? The voltage between the plates, and the gap. Voltage, or potential difference, is measured in volts. You met it with batteries and circuits, and later we'll see exactly what a volt measures. Imagine a voltmeter with one lead on the negative plate, and a probe we slide across the gap. ## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "Two parallel plates are 2.5 cm apart with a potential difference of 450 V between them. The top plate is positive. (a) Describe the electric field lines between the plates, including near their edges. (b) Calculate the electric field strength between the plates."
Model answer: (a) Between the plates the field lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced, running from the positive top plate down to the negative bottom plate and meeting the plates at right angles, showing a uniform field. (b) d = 2.5 cm = 0.025 m; E = V/d = 450 ÷ 0.025 = 1.8 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹, directed downwards (from the positive plate to the negative plate). Classic mistake (crossed): "E = 450 ÷ 2.5 = 180 V m⁻¹".

**Attempt 2 (scene 16; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "The potential difference across a pair of parallel plates is tripled and the separation of the plates is halved. (a) By what factor does the electric field strength between the plates change? (b) Explain why increasing the area of the plates, with the same potential difference and separation, does not change the field strength between them."
Model answer: (a) E = V/d; E is directly proportional to V and inversely proportional to d, so E′ = (3V)/(d/2) = 6V/d: the field is 6 times stronger. Area does not appear in E = V/d, so the field strength between the plates is the same (the uniform region is simply wider). Classic mistake (crossed): "1.5 times stronger (3 ÷ 2)".
