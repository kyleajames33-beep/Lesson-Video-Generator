### 1. hook (a current moved a compass)
> Last time, a current in a lead made a compass needle swing. A compass needle is a tiny magnet, so that current must make a magnetic field. To see what a field like that looks like, let's start with an ordinary bar **magnet**.

Visual: opens on v23's last frame (see "Opening frame"). The "Next" chip becomes the heading: "Magnetic fields and field lines"; the memory line fades. On "swing" the knife switch closes and the needle swings again, then the switch opens. On "tiny magnet" the compass enlarges; its needle's north half labels "N", its south half "S". On "bar magnet" board change: the bench fades and the painted bar magnet (`phys-y11-bar-magnet-v1.png`, ink "N" and "S" letters) slides in, centred.

### 2. compasses draw the field lines
> A magnetic field is the region around a magnet, or a current, where another magnet feels a force. Put small plotting compasses around the bar magnet. Each needle's north end turns to point along the field at that spot. Join the needles head to tail, and you've drawn a field line. Its arrow shows which way a compass's north end points. Outside the magnet, the lines leave the north pole and curve round into the **south**.

Visual: camera continue. On "magnetic field" chip "magnetic field: where another magnet feels a magnetic force". On "plotting compasses" eight painted compass bodies (`phys-y11-plotting-compass-v1.png`) slide into a ring round the magnet, each with a coded `CompassNeedle`; on "turns" each needle swings and settles (damped). On "head to tail" a plum dashed line draws through the needles from the north pole round to the south pole, with arrowheads. On "field line" chip "field line: the way a compass's north end would point". On "south" `FieldLines` draws six more lines, all leaving N and entering S. Grey tag (screen only): "near the magnet, its field is much stronger than the Earth's".

### 3. prediction: where do the filings crowd?
> Now sprinkle iron filings on a card over the magnet. Each filing lines up with the field, like a tiny compass. Where will the filings be packed most **closely**?

Visual: camera continue; the compasses fade; a coded white card lies over the magnet. On "sprinkle" `IronFilings` scatter at random. On "closely" chips "near the middle?", "at the ends?", "evenly everywhere?" and a 3-second countdown ring.

### 4. close lines, strong field
> At the ends, around the poles. That's where the field is strongest, so the filings crowd together there, and further away they spread out. A field line diagram shows this the same way. Lines drawn close together mean a strong field, and lines spread apart mean a weak one. So the arrows give the field's direction, and the spacing gives its **strength**.

Visual: camera continue. Chip "at the ends?" glows; the others take grey scribble crosses. On "crowd together" the filings rotate into the field and gather at the poles. On "field line diagram" `FieldLines` fades in over the filings; on "close together" two small grey circles highlight a crowded patch near a pole and a sparse patch far off, labelled "strong" and "weak". On "strength" two-row chip "arrows: direction"; "spacing: strength". A chip "further apart means stronger" fades in and takes a grey scribble cross (site true or false).

### 5. why lines never cross
> Notice that the lines never cross. If two of them did, a compass sitting at that point would have to point two ways at once. It can only point one way, so the field has one direction at each **point**.

Visual: camera continue. On "If two of them did" a ghost pair of plum lines crosses at a point; a compass there spins between the two directions. On "one way" the ghost crossing takes a grey scribble cross and fades; the compass settles along the real line. Chip "one direction at each point: lines never cross".

### 6. prediction: cut the magnet in half
> Here's a puzzle. Cut a bar magnet in half, right through the middle. What do you get: a north pole on its own and a south pole on its own, or something **else**?

Visual: board change: the magnet alone; a grey dashed cut line across its middle. On "Cut" the magnet splits into two halves which slide apart. Labels "?" on the new cut faces. On "else" chips "a lone N and a lone S?", "two smaller magnets?" and a 3-second countdown ring.

### 7. closed loops
> You get two smaller magnets, each with its own north and south pole. However many times you cut it, the poles come in pairs. No one has ever found a north pole on its own. So a magnetic field line has nowhere to start or stop. Outside the magnet it runs from north to south, then it carries on inside, from south back to north, and closes into a loop. That's what continuous means here. Electric field lines are different. They start on positive charges and end on negative **ones**.

Visual: camera continue. On "two smaller magnets" the cut faces label "S" and "N"; each half grows its own small `FieldLines` pattern. On "pairs" the halves cut again into quarters, each labelled N and S. On "nowhere to start or stop" the halves rejoin into the whole magnet; one field line draws outside from N round to S, and on "carries on inside" its inside segment draws through the magnet from S to N (darker plum dashed), closing the loop; all lines close the same way. Chip "continuous: closed loops, no start and no end". On "Electric field lines" a small inset of v03's teal lines from a red "+" to a navy "−" (tag "from the electric fields video"). A chip "magnetic lines start at N and stop at S" takes a grey scribble cross (site misconception 1).

### 8. a current's field: circles
> Now the field of a current. Run a straight wire vertically through a card, and switch on a large current. Compasses placed around the wire don't point at it or away from it. They line up in a circle around it. Sprinkle filings, and they form rings. The field lines are circles centred on the wire, in the flat card at right angles to **it**.

Visual: board change: the painted retort stand (`phys-y11-retort-stand-v1.png`) holds a coded vertical wire through a coded horizontal card in perspective; leads to the painted bench supply (`phys-y11-low-voltage-supply-v1.png`); a bold ink "I" arrow up the wire. Grey tag "a large current, switched on briefly". On "Compasses" six compass bodies on the card, needles pointing north; on "switch on" the needles swing to form a circle round the wire. On "filings" `IronFilings` on the card form rings. On "circles centred on the wire" plum dashed circles draw on the card with arrowheads (gloss "concentric: sharing one centre"). A chip "the field points along the wire" fades in and takes a grey scribble cross (site misconception 2).

### 9. strongest near the wire, and it reverses
> Look at the spacing. The circles are close together near the wire and spread out further away, so the field is strongest near the wire and weakens with distance. And if you reverse the current, every compass turns right **around**.

Visual: camera continue; the view tilts to look straight down on the card. On "close together" the inner circles pulse; on "spread out" the outer ones; small grey tags "strong" and "weak". On "reverse the current" the "I" arrow flips to point down; every needle swings through 180° and the circles' arrowheads flip. Chip "reverse I, reverse the field".

### 10. dots and crosses
> To draw the wire end on, we need a way to show current coming out of the page or going into it. A dot is the tip of an arrow coming towards you: current out of the page. A cross is the tail feathers of an arrow flying away: current into the **page**.

Visual: board change: a blank board. On "tip of an arrow" a coded arrow flies towards the viewer, growing, and shrinks to its tip: an ink dot in a circle, labelled "out of the page". On "tail feathers" an arrow flies away, showing its cross-shaped tail, and shrinks to an ink cross in a circle, labelled "into the page". Both symbols stay side by side.

### 11. the right-hand grip rule

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 17; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A long vertical wire carries a conventional current downwards. **Attempt 2 (scene 19; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "A circular loop of wire lies in the plane of the page and carries a conventional current anticlockwise, as shown. (Diagram printed.)
Model answer: (a) Out of the page (curl the right fingers anticlockwise with the current; the thumb points out of the page). (b) The face towards the viewer, because the field lines come out of that face (and enter the far face, which acts as a south pole). Classic mistake (crossed): "the field at the centre is zero, because the two sides cancel".
