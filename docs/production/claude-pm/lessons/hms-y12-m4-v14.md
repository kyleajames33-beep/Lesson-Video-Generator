### 3. three windows, three jobs
> The dot point names three windows, and each has a different job. Before performance, the aim is to start with full glycogen, good hydration and a settled stomach. That means a carbohydrate-rich meal, moderate in protein and low in fat and fibre, about three to four hours before, plus a small snack nearer if needed. A big fatty meal too close to the start sits in the stomach, because fat and fibre slow its emptying. During performance, the aim is to delay running out. For events longer than about an hour, take carbohydrate that's easy to swallow, plus fluid. For short events, you usually need nothing, because the glycogen you start with lasts that long. After performance, replace glycogen, add protein for repair, and replace fluid and sodium. How urgent that is depends on the next effort. **Carbohydrate loading**, raising glycogen above normal over the days before, only suits long, continuous events, because a fuller store only helps if the event would otherwise reach the wall. And try nothing new on competition day.

### 4. fluid: two errors in opposite directions
> Sweating is how the body sheds heat, so losing fluid is the price of staying cool. In Year 11 you saw sweat shrink the plasma, so stroke volume falls and heart rate drifts up. What matters here is what that does to performance. Core temperature climbs faster, effort feels harder, and decisions and skill get worse too. That's **dehydration**. But the opposite error is more dangerous. Drink far more than you lose, and blood sodium is diluted. That's **hyponatraemia**, most often seen in slower participants in long events who drink at every station. In severe cases it can be life-threatening. So the goal isn't to drink as much as possible. It's to replace roughly what you lose.

### 5. estimating a sweat rate
> So how much is that? Sweat rates vary a lot between people, so measure them. Weigh the athlete before a session, dry. Record everything they drink. Weigh them again straight after, towelled dry. Sweat loss is the weight lost plus the fluid drunk. Say an athlete goes from seventy kilograms to sixty-eight point eight in an hour and a half, drinking point eight of a litre. That's one point two plus point eight, two litres of sweat, or about one point three litres an hour. They're still one point two kilograms down, and some of what they drink is lost as urine, so aim to drink about one and a half times that, around one point eight litres, with some sodium. And repeat it in different conditions, because heat raises sweat rate. The physiology is general, but the numbers are individual.

### 6. different sports: duration, intensity, opportunity
> Different sports don't need a memorised menu. They need three questions: how long, how hard, and when can the athlete refuel? The open-water swimmer goes five hours or more, moderately hard, and can only feed at boat stops. So carbohydrate loading makes sense, carbohydrate and fluid at every feed, rehearsed in training, and sodium matters. The netballer plays under an hour, hard and stop-start, with only breaks. So a normal well-fuelled diet, a light carbohydrate meal beforehand, fluid at the breaks, and little else for one match. And in a multi-day tournament, the recovery meal is also tomorrow's pre-game meal, so the windows merge. That answers the hook: it's mostly about when. And "using research" means judging claims. Ask who made it and whether they profit, who was studied, what was actually measured, and how big the effect was.

### 9. decision rule and handoff
