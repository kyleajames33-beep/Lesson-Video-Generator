> Here's the road trip, Sydney to Melbourne, eight hundred and eighty kilometres. The car drives, stops for a break, then drives on at a different speed. So what does a distance-time graph tell us about a **journey**?

### 3. gradient is speed
> The steepness of a straight section tells you the speed. Pick two points on it, and divide the distance gained by the time taken. That's rise over run, which you met as gradient in Year eleven. Here the rise is four hundred kilometres, and the run is four hours, so the gradient is a hundred kilometres per **hour**, the car's speed. A line that gains more distance each hour is steeper, so steeper means **faster**.

### 6. coming back: a line going down
> Not every trip only goes away. A delivery motorbike rides six kilometres from a shop to a customer, in fifteen minutes. It stops for five minutes, then rides the six kilometres back, in twenty minutes. On the way out, the distance from the shop goes up. On the way back, it goes **down**, all the way to zero, at the shop. So a line sloping down means travelling back towards the start. For the speed, use the distance covered. Six kilometres in twenty minutes, a third of an hour, is eighteen kilometres per **hour**. On the way out, it covered the same six kilometres in a quarter of an hour, twenty-four kilometres per hour, so that part of the line is **steeper**.

## Memory aid check

- **"Up is away, flat is stopped, down is back."** with key line **"Steeper is faster. Speed = rise ÷ run, in km and hours."** Original. Meaning, as said in scene 7: on a graph of distance from the start against time, a rising section means the distance from the start is growing, a flat section means it isn't changing (stopped), and a falling section means it is shrinking (coming back); the steepness of a straight section is the speed, found as distance gained ÷ time taken.
