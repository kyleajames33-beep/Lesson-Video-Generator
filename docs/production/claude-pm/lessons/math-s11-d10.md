### 1. hook: is this poster fair?
> Our survey has become a poster, an infographic. That's a display that combines graphs, numbers, icons and short text to tell a story from data quickly. Thirty-five per cent get eight or more hours. A sector graph of where phones charge. A row of twenty phones, twelve lit. And a column graph, where the bedroom group's sleep looks tiny. Infographics are everywhere, from news sites to council letters, because they're quick to read. That's also what makes them easy to get wrong. So how can a graph or infographic inform us, or mislead **us**?

### 2. analysing the infographic
> Start with what the poster gets right, because a good analysis justifies the choices as well as finding faults. Justifying means saying why each graph suits its data, not just naming it. The big number shows one key fact, the share of students getting eight hours or more, seven of twenty. One number deserves one big, clear tile. The sector graph shows where phones charge. Those four places make one whole, a hundred per cent, and a sector graph is built for parts of one whole. The phone row uses one icon per student, so twelve of twenty is visible at a glance. And a column graph is the right choice to compare two groups, phone in the bedroom against phone elsewhere. Each graph was chosen for its **data**.

### 3. the truncated axis
> Now the column graph's problem. Its vertical axis starts at six hours, not at nought. The bedroom group averaged six point six hours, so its column rises just nought point six above the base. The other group averaged eight point one, two point one above the base. On the page, one column is three and a half times as tall as the other. Slide the axis back to nought, and the truth appears. Six point six against eight point one. The elsewhere group's average is about a quarter more, an extra one and a half **hours**. That's a real difference, worth reporting. But the cut-off axis made it look like the bedroom group sleeps less than a third as much, which is **false**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 1): the column graph enlarges (about 1,000 px tall area), teal columns "phone in bedroom 6.6 h" and "elsewhere 8.1 h", axis from 6 (hand tag "starts at 6!"). On "false" a red slip "the bedroom group sleeps under a third as much" writes and is crossed (caption "the axis starts at 6").

### 4. other ways graphs mislead
> The truncated axis is the most common trick, but there are others. Pictures can lie with area. To show twice as many phones, someone draws a phone twice as tall. But to keep its shape, it's twice as wide too, so it covers four times the space, and the eye sees four times as much. Three D sector graphs tilt the circle, so the slices at the front look bigger than they are. Scales with uneven steps squash some changes and stretch others. A line graph can start and stop at handy dates, and hide the rest. And a percentage needs its sample size. Thirty-five per cent sounds solid, until you ask, of how many? Here, it's seven students out of **twenty**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 2): a square grid (about 900 px) with one phone icon covering 1 square; on "twice as tall" it stretches upward (2 squares); on "twice as wide too" it widens to cover a 2 × 2 block; a counter "area: 1 → 4" (hand tag "area × 4"). On "Three D sector graphs" a tilted 3D sector graph of the phone shares: the front slice (10%) looks nearly as big as the 25% slice behind it (Revision 2, D10-3D: claim softened so the build can show it honestly at playback speed); it flattens to 2D and the true sizes return (chip "3D distorts"). On "uneven steps" an axis with ticks 0, 5, 10, 50, 100 flashes amber. On "handy dates" a battery-style line graph shows only its falling part, then the full day appears (grey chip "d08: the battery's day"). On "of how many?" the poster's "35%" tile flips to show "7 of 20" (hand tag "of how many?"). On "twenty" (bold) chip "always give n".

### 5. what a graph can't tell you
> Even an honest graph has limits, and conclusions and predictions have to respect them. Our graphs describe twenty Year 11 students, at one school, on one night. They can't tell us about all teenagers, or about every night. Our data came from an observational study, so they can't show that phones cause less sleep. An average hides the spread. Behind six point six hours is one student who slept just three. And no graph can safely predict outside its data. It can't tell us how next year's Year 11 will sleep. So here's a fair conclusion. In our sample of twenty Year 11 students on one school night, those whose phones charged outside the bedroom slept about one and a half hours more on average. That doesn't show the phone caused it, or that all teenagers are the **same**.

Visual: stage change: a limitation checklist at the right, 40 px, each line lighting as said: "only these 20 students, one school", "one night", "observation: not cause" (grey chip "d04: just watching"), "averages hide spread", "no prediction beyond the data"; the poster small at the left with a lens moving to the matching tile for each line. On "one student who slept just three" a mini dot plot under the bedroom column shows the dots behind 6.6, the 3 ringed (hand tag "averages hide spread"; grey chip "spread: d13"). On "So here's a fair conclusion" the conclusion writes, 40 px, across the lower half; on "same" (bold) it frames green (hand tag "tended to").

### 6. fixing the poster
> Let's fix the poster. Start the column graph's axis at nought. Print the sample, twenty Year 11 students, one school night, so every percentage has its count. Add one line saying it's an observational study, and doesn't show cause. Keep the good choices, the big number, the sector graph and the phone row. Now the poster informs, and doesn't **mislead**. A good poster is quick to read, and it still tells the truth.

Visual: stage change (simulation 3): the poster at full size on the board; the toggle "before | after" flips: the column graph's axis drops to 0 (flash); a strip "n = 20 Year 11 students, one school night" writes under the title (flash); a footnote "An observational study: it doesn't show cause."
