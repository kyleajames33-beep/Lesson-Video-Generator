### 2. the two players (lattice and polar water)
> An ionic solid like table salt is a **lattice**: a regular stack of positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions, where each ion is held by the pull of all the opposite charges around it. Water molecules are **polar**: the oxygen end is slightly negative, and the two hydrogen ends are slightly positive. You met both of these in Year 11.

### 3. water pulls the ions out (hydration)
> Water molecules crowd around the crystal and **turn to face the ions**: their oxygen ends point at the positive sodium ions, and their hydrogen ends point at the negative chloride ions. This pull between an ion and the oppositely charged end of a water molecule is called an **ion-dipole attraction**. An ion on a **corner** of the crystal has the fewest neighbours holding it, so it's the easiest to pull away. Once it's out, water molecules **surround it completely**. An ion wrapped in water like this is **hydrated**, and that's what aqueous means.

### 4. the equation (the ions were already there)

Dissolving just **separates** them, which is the dissociation you met last video, and wraps each one in water. So there are **no salt molecules** floating around.

### 5. two energy steps (the ledger)
> Dissolving also involves energy, and you can count it in two steps. Step one: pulling the ions **away from each other** costs energy, because opposite charges attract. Energy goes **in**. Step two: when water molecules **cling to** the separated ions, energy is **released**. Energy comes **out**. In a real crystal both happen together, but splitting them lets us count the energy. The overall heat change when one mole dissolves is the **enthalpy of solution**, and it's just the two steps added together. If more energy comes out than goes in, the extra **heats the water**. If more goes in than comes out, that energy is taken **from the water**, and it gets colder.

### 7. if it costs energy, why does it dissolve?
> Here's a puzzle. If ammonium nitrate takes in energy to dissolve, why does it dissolve at all? Remember from the enthalpy and entropy video: a change is favoured by **releasing heat**, and also by **spreading out**. Ions locked in a lattice spread out through the water when they dissolve, a big **increase in entropy**. For ammonium nitrate, the spreading out **wins** over the energy cost. So you can't predict whether a salt dissolves from its energy change alone.

### 8b. two rates meet (dynamic equilibrium)
> Zoom in. Ions keep **leaving** the crystal at a steady rate. But dissolved ions also **bump into** the crystal and stick to it. That's crystallising. At first there are few dissolved ions, so crystallising is slow. As more ions dissolve, they hit the crystal more often, so crystallising **speeds up**, until the two rates are **equal**. From then on ions still leave and rejoin, but the amount dissolved stays the same. That's a dynamic equilibrium, so it gets the **two-way arrow**. And its K, with the solid left out, is the **Ksp** from last video.

The amount dissolved doesn't change, yet radioactivity soon turns up **in the solution**. So ions must keep leaving the solid while others keep **rejoining** it.

### 10. the added-solid trap
> One trap. What if you tip **more salt** into a saturated solution? The solution doesn't change. The extra solid just joins the pile, because the water already holds as much as it can at that temperature. In K terms, the solid is **left out** of the expression, so adding more doesn't change Q. Q still **equals K**, so there's no shift. Temperature is different, because it changes K itself. Potassium nitrate dissolves endothermically, so **warming** its saturated solution raises K, and more dissolves.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Dissolving an ionic solid: polar water molecules turn their oppositely charged ends to the ions (ion-dipole attraction), pull ions off the lattice and surround them (hydrated ions). NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq). The ions already existed; dissolving separates them.
2. Two energy steps: separating the ions from the lattice takes in energy (+); hydrating the ions releases energy (-). ΔH(solution) = the sum. Hydration bigger: exothermic, the water warms (CaCl2, -81 kJ/mol). Separation bigger: endothermic, the water cools (NH4NO3, +25.7 kJ/mol).
3. An endothermic salt can still dissolve because spreading out (an increase in entropy) also favours dissolving. Energy alone does not predict solubility.
4. Saturated solution with solid present: dynamic equilibrium, NaCl(s) ⇌ Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq). Dissolving rate = crystallising rate, so the concentration and the mass of solid stay constant. Evidence: radioactive ions added in a crystal spread into the solution.
5. Changes: adding solid, no shift (solid left out of K). Adding water, more dissolves until saturated again (same concentration). Temperature changes K: for endothermic dissolving, heating dissolves more.

Memory aids (large, on the notes screen): *Lattice costs, hydration pays.* *Saturated means equal rates, not stopped.* Condition tag: "same temperature; solid still present."

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why ions come out: the oppositely charged ends of polar water attract them, and exposed corner ions are held by fewer neighbours (3). Why no salt molecules: the ions already existed (4). Why the water warms or cools: the sign of step 1 plus step 2 (5, 6a, 6b), with the CaCl2 cause named (small, doubly charged ion hydrates strongly). Why an endothermic salt dissolves: spreading out (7). Why dissolving appears to stop: the crystallising rate rises with dissolved ion concentration until it equals the steady dissolving rate (8b). Why added solid does nothing: the solid is not in K, so Q is unchanged (10).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** dissolving always takes in energy (6a); NaCl molecules in water (4); predicting solubility from energy alone (7); saturated means stopped (8b, 9, aid); equilibrium as equal amounts (8b says equal rates; NESA 2023 Q33); adding solid shifts the equilibrium (10); "already saturated, so heating does nothing" (10); hydration absorbs energy (13); "adding water leaves it more dilute" while solid remains (15).
