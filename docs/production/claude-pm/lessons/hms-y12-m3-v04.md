### 2. why compare with the OECD
> Whether a country's health is good is always a comparison. Compare Australia with a poor country, and you mostly learn about wealth. So we compare with the **OECD**, a group of mostly high-income countries that collect health data on agreed definitions. That holds wealth roughly steady, so the differences left point at choices: how the system is designed, what's spent on prevention, what's regulated. The most useful indicator is **avoidable mortality**: deaths that prevention or timely care could have stopped, because it shows what a system can influence. And three cautions. Rankings jump around, because many countries sit very close together. A national average can hide big gaps. And countries count differently. Register very premature births differently, and infant mortality changes with no change in babies' health. A difference in the data can be a difference in the counting.

### 3. where Australia sits: two columns

It does better than average on avoidable deaths. Its daily smoking rate is among the lowest, and its cancer survival among the better. But it's average or weaker on others. Overweight and obesity are above average, and have risen for decades. Alcohol use sits around average or above. ### 4. why: three families, matched to the indicator

### 5. why others rank higher or lower
> And why do some countries rank above or below us? Four variables explain most of the spread. One, whether coverage is universal. Where many people face cost as a barrier, avoidable deaths stay high, however advanced the hospitals. That answers the puzzle: the spending is real, but it isn't reaching everyone. Other answers could be an older population, or different counting. Two, how much goes to primary care and prevention, which act earlier in a disease. Three, income inequality and social spending, because health is made largely outside the health system. Four, long-standing patterns of diet, smoking and drinking, and the policies that shape them. And one idea explains many paradoxes: today's deaths reflect yesterday's exposures. A country that cut smoking thirty years ago is collecting the benefit now.

### 6. what can we learn: the transfer test
> The last question is what we can learn and apply here. That needs a **transfer test**. Don't ask what another country does. Ask what makes it work, and whether that thing exists here. Step one, name the mechanism. Step two, check it against Australia: how spread out we are, our tax base, where our health workers live, and what's already covered. If the mechanism exists here, the policy transfers, with adaptations. If it doesn't, only the principle transfers, and you find a local way to carry it. So what passes? Sustained regulation, which worked for tobacco, applied to food marketing. Shifting spending toward prevention and primary care. And reporting by group, not just nationally. What fails? Anything that relies on dense cities, or on health workers spread evenly.
