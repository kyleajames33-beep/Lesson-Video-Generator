### 2. two halves, and pain as information
> This dot point has two halves. The first is drugs in injury management: painkillers, **anti-inflammatories**, which reduce swelling and pain, local anaesthetic injections that numb an area, and cortisone injections. They're mostly legal, often prescribed, and used at every level of sport. The second is drugs to improve performance, which are banned. The first affects far more athletes. Its central risk is **masking**: removing the pain signal without removing the damage. Pain is information. It tells you a tissue isn't coping with the load. Switch it off and keep playing, and a minor injury can become a serious one, with no banned substance at all. Pain relief still has a place, in sleep and rehab. The question is whether a drug is supporting recovery, or letting them ignore the injury.

### 3. health implications, by category
> The performance substances are best learned by category, each with a mechanism. **Anabolic agents** build muscle and speed recovery, but raise blood pressure, upset blood fats, damage the liver and disrupt hormones, sometimes permanently. **Stimulants** raise alertness and hide fatigue, but push heart rate and blood pressure up, can trigger abnormal heart rhythms, and impair cooling, so heat illness becomes a real risk mid-game. **Blood doping** and hormones that boost red blood cells raise oxygen carrying, as in v-eight, but thicken the blood, raising the risk of clots and stroke, worse when dehydrated. **Beta blockers** steady the heart and hands in precision sports like archery, but can drop heart rate and blood pressure too far. Notice that many of these risks are worst during exercise. And for teenagers, still growing, hormonal agents carry extra risks.

### 4. ethics: fairness, safety, and coercion
> The syllabus asks about ethics, so make each argument properly, including the reply. Fairness: sport compares athletes under shared conditions, and a hidden advantage changes what the result means. The reply is that sport is already unequal, in money, coaching and technology, as in v-nineteen. The answer: the problem isn't inequality as such, but a concealed and harmful advantage. And the strongest argument: **coercion**. If doping works and goes undetected, every other athlete faces a choice: dope too, or lose. So "it's my body, my choice" misses the point, because the choice doesn't stay with one person. Back to the hook. Only the stimulant is unfair to rivals. But both override a warning signal, and the painkiller may well cause more harm.

### 5. testing, and what it can't do
> Drug testing has several working parts. A **prohibited list**, updated every year, with some substances banned at all times and others only in competition. Testing out of competition, without notice, because many drugs are used in training and cleared before the event. Each sample is split into A and B, so one lab error can't end a career. **Strict liability**, from v-sixteen: the athlete is responsible for whatever is in their sample, however it got there, because intent can't be proven. So "I didn't know it was in my supplement" isn't a defence. But testing has limits. A test only finds what it's designed to find, so new substances go undetected. Small, repeated doses can stay under detection limits. Testing is expensive, so coverage below the elite level is thin. And so a negative test doesn't prove an athlete is clean.

### 6. a discuss answer, and where you stand
> So a discuss answer does four things. Cover both halves. Give mechanisms, not just claims. State the strongest counter-argument and answer it. Then judge. Doping should be banned and tested for, because it causes real harm, often worst during exercise, it changes what results mean, and it coerces others. Testing is necessary but not enough, so education and culture matter too. And for most athletes, the injury half matters more, because legal pain relief that lets damaged tissue keep working is common at every level. For a school-age athlete, two things follow. Strict liability applies to you too, so anything you take is your responsibility. And pain is a message to listen to. If you're unsure about any medicine or supplement, ask a doctor or a sports dietitian.
