### 10. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Conditions for photosynthesis: light (energy source), carbon dioxide and water (raw materials), chlorophyll (absorbs light), a suitable temperature (enzymes run every step). Remove one, and photosynthesis stops or nearly stops.
- Limiting factor: the condition in shortest supply, which holds the rate back. Raising another condition does little until that one rises; sometimes two limit together.
- Rate against light intensity: rises while light limits, then plateaus when another condition (carbon dioxide or temperature) limits. More carbon dioxide raises the plateau (greenhouses enrich carbon dioxide).
- Temperature: rate rises to an optimum (about 25 to 35 °C for many plants), then falls, because high heat can change enzymes' active sites and disrupt other parts of photosynthesis. More heat can lower the rate.
- The limiting factor changes with conditions. One example: dawn, light; warm bright afternoon, perhaps carbon dioxide; cold bright morning, perhaps temperature.

Memory: a barrel only fills to its shortest stave.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (2 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Two-plant test (scenes 1, 2, 5).** In bright light, photosynthesis in most C3 plants is limited by carbon dioxide at normal air concentrations, so extra carbon dioxide increases the rate more than extra light. Correct (the site's own Before You Read result).

White patches of variegated leaves lack chlorophyll and do not photosynthesise. Near freezing, enzyme rates are very low ("slows to a crawl"). Correct.
- **Limiting factor (scene 3).** Blackman's principle of limiting factors (1905): the rate is limited by the factor in shortest supply. Liebig's barrel illustrates the law of the minimum (originally for nutrients). Simplification: in reality two factors can co-limit near the bend of the curve; author note only. Correct at this level.
- **Light curve (scene 4).** Rate rises approximately linearly at low light, then levels to a plateau set by carbon dioxide, temperature or the enzymes' capacity. Correct.
- **Carbon dioxide raises the plateau (scene 5).** Higher carbon dioxide does not change the initial light-limited slope much and raises the light-saturated plateau. (Strictly, raising carbon dioxide also slightly raises the initial slope in C3 plants by reducing photorespiration; ignored at this level, author note.) Greenhouses commonly enrich to about 800 to 1,200 ppm against about 420 ppm outside, "about two or three times" (the site's figure). Correct.
