## Decisions

- **Learner question (placeholder rewritten; open decision 3).** Route placeholder: "How can I analysing data and information in a defensible physics investigation?" Rewritten as: "How do repeated measurements become a result you can trust, and how sure can you be about it?" The answer, built in order: one bad reading can drag a mean a long way, so an odd reading is checked for a cause before anything else (1, 2); the mean is the result, and the spread of good repeats says how sure we are, first as half the range, then as a standard deviation that uses every reading (3, 4); the answer is written with a sensible prefix and only the significant figures the data justify (5, 6); a tight set can still be wrong, because precision (spread) and accuracy (gap from the accepted value) are different, and a second day's run tests reliability while the method's physics decides validity (7, 8); a pattern is described in words and in numbers, and the trend predicts safely inside the measured range only (9, 10); the conclusion states the result with its uncertainty and names improvements and a further test (11).
- **The hook (a surprise from one bad reading).** Five drops from 1.000 m: 0.451, 0.448, 0.497, 0.453, 0.456 s. The mean of all five is 0.461 s and g = 2s ÷ t² = 9.41 m s⁻²; without the odd reading the result is close to 9.80. 3 s prediction "So which value should you use?" with chips "average all five", "delete the odd one", "check the odd one first". Answered in scene 2 (check first).
- **Outliers and inconsistencies (`ci8856aeb0`, `ci7afa7b23`).** Plain meaning (as v10): "outlier: a reading that doesn't fit the pattern of the others". Rule taught: look for a cause, repeat the measurement, and report an unexplained odd reading rather than quietly deleting it. Cause shown on a coded lab-note card: "drop 3: ball clipped the trapdoor edge" (the same physical cause v10 tagged; a clipped edge opens the switch late, so the time is too long). Repeat: 0.452 s, which fits. The second inconsistency (the uncorrected set sitting 6 ms high in scene 7) is explained by its cause, the release delay (v10).
- **Uncertainty first, then standard deviation (`ci914a5e7e`).** Mean and range recalled from v10 with their plain meanings. Half the range, ±0.004 s, is "a quick first estimate of the uncertainty: how far the true value could reasonably be from our mean" (v15's wording, so v15's scatter bars need no new idea; root decision 5). Standard deviation: "the typical distance of a reading from the mean". Steps shown one at a time, in milliseconds so the arithmetic is light: deviations −1, −4, 0, +1, +4 ms; squares 1, 16, 0, 1, 16; sum 34; divide by n − 1 = 4 to get 8.5; square root 2.9 ms. Reasons said: squaring stops negatives cancelling; dividing by one less than the number of readings is the standard rule for a sample (grey tag "calculator: use sₓ or σₙ₋₁, the sample standard deviation"; open decision 4). Why it beats the range: it uses every reading, not just the two extremes. Percentage error is recalled, not re-taught (v10), in scene 11.
- **Prefixes (`cif8bde07b`).** Taught where they are needed: 0.0029 s is easier to read as 2.9 ms. The full syllabus ladder from femto to peta, each step a factor of 10³, with kilo the one lower-case prefix above the base unit (memory note on screen only: "capitals from mega up"). Conversion rule: swap the prefix for its power of ten. Worked example: 530 nm (green light) = 530 × 10⁻⁹ m = 5.30 × 10⁻⁷ m (FA2 meets wavelengths; the value is typical and tagged "about"). Centi is not on the syllabus list; fa0-v04 uses it and stays the home for unit conversions.
- **Significant figures (`ci5a02ec2a`).** g = 2s ÷ t² from s = ½gt² (v09, v10). Calculator: 9.789 33... Rules shown: significant figures are the digits that carry real information; leading zeros don't count (0.452 has three; 0.0029 has two); zeros after the decimal point at the end do (1.000 m has four); in multiplying and dividing, the answer keeps the fewest significant figures of the data used; keep every digit until the end, then round. Result 9.79 m s⁻². The uncertainty sets the last digit of a mean (±0.004 s, so 0.452, not 0.45200), said on screen only.
- **Precision, accuracy, reliability, validity (`ci15366d75`).** Definitions chosen so the four don't blur (open decision 5): **precise** "repeated readings agree closely (small spread, small standard deviation)"; **accurate** "the result is close to the accepted value" (v10); **reliable** "repeating the whole experiment gives consistent results"; **valid** "the method measures what it claims to" (v15 and fa0-v02's wording). v10 defined reliability as "how closely repeated results agree" using whole repeat runs of the method, which matches "repeating the whole experiment" here; precision is new and is the spread within one set. Two separate illustrative sets on one number line: set A (a different run on a rig whose delay was not subtracted) 0.457 to 0.459 s, standard deviation 0.7 ms, mean 0.458 s; set B (our run, delay subtracted) mean 0.452 s, standard deviation 2.9 ms; the time the accepted g predicts, 0.452 s (√(2 × 1.000 ÷ 9.80) = 0.4518), as a gold mark. A is precise but 6 ms off, far more than its spread: a systematic error (v10), which repeats cannot remove. Reliability: a second day's run, mean 0.453 s, inside 0.452 ± 0.004 s. Validity: a steel ball over 1 m barely feels the air (v10 author note d), so the fall measures g; a table tennis ball would not (grey tag only).
- **Prediction 2 (3 s, scene 7).** "Which set is more accurate?" chips "set A (tight)", "set B (ours)". Answered in scene 8. Revision 2: A and B are two separate illustrative repeat sets (A biased and precise, B less precise and closer to the accepted time), not one set before and after a correction, because subtracting a fixed delay cannot change the spread.
- **Relationships (`ci7f1f6fbd`) and trends.** v10 run 1 (heights 0.200 to 1.000 m; times 0.207 to 0.458 s; tagged "from the falling-ball practical (illustrative data)"). Qualitative: "as the height increases, the fall time increases, but each extra 20 cm adds less time" (increments 84, 64, 54, 49 ms shown as small brackets). Quantitative: "height is directly proportional to time squared" (plain meaning: "doubling one doubles the other; a straight line through the origin"); v10's best-fit gradient 4.806, said "about four point eight", so h ≈ 4.8t² (the fitted line's intercept, −0.006 m, is shown as "close to the origin"; author note e). Linear but not proportional is named in one grey tag ("straight, but not through the origin: linear"). Inverse proportion is left to v15 (a ∝ 1/m).
- **Predictions from data (`ci8856aeb0`).** Interpolation (plain meaning "reading a value from the trend inside the measured range"): h = 0.500 m gives t ≈ 0.32 s from the line. Extrapolation ("predicting outside the measured range, which assumes the pattern carries on"): a 50 m drop, where even a steel ball feels the air noticeably, so the real time would be longer than the line predicts (author note f). "Trust interpolation; test an extrapolation."
- **Conclusion, improvements, further investigation (`ci8856aeb0`, `ci7e38e278`).** Conclusion: the mean time 0.452 ± 0.004 s matches the 0.452 s the accepted g predicts, and gives g = 9.79 m s⁻², 0.1% from 9.80 (percentage error recalled from v10). Improvements matched to evidence: more repeats reduce the effect of random error on the mean; centring the ball over the trapdoor removes the clipped-edge cause of the outlier. Further investigation: lighter balls or longer drops, to find where the model fails (ties to validity and extrapolation).
