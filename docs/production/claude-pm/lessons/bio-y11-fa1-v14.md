### 2. question one: can it cross the oily core?
> Question one: can it slip through the oily middle of the membrane? That depends on size, polarity and charge. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are small and non-polar, so they mix easily into the oily core and pass straight through. That's simple diffusion. Glucose is bigger, and it's polar, with slightly charged parts that cling to water. Salts split into ions, which carry a full charge and hold a shell of water around them. The oily core pushes both of them back, so a bare bilayer lets them cross only very slowly, and real cells use a **protein** to move them much faster.

### 4. does oxygen always move in?
> So does oxygen always move into cells? No. Take a leaf cell in bright sunlight. Photosynthesis makes oxygen faster than respiration uses it, so oxygen builds up inside and diffuses **out**, while carbon dioxide, which photosynthesis uses up, diffuses **in**. Same gases, same membrane, opposite directions. The substance doesn't choose the direction. The cell's activity does.

### 5. steeper gradients, faster diffusion
> The steeper the gradient, the faster the diffusion. That's why exchange surfaces keep their gradients steep. In the lungs, each air sac keeps getting fresh air, high in oxygen, while the blood arriving is low in oxygen. So oxygen diffuses into the blood, carbon dioxide diffuses out, and flowing blood carries each load away, keeping both gradients **steep**.

### 6. glucose: one molecule, two processes
> Now glucose. Real cells move it through carrier proteins, so the gradient decides whether that costs energy. In a respiring cell, glucose is used up, so it's lower inside, and it enters by facilitated diffusion, for free. But the cells lining a kidney tubule have to collect nearly all the glucose from the fluid, even when there's less in the fluid than in the cell. There, glucose is taken in by active transport, which uses energy from **ATP**. Same molecule, two processes. The direction decides.

### 7. salts: channels and pumps
> Salts follow the same logic. Ions are charged, so they need a protein. When an ion moves down its gradient, it passes through a channel protein, by facilitated diffusion. Sodium does this, leaking slowly into animal cells. When the cell needs an ion moved against its gradient, a pump does it by active transport, like the sodium-potassium pump throwing that sodium back out. One ion can use both routes. Ions also respond to electrical conditions across the membrane, but in these examples the direction still decides the **energy**.

### 8. wastes: free to leave
> Finally, wastes. Carbon dioxide is made in every respiring cell, and urea, a waste that carries nitrogen, is made in liver cells. Each is more concentrated inside the cell that makes it, so each diffuses out, down its gradient. Carbon dioxide is small and non-polar, so it crosses the oily core easily. Urea is small but polar, so it slips through the bilayer slowly, and many cells have channel proteins that let it through faster. Either way, it costs nothing. Making the waste builds the very gradient that carries it **away**.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Two questions place any material: (1) can it cross the oily core, given its size, polarity and charge? (2) which way does its concentration gradient run (for ions, with electrical conditions across the membrane also counting)?
2. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are small and non-polar, so they cross the bilayer by simple diffusion. Glucose (polar) and ions (charged) cross a bare bilayer only very slowly; real cells use proteins to move them much faster.
3. Down the gradient through a channel or carrier: facilitated diffusion, no ATP. Against the gradient through a carrier: active transport, ATP (for example glucose in kidney tubule cells, sodium out of animal cells).

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Molecule sizes for the icons (scene 2, 10b).** Relative masses: oxygen 32, carbon dioxide 44, urea 60, glucose 180 (g/mol). A sodium ion (radius about 0.1 nm) is smaller than glucose (about 0.9 nm across) even with its water shell (about 0.36 nm). Icons drawn roughly to these proportions; "A sodium ion is tiny" is spoken.
- **Artificial bilayer permeability (A1).** Standard textbook permeability coefficients for synthetic lipid bilayers (for example Alberts, *Molecular Biology of the Cell*): oxygen and carbon dioxide high; urea about 10⁻⁶ cm/s; glucose about 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹⁰ cm/s; Na⁺ about 10⁻¹² cm/s. The card's ranking (very fast, slow, very slow, almost none) follows this order. Codex to confirm against a standard source.
- **Leaf cell in bright light (scene 4).** In bright light the photosynthesis rate exceeds the respiration rate in mesophyll cells, so net oxygen leaves and net carbon dioxide enters. Correct.
- **Alveolus (scene 5).** Alveolar air is high in oxygen and low in carbon dioxide relative to the deoxygenated blood arriving in pulmonary capillaries; blood flow and ventilation maintain the gradients. Correct at setting level.
- **Kidney glucose (scene 6).** Proximal tubule cells reabsorb nearly all filtered glucose, continuing when the filtrate concentration has fallen below the cell's, by sodium-glucose cotransport (secondary active transport; author note in Decisions). Spoken as "active transport, which uses energy from ATP".
- **Muscle glucose (scene 6).** Skeletal muscle takes glucose in by facilitated diffusion through GLUT4 carriers (the teal carriers painted in the existing plate). Correct; the carriers are not named.
- **Sodium leak (scene 7).** Sodium leaks into animal cells down its gradient through channels and is pumped out by the sodium-potassium pump (v12). Correct.
- **Urea (scene 8).** Urea is made mainly in liver cells (the urea cycle), is small and polar, crosses lipid bilayers slowly, and many cells (for example red blood cells and kidney cells) have urea transporter channels that speed its passive movement. Correct.
- **Yeast (A2).** Yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) takes up glucose by facilitated diffusion through hexose transporters; when glucose metabolism is blocked, internal glucose rises and net uptake falls. The stem's chemical is generic ("a chemical that stops the cells breaking down glucose"). Correct in principle; Codex to confirm the facilitated diffusion statement for yeast.
