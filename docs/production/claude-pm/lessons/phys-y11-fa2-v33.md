### 1. hook (more, less or the same?)
> Last time ended on a siren that sounds different as a car drives past, even though the siren never changes. It plays a steady five hundred hertz. As the car comes towards this microphone, will the microphone pick up more than five hundred hertz, less, or the **same**?

Visual: opens on v32's last frame (the road, the blue car with its beacon and "siren: 500 Hz", the roadside microphone marker and its panel, the wavefronts, the chips). The "Next" chip becomes the heading: "The Doppler effect"; the memory line fades. On "steady five hundred hertz" the beacon's readout pulses. On "comes towards this microphone" the car rolls back to its start and drives towards the marker. On "same" chips "more?", "less?", "the same?" and a 3-second countdown ring.

### 2. the siren doesn't change
> First, what doesn't change. A second microphone rides on the car's roof, and it reads five hundred hertz the whole time. So the siren itself doesn't change its note. And the sound travels through the air at the same speed, three hundred and forty metres per second, whichever way the car is going, because the air sets the speed. What changes is how the wave's crests are spaced when they reach the **microphone**.

Visual: on "second microphone" a small coded microphone marker drops onto the car's roof with its own readout "roof: 500 Hz", which holds steady as the car moves (grey tag "moves with the siren"). On "same speed" a chip "v_wave = 340 m s⁻¹ (set by the air)" and two equal amber-outlined travel arrows on fronts ahead and behind. On "spaced" the camera pushes to the fronts between the car and the roadside microphone.

### 3. bunched ahead, spread behind
> Each crest spreads out as a circle, called a wavefront, centred on where the siren was when it sent that crest out. But the car keeps moving between one wavefront and the next. So each new wavefront starts a little closer to the one in front, and the wavefronts crowd together ahead of the car and spread out behind it. They all travel at the same speed, so ahead, closer wavefronts reach the microphone more often each second. That's a higher frequency. Behind the car, they arrive less often, so the frequency is lower. This change in frequency is called the Doppler **effect**.

Visual: `DopplerFronts`, freeze-and-step (tag "exaggerated: car at 0.4 × the speed of sound"). On "spreads out as a circle" one front grows from a grey emission dot (gloss "wavefront: one crest spreading out"). On "keeps moving" the car steps forward one period's distance (blue velocity arrow) and a second front starts from the new dot; on "a little closer" the gap ahead between the two fronts brackets short, the gap behind long. Steps continue for six fronts: on "crowd together" the front side bunches, on "spread out behind" the back side opens. On "same speed" the travel arrows stay equal. On "more often each second" the roadside counter ticks faster than a ghost counter for a parked siren beside it ("parked: 500 per second"); on "higher frequency" the panel row "approaching:" shows an up-arrow "> 500 Hz"; the hook chip "more?" ticks, "less?" and "the same?" take scribble crosses. On "lower" a second roadside marker behind the car ticks slower ("< 500 Hz"). On "Doppler effect" gloss "Doppler effect: a change in the frequency received because the source or the observer is moving".

### 4. as the car passes

The note falls as the car goes by, fastest as it passes, then settles low as it heads away. It doesn't keep falling the whole time. It mostly drops while the car is going **past**.

Visual: camera pull back to the full road at real speed (the bunching now slight; tag off). On "put the microphone" the roadside marker highlights, set back from the road, and the car drives past it while a frequency trace draws under the road against time: on "nearly steady" a nearly flat high section (thin dashed lines from the car to the marker show it heading almost straight at it); on "falls" a smooth fall, steepest at the passing moment (a dashed vertical "passing"); on "settles low" a nearly flat low section. ### 5. prediction: a moving microphone

### 6. the moving observer
> Higher. This time the wavefronts are evenly spaced, because the siren is parked. But the microphone is running into them, so it meets more of them each second than it would standing still. More wavefronts per second means a higher frequency. Moving away from the siren, it meets fewer, so the frequency is lower. So there are two causes. A moving source changes the spacing of the wavefronts, and a moving observer changes how often it meets **them**.

Visual: on "Higher" the chip "higher?" ticks; "the same?" takes a scribble cross. On "evenly spaced" the navy brackets between fronts are all equal. On "running into them" the yellow car's microphone meets fronts and its counter ticks faster than a ghost counter for a parked microphone ("still: 500 per second"; readout "> 500 Hz"). On "Moving away" the yellow car turns and drives off; its counter slows ("< 500 Hz"). On "two causes" two chips side by side: "source moves → spacing changes" (with a small bunched-fronts icon) and "observer moves → meets fronts more or less often" (with a small even-fronts icon).

### 7. the equation
> Both causes are in the equation. The observed frequency, f prime, equals the source frequency times v wave plus v observer, over v wave minus v source. The top grows when the observer moves towards the source, because it meets the wavefronts faster. The bottom shrinks when the source moves towards the observer, because the wavefronts are squeezed together. So each speed, measured through the air along the line joining them, is positive when it's moving towards the other one, and negative when it's moving away. Then check: coming closer must always give a higher **frequency**.

Visual: camera continue; the right column clears. Equation strip, stacked fraction: "f′ = f (v_wave + v_observer) / (v_wave − v_source)". On "observed frequency, f prime" the gloss "f′: the frequency the observer receives" and "f: the source's own frequency". On "The top grows" the numerator glows and the "observer moves" chip slides beside it; on "The bottom shrinks" the denominator glows and the "source moves" chip slides beside it. On "positive when it's moving towards" a sign box: "towards: +   away: −" for both speeds (grey tag "speeds through still air, along the line joining them"). On "check" a grey check line "coming closer → f′ > f" stays under the equation.

### 8. worked example: the siren
> Let's put numbers on the hook. Task: find the frequency the microphone picks up as the car approaches, and as it moves away. The givens: the siren is five hundred hertz, the car moves at thirty metres per second, along the line to the microphone, the microphone is still, and sound travels at three hundred and forty metres per second. Approaching, v source is plus thirty, so f prime is five hundred times three forty over three ten, about five hundred and forty-eight **hertz**. Higher, as it should be. Moving away, v source is minus thirty, so the bottom becomes three seventy, and f prime is about four hundred and fifty-nine **hertz**. Lower, as it should **be**.

Visual: clear change to the calculation layout; the road small at the left with the car and the roadside microphone, the panel beside it. Task strip "Find f′ approaching and moving away". Equation strip as in 7. Givens group (navy): "f = 500 Hz; v_source = 30 m s⁻¹; v_observer = 0 (microphone still); v_wave = 340 m s⁻¹" (grey tag "site example values"). Rows, one per cue: "approaching: v_source = +30"; on the first "hertz" "f′ = 500 × 340 ÷ (340 − 30) = 500 × 340 ÷ 310 = 548 Hz", boxed; check "548 > 500 ✓". "moving away: v_source = −30"; on the second "hertz" "f′ = 500 × 340 ÷ (340 + 30) = 500 × 340 ÷ 370 = 459 Hz", boxed; on "be" check "459 < 500 ✓" and the panel fills "approaching: 548 Hz", "moving away: 459 Hz".

### 9. light: blueshift and redshift
> Light shifts too, but with a different equation, so we'll just describe it. A star or galaxy moving towards us has its light's wavelengths shortened, shifted towards the blue end of the spectrum. ## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A car horn emits sound of frequency 420 Hz. The car moves directly away from a stationary microphone at 20 m s⁻¹. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹. Calculate the frequency detected by the microphone, and state whether it is higher or lower than 420 Hz."
Model answer: The source moves away, so v_source = −20 m s⁻¹; the microphone is stationary, so v_observer = 0. f′ = f (v_wave + v_observer)/(v_wave − v_source) = 420 × (340 + 0)/(340 + 20) = 420 × 340/360 = 397 Hz (3 s.f.). This is lower than 420 Hz, as expected: the receding source spreads its wavefronts, so they arrive less often. Classic mistake (crossed): "f′ = 420 × 340 ÷ 320 = 446 Hz" (subtracting the source's speed while it moves away gives a higher frequency, the wrong direction).

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested).** "(a) A microphone on a train moves at 25 m s⁻¹ directly towards a stationary alarm emitting sound of frequency 600 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹. Calculate the frequency detected by the microphone. (b) A hydrogen spectral line with a laboratory wavelength of 656 nm is measured at 662 nm in the light from a distant galaxy. State whether this is a blueshift or a redshift, and explain what it shows about the galaxy's motion."
Model answer: (a) The observer moves towards the source: v_observer = +25 m s⁻¹; v_source = 0. f′ = 600 × (340 + 25)/340 = 600 × 365/340 = 644 Hz (3 s.f.), higher, because the microphone meets the wavefronts more often. (b) The line appears at a longer wavelength (662 nm > 656 nm), so it is a redshift. The light's wavelengths have been stretched, which happens when the source moves away from the observer, so the galaxy is moving away from us. Classic mistake (crossed): "the galaxy is moving towards us".
