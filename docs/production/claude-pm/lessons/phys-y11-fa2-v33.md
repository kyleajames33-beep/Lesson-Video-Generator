- **The passing trace (site misconception 2, corrected).** A coded trace of received frequency against time draws under the road as the car passes: steady and high, a quick fall around passing, steady and low. For a microphone beside the road (not in the car's path) the fall is quick but smooth, because only the part of the car's velocity towards the microphone counts; it is a sudden step only for a source heading straight through the microphone (site correction 1).

- **Moving observer (needed for the syllabus equation's v_observer; site card 2's sign rule).** The fronts are evenly spaced (the siren is parked), but the moving microphone runs into them faster than they would arrive on their own, so it meets more fronts each second: a higher frequency. The yellow car-top plate (`phys-y11-car-top-yellow-v1.png`) carries the coded microphone marker. Same direction of shift, different cause: the source changes the spacing; the observer changes the rate of meeting.
- **The equation (site card 2; syllabus form).** f′ = f (v_wave + v_observer)/(v_wave − v_source), written once as a stacked fraction in an equation strip with each symbol glossed. Read as the two causes: the numerator grows when the observer moves towards the source (meets fronts faster); the denominator shrinks when the source moves towards the observer (fronts squeezed). Sign rule: each speed is positive when that object moves towards the other, negative when it moves away. Sense check every time: coming closer must give a higher frequency (site card 2's check line).
- **Worked example (site `we-1`, unchanged numbers, then its moving-away case).** f = 500 Hz, v_source = 30 m s⁻¹ towards, v_observer = 0, v_wave = 340 m s⁻¹: f′ = 500 × 340/310 = 548 Hz. Then the same car moving away (v_source = −30 m s⁻¹): f′ = 500 × 340/370 = 459 Hz. The hook's panel fills: "approaching: 548 Hz", "moving away: 459 Hz". Layout: task strip, equation strip, givens group with units and signs; one row per cue. The receding value is computed here (the site's activity 1 does both cases with a 400 Hz train, kept for students).
- **Light (site card 3; v13 to v15).** The same rule: a star or galaxy moving towards us has its light's wavelengths shortened, shifting towards the blue end: blueshift; moving away, lengthened towards the red end: redshift. How we see it: known spectral lines (a hydrogen line at 656 nm, from v15's spectrum if present; tag otherwise) land at a longer wavelength in a receding galaxy's light. A coded spectrum strip with three lines slides as a block. Grey tags: "light's shift is calculated differently: not this equation"; "most distant galaxies are redshifted: more in Year 12". The words blueshift and redshift are used for light only; for sound the video says "higher" and "lower" (site correction 2).

### 2. the siren doesn't change
> First, what doesn't change. A second microphone rides on the car's roof, and it reads five hundred hertz the whole time. So the siren itself doesn't change its note. And the sound travels through the air at the same speed, three hundred and forty metres per second, whichever way the car is going, because the air sets the speed. What changes is how the wave's crests are spaced when they reach the **microphone**.

Visual: camera continue on the hook stage. ### 3. bunched ahead, spread behind
> Each crest spreads out as a circle, called a wavefront, centred on where the siren was when it sent that crest out. But the car keeps moving between one wavefront and the next. So each new wavefront starts a little closer to the one in front, and the wavefronts crowd together ahead of the car and spread out behind it. They all travel at the same speed, so ahead, closer wavefronts reach the microphone more often each second. That's a higher frequency. Behind the car, they arrive less often, so the frequency is lower. This change in frequency is called the Doppler **effect**.

### 6. the moving observer

### 7. the equation
> Both causes are in the equation. The observed frequency, f prime, equals the source frequency times v wave plus v observer, over v wave minus v source. The top grows when the observer moves towards the source, because it meets the wavefronts faster. The bottom shrinks when the source moves towards the observer, because the wavefronts are squeezed together. So each speed, measured through the air along the line joining them, is positive when it's moving towards the other one, and negative when it's moving away. Then check: coming closer must always give a higher **frequency**.

### 9. light: blueshift and redshift
> Light shifts too, but with a different equation, so we'll just describe it. A star or galaxy moving towards us has its light's wavelengths shortened, shifted towards the blue end of the spectrum. That's called a blueshift. One moving away has its wavelengths stretched towards the red end, a redshift. Astronomers see it in spectral lines, sharp lines at wavelengths set by the atoms that made the light. In a galaxy moving away, a hydrogen line that belongs at six hundred and fifty-six nanometres turns up at a longer wavelength. The galaxy doesn't turn red. Its whole pattern of lines slides towards the red **end**.
