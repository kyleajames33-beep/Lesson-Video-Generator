## Decisions

- **Site alignment:** site lesson 06 (`site-extracts/chem-m5-l06.md`) supplies idea 6 (catalysts lower both activation barriers, speed up equilibrium attainment, and do not change position or heat of reaction) and the misconception "a catalyst increases yield". Reused: the Haber iron catalyst (as the unseen attempt system), the catalyst-claim analysis (as the feedback's named wrong answers), and the "same final composition, reached sooner" simulator story. Not used: Keq statements (idea 7 and "Keq unchanged": K waits for c05 and c09), the combined 550 C and 300 atm Haber question (c07), and the simulator's N2O4 numbers (12.1 s, 1.5 s, 7.9 times; replaced, see next point).
- **Why not the site's N2O4 simulator or the C4a syringe:** the real 2NO2 and N2O4 equilibrium is established almost instantly and needs no catalyst, so a "catalysed syringe" would invite the question "why would anyone catalyse that?". The worked example instead uses H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI with a metal catalyst (learner copy says "a metal catalyst"; platinum is named only in these notes, as an accepted teaching model, see author note (a)): the same hot hydrogen and iodine tube as C4a scene 6a (and the sealed H2/I2 tube of C1), purple iodine gives visible evidence in both tubes, the gas counts are equal so no pressure effect competes, and platinum is a textbook catalyst for the HI reaction (decomposition: roughly 184 kJ/mol uncatalysed against roughly 59 kJ/mol on platinum, to be confirmed by Codex). The unseen attempt uses the Haber process with iron, which is the site's own catalyst example.
- **Naming activation energy (decision requested by the task):** yes, now named, with the plain meaning "the minimum energy a collision needs to react". C3 deliberately avoided the word and taught "only collisions with enough energy make a reaction happen"; C4b reuses that sentence verbatim as the bridge, then names the quantity. The coverage ledger gives C4B ownership of "activation energy: minimum reacting energy" and the syllabus action C-activation (p1072, "examine how activation energy and heat of reaction affect the position of equilibrium"). The answer to that dotpoint is taught as two sentences: a lower activation energy changes how fast a mixture arrives, never where it is; the heat of reaction (the height difference between the valleys) decides which direction a temperature change favours. "Heat of reaction" is new wording (C3 said only that a reaction "takes in heat" or "gives out heat"), so it is introduced in 2c with its C3 meaning: going down the slope gives out heat, going up takes it in. The sentence "the height is energy" in 2a says what a valley's height means.
- **Entry knowledge:** C1 to C4a and Year 11. A student who has watched C4a knows forward and reverse rates on a made-up scale, "the moment of the change" then "the response", the faster rate falls and the slower rate rises until they meet, that heating, and squeezing a gas with unequal gas counts, change both rates by different factors, the equal-count H2 + I2 squeeze raised both rates four times so nothing shifted, and the hot H2 + I2 tube is purple. Nothing about catalysts or activation energy is assumed (a student may have met them in Year 11; the video does not rely on it).
- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) only collisions with enough energy react, and the minimum energy is the activation energy, drawn as a ridge between two valleys, the same ridge standing in the way of the reverse trip; (2) a catalyst gives the molecules an alternative pathway (they land on the metal catalyst, split into atoms, pair up and leave) and the metal is left unchanged (2b); the hardest step on that route needs less energy than the old ridge, so the pass is lower, the valleys do not move, and the heat of reaction (the height difference between the valleys: down gives out heat, up takes it in) is unchanged (2c); (3) a lower pass means more collisions get through, so the rate rises; the trip back climbs to the same new top from a valley that has not moved, so its climb is lower by the same height, and a drop of the same height multiplies the successful collisions by the same factor from either side; (4) so at every mixture both rates are ten times bigger (the made-up factor), every change takes a tenth of the time, and the journey is the same journey played ten times faster; (5) the balance point cannot move: at the old balance mixture the rates were equal (0.2 and 0.2 per tick), ten times each is 2 and 2, still equal, and any other mixture had unequal rates, still unequal; a tube stops changing only where its rates are equal, and that is the same mixture; (6) so the catalysed tube settles sooner at the same mixture; (7) stopping the clock early shows more product with the catalyst (limited time), but not at equilibrium; (8) a catalyst added to a tube already at equilibrium raises both rates together, so there is a moment of the change but no response; (9) one sorting rule for the series: a shift needs the two rates to change by different factors. Adding a reacting substance changes one rate (an inert gas in a fixed box changes none, as in C4a); heating and squeezing a gas with unequal gas counts change both by different factors; a catalyst, and a squeeze with equal gas counts (the C4a H2 + I2 case), change both by the same factor, so nothing pulls ahead.
- **Equal factor stated with its scope (route stop rule: "no universal equal enhancement factor claim"):** the learner copy says that in this example the factor is ten and that the same pass serves both trips, so both rates rise by the same factor. It never claims a general number, never says "catalysts always speed both directions by exactly the same factor under all conditions", and keeps the conclusion that is universal: at equilibrium the two rates stay equal, so the mixture does not shift. See author note (b).
- **Numbers (made-up scale, same status as C2 and C3):** concentrations in "units". Start for both tubes: H2 9, I2 9, HI 0. Equilibrium: H2 2, I2 2, HI 14 (K would be 14^2 / (2 x 2) = 49, consistent with the textbook value near 450 C; K is never named). **One scale for rate and clock (review finding 3):** the rates are the model's own rates per tick of the clock, shown in "units of H2 used per tick" (one unit of H2 used per reaction event; each event also uses one I2 and makes two HI, which the 4a chip says): forward starts at about 4 (model 0.05 x 81 = 4.05) and reverse at 0; at the settled mixture both are 0.2 (spoken "a fifth"; 0.05 x 2 x 2 = 0.2 and (0.05 / 49) x 14^2 = 0.2), and with the catalyst both are 2 (0.2 x 10). These agree with the clock: at the start H2 falls by about 4 units per tick, and the rates fall to 0.2 as the mixture approaches 2, 2, 14. Time axis labelled "time (made-up clock)" in ticks: uncatalysed settles at about 13, catalysed at about 1.3 (13 / 10; exactly one tenth because every rate is ten times larger). "Settles" means within about 5 per cent of the way to the final values: the model reaches that at clock 13.4 (tube A) and 1.34 (tube B); at clock 12 the rates have not quite met (forward 0.230, reverse 0.192) and I2 is 2.146, so "about twelve" was withdrawn after the Claude science check. At clock time 3 the uncatalysed tube has about 10 units of HI and the catalysed tube has about 14 (the catalysed curve at time 3 equals the uncatalysed curve at time 30). The values come from the model forward = 0.05 x [H2][I2], reverse = (0.05 / 49) x [HI]^2 on the clock, integrated numerically (reproduced independently by the learning review and the Claude science check: 10.13 at clock 3; within 5 per cent at clock 13.4 and 1.34); they are shown as labelled "about" tags read off the graph, never as dots. The catalysed curve is the uncatalysed curve compressed by ten along the time axis, nothing else. A chip says "made-up numbers to show the pattern" in scene 4a. The dashed settle lines carry a small visual chip "near enough" (the curve is still drifting slightly between 13 and 30).
- **Learner-visible definitions:** catalyst (a substance that speeds a reaction up without being used up, said in the hook); activation energy (the minimum energy a collision needs to react); alternative pathway (a different route from reactants to products, spoken in 2b); the height of a valley is energy (2a); heat of reaction (the energy difference between the valleys; down gives out heat, up takes it in, 2c); "settles" (the mixture has almost stopped changing; used throughout instead of "final mixture" or "reaches equilibrium" in the worked example and the attempt); yield is not used in learner copy (the misconception chip reads "a catalyst gives more product at equilibrium"); forward = making HI, reverse = splitting HI (defined once at scene 4a, forward arrow blue and reverse arrow amber as in C2 to C4a).
- **Attempt system (unseen):** N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g) in two identical rigid sealed vessels at the same steady temperature, starting from nitrogen and hydrogen only, vessel B with iron. Item one tests which vessel settles first, the amount "if we stop the clock early, before either vessel has settled", the amount "once both have settled", and the activation energy explanation (the same two cuts as scenes 4d and 4b). Item two tests a catalyst added to a mixture already at equilibrium (rates, amount, reason). Both items use only taught content. The answer to the first item's colour-free demands differs from the worked example only in system and labels, so copying is possible but the reasoning has to be rebuilt; the early-stop demand is the transfer step (the catalyst does win while the clock is early, before either vessel has settled). The Claude science check found that uncatalysed gas-phase N2 and H2 is negligibly slow at any temperature where ammonia is favoured, so the attempt is an idealised comparison: the narration and the board say "imagine both are left long enough to settle; without a catalyst that takes an extremely long time", and feedback 9a says "however long vessel A takes". It never implies that A settles in a practical time.
- **Mistake scene:** none separate. The wrong instincts ("a catalyst gives a higher yield", "it only speeds up the forward reaction", "adding it to an equilibrium shifts it", "a lower activation energy moves the position") are corrected in scenes 3, 4c, 4d, 5, 6 and the feedback.
- **Reuse:** C2 and C3 rate bars (made-up units), stage table, concentration and rate graphs with snapshot dots and the teal "rates equal" line, the C3 collision strip, the C4a hot H2 + I2 tube styling (purple iodine, colourless HI), countdown, key notes board, colour tokens (forward blue, reverse amber, equal teal). New in C4b: a coded energy profile (two valleys, a ridge, a dashed lower pass, activation-energy brackets and a heat-of-reaction bracket), a metal-catalyst surface mini-scene, a two-tube comparison board with tube A's curves drawn first, a "film at ten times speed" clock tag, and a three-row comparison of disturbances. No prototype components.
- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) the rate model forward = k[H2][I2], reverse = k'[HI]^2 is the textbook homogeneous gas-phase law; a metal-catalysed reaction (platinum is the textbook catalyst for HI decomposition, roughly 184 kJ/mol uncatalysed against roughly 59 kJ/mol; no source was found for H2 + I2 over platinum near 450 C, and adsorbed iodine is reported to block H2 adsorption on Pt/Al2O3) is a surface process with its own rate law, so "ten times at every mixture" is a teaching model for the pattern, not a measured law. (b) What is universal is thermodynamic: a catalyst cannot change K, so at fixed temperature it must change the forward and reverse overall rates by factors that keep their ratio at equilibrium, which means the rates at equilibrium stay equal. Real catalysed rates can saturate or be inhibited by a species, so equal factors "at every mixture" is not claimed outside the model. (c) The "one in twenty to ten in twenty" collision strip is a counting illustration of a tenfold increase, not a real fraction; real activation energy changes of about 100 kJ/mol change the fraction by many orders of magnitude, which is why "we use ten" is said. (d) "Settles" is defined on the graph as the clock reading where the concentrations are within about 5 per cent of the way to their final values (clock 13.4 for tube A, 1.34 for tube B); a real approach never reaches the final value exactly. (e) The site says catalysts leave "Keq, delta H unchanged"; the video says the heat of reaction is unchanged and does not mention K. (f) A real catalyst can in principle change which products form when several reactions compete; this video concerns one reversible reaction only. (g) Pronunciation: "H two", "I two", "H I", "N two", "H two", "N H three" for ammonia; "iron" plain; the worked-example catalyst is "a metal catalyst". (h) The heating example in scene 6 refers to C3's finding that the reaction that takes in heat gains most: that reaction has the higher ridge, because the heat of reaction equals the difference between the two ridge heights (forward minus reverse). This is stated in words only, never as an equation. (i) The ridge and ball picture is an analogy with limits: a ball is pushed over a hill by gravity and momentum, while a molecule gets its energy from collisions; the horizontal axis is labelled "reaction pathway", the ridge is a barrier on a pathway and not a real hill, and the catalyst route is drawn as surface steps, not as gas collisions. If the voiced preview shows students reading the ball as rolling by itself, add "a collision gives the molecules the push" to 2a. (j) The 20-slot count strips are a counting illustration of a tenfold rise on the same twenty events; the amber strip starts lower (1 in 100) because the return ridge is higher.

Main question: *A catalyst makes a reaction faster. So why doesn't it give more product at equilibrium?*
Entry knowledge (C1 to C4a and Year 11): dynamic equilibrium means equal non-zero rates in a closed system; rate means how much changes per second; the faster rate falls and the slower rate rises until they meet; a disturbance shows up as "the moment of the change" then "the response"; only collisions with enough energy make a reaction happen (C3); heating helps the reaction that takes in heat most (C3); a squeeze with unequal gas counts changes the two rates by different factors, and the equal-count H2 + I2 squeeze raised both by the same factor with no shift (C4a).
Stops after: explaining a catalyst as an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy that raises the forward and reverse rates, so equilibrium is reached sooner at an unchanged mixture; telling product formed in a limited time from the amount at equilibrium; and stating what a catalyst does to a mixture already at equilibrium. No K or Q, no Boltzmann distribution, no mechanisms beyond one picture of a surface, no industrial trade-offs, no early K aside.
Handoff: putting a number on where an equilibrium sits (c05), without naming it.

### 2a. the ridge (activation energy)
> Most collisions just bounce apart. Only collisions with enough energy make a reaction happen. The minimum energy a collision needs is called the **activation energy**. Picture it as a ridge between two valleys. The height is energy: each valley is the energy stored in those molecules. Hydrogen and iodine sit in one valley, hydrogen iodide in the other, and the top of the ridge is the energy a collision must reach. Hydrogen iodide has to climb the same ridge to go back.

Visual: the tubes shrink away. A short collision strip (reuse from C3): two molecules bump slowly and bounce (grey "bounce"), then bump hard and a reaction event happens. Then the energy profile draws on (vertical axis "energy", horizontal axis "reaction pathway", both labelled): a smooth curve from a left valley labelled "H2 + I2" up over a high ridge down into a right valley "2HI" lower than the left by about one fifth of the ridge height (the gap stays clearly visible so the lower amber strip in scene 3 does not look arbitrary). On "The height is energy" a pen arrow runs up the energy axis and each valley is ringed in turn. The heat-of-reaction bracket is not drawn yet.

### 2b. an alternative pathway (the route)
> A catalyst gives the molecules an **alternative pathway**, a different route. They land on the metal, split into atoms that stick to the surface, the atoms pair up as hydrogen iodide and leave. The metal ends up unchanged, ready for the next molecules.

### 2c. a lower pass (the energy picture)

### 3. a lower pass speeds both trips
> A lower pass means a faster reaction, because more collisions have enough energy to get through. Of the same twenty collisions, say one gets over the high ridge, and ten get through the low pass. That is ten times as many successful collisions every second. Hydrogen iodide going back climbs to the same new top. The valleys have not moved, so its climb is lower by the same height. A drop of the same height multiplies the successful collisions by the same factor, whichever side you start from, so its successful collisions also rise ten times. Ten is a made-up factor.

### 6. which rates change, and by how much
> Adding a reacting substance changed one rate. Heating, or squeezing a gas with different gas counts on each side, changed both rates by different factors, so one reaction pulled ahead and the mixture shifted. Squeezing hydrogen and iodine, with two gas particles on each side, raised both rates by the same factor, and nothing shifted. A catalyst lowers both ridges by the same height, so it is like that case: both rates rise by the same factor and nothing pulls ahead.

### 11. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Activation energy is the minimum energy a collision needs to react. A catalyst gives the reaction an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, and it is not used up.
- The heat of reaction is the energy difference between reactants and products, and a catalyst does not change it. So the catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount, and at a steady temperature both rates rise by the same factor.
- A catalyst makes a mixture settle sooner but does not change the mixture it settles at. Before the uncatalysed mixture has settled, a catalyst can give more product; once both have settled, the amounts are the same.
- Added to a mixture already at equilibrium, a catalyst raises both rates by the same factor, so they stay equal. There is a moment of the change but no response, and nothing shifts.
- A shift needs the two rates to change by different factors. Adding a reacting substance, heating, or squeezing a gas with different gas counts does that. A catalyst, or squeezing a gas with equal gas counts, does not. The reaction that takes in heat has the higher activation energy, so heating helps it most.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *A catalyst changes the speed, not the destination.* Condition tag: "same temperature, closed system. Speed: forward and reverse together."
