### 2. how the eye focuses
> Light enters the eye through the **cornea**, its clear, curved front, which does most of the bending. The **lens** behind it bends the light a little more and fine-tunes the focus. The light should meet at a sharp point on the **retina**, a layer of light-sensitive cells at the back, which send signals along the **optic nerve** to the brain. For a near object, a ring of muscle around the lens contracts, the fibres holding the lens go slack, and the lens rounds up to bend light more. That's **accommodation**.

### 3. myopia: power against length
> Clear vision needs the eye's bending power to match its length. In **myopia**, or short-sightedness, the eyeball is usually too long, or the cornea too steeply curved. Light from a distant object comes to a point in front of the retina, then spreads out again, so distant things look blurred. Light from a near object is already spreading as it arrives, so it focuses further back, on the retina, and near things look clear. To remember it: with myopia, you see what's near "my" eyes.

### 4. hyperopia, astigmatism, presbyopia

### 5. cataract: a clarity fault
> Some faults aren't about focus at all. A **cataract** is a lens that has turned cloudy. Proteins in the lens clump together, so instead of passing straight through, light is scattered. Vision becomes dim and blurred, colours fade, and headlights cause glare. Ageing is the main cause, and sunlight, smoking and diabetes can raise the risk. So the eye might focus perfectly, but the light can't get through clearly, and glasses can't clear a cloudy lens.

### 6. glasses and contact lenses
> For a focus fault, change how light is bent before it reaches the retina. **Glasses** do it from outside the eye. For myopia, a concave lens, thinner in the middle, spreads the light a little, moving the focus back onto the retina. ### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Light path: cornea (most of the bending, fixed) → lens (fine focus; rounds up for near objects, accommodation) → retina (light-sensitive cells) → optic nerve → brain.
- Myopia: eye too long or too powerful; distant light focuses in front of the retina; concave lens. Hyperopia: eye too short or too weak; focus behind the retina; convex lens. Astigmatism: uneven cornea; toric lens. Presbyopia: stiff lens with age; convex reading glasses.
- Cataract: cloudy lens scatters light (a clarity fault); glasses can't clear it; replace the lens with a clear artificial one (reading glasses usually still needed).
- Laser surgery (LASIK): flap, laser reshapes the cornea (flatten the centre for myopia); permanent, needs a stable prescription and thick enough cornea; dry eyes and glare; doesn't stop presbyopia.
- Glasses: safe, cheap, reversible, any age. Contacts: wider view, infection risk. Match the technology to the fault and the person; none removes the cause.

## Numbers or facts to verify

- **Myopia projection (scene 1).** Holden BA et al., *Ophthalmology* 2016;123(5):1036 to 1042: 4,758 million people (49.8 per cent of the world) projected to have myopia by 2050. Spoken "about half the people in the world may be short-sighted, if current trends continue". Correct.
- **"A millimetre or so" (scene 1).** About 1 mm of extra axial length gives about 2.5 to 3 dioptres of myopia; most myopes have low to moderate myopia. Spoken as "a millimetre or so". Codex to confirm the wording is fair; the site says "a few millimetres", which fits high myopia.
- **Cornea and lens (scene 2).** The cornea provides about two thirds of the eye's refractive power (about 40 of about 60 dioptres) and is fixed; the lens provides the rest and changes shape. Spoken "does most of the bending". Correct.
- **Accommodation (scene 2).** Ciliary muscle contracts, zonules (suspensory ligaments) slacken, the elastic lens becomes more convex. Spoken in plain words. Correct.
- **Myopia and near vision (scene 3).** Light from near objects diverges, so in a myopic eye its image forms further back, on or near the retina. Correct as a Year 12 account.
- **Hyperopia (scene 4).** Short axial length or weak cornea; young people can accommodate to compensate; near vision blurs most. Correct.
- **Astigmatism (scene 4).** Unequal corneal curvature in different meridians; no single point focus. Correct.
- **Presbyopia onset (scene 4).** Near-vision symptoms commonly from about age 40 to 45. Spoken "from about the mid-forties". Correct.
- **Cataract (scene 5).** Aggregation of lens crystallins causes opacity and light scatter; symptoms include blur, dimness, faded colours and glare; age is the main risk factor; UV exposure, smoking and diabetes are recognised risk factors (WHO, AIHW, RANZCO). Spoken "Ageing is the main cause, and sunlight, smoking and diabetes can raise the risk". Correct.
- **Concave and convex (scene 6).** Concave (diverging) lenses correct myopia; convex (converging) lenses correct hyperopia and presbyopia; toric lenses correct astigmatism. Correct.
- **Contact lens infection (scene 6).** Microbial keratitis risk rises several-fold with overnight (extended) wear and poor hygiene. Spoken "worn overnight or cleaned poorly, they can let an infection take hold in the cornea". Correct.
- **LASIK (scene 7).** Flap about 100 to 120 micrometres (microkeratome or femtosecond laser); excimer laser (193 nm) ablates stroma; myopic ablation removes more centrally, flattening the cornea; flap adheres without sutures; candidates need a stable refraction (commonly at least 12 months) and adequate corneal thickness; common side effects dry eye and night glare or halos; presbyopia still develops.
