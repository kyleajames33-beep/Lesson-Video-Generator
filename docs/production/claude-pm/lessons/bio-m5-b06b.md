So how do the instructions in a gene get from one place to the other, when the DNA itself **never leaves**? The cell sends out a copy. Today, how a cell copies one gene, and why it bothers to copy at all.

### 2. a gene, and the copy called mRNA
> A **gene** is a section of DNA whose order of bases codes for a product, usually a **polypeptide**, a chain of amino acids that folds into a protein. To use a gene, the cell copies its bases into a single strand of RNA called messenger RNA, or **mRNA**. That copying is called **transcription**. Think of a recipe book that is **never allowed** out of the library. You copy the one page you need, and carry the copy to the kitchen. The book stays safe, and you can make as many copies as you like.

### 3. how RNA differs from DNA
> RNA is a nucleic acid like DNA, built from nucleotides, but with three differences. It is a **single strand**, not a double helix. Its sugar is **ribose**, not deoxyribose, which is what the R in RNA stands for. And it uses a base called **uracil**, U, in place of thymine, T. U pairs with A, just as T does, so the pairing rule becomes **A with U**, and C with G.

On "U pairs with A" the amber U slides up against a coral A tile and clicks into its notch; a ghost T tile beside it shows the same fit. ### 4a. RNA polymerase opens the gene
> The copying is done by an enzyme called **RNA polymerase**. First it has to find where the gene starts. Just before each gene is a stretch of DNA called the **promoter**, a landing site that the polymerase binds to. Once it has bound, the polymerase pulls the two DNA strands apart over a short stretch, **breaking the hydrogen bonds** between the bases, so the bases are exposed.

### 4b. one strand is the template
> Here is the key point. The polymerase copies **only one** of the two strands, called the **template strand**. The other one, the **coding strand**, is not read. One strand is enough, because the template fixes every base of the mRNA, just as one DNA strand fixed its partner when DNA was copied.

Visual: on "only one" the lower strand of the bubble glows amber along its backbone and the tag "template strand: read" writes on; the upper strand dims to about 35 per cent (dim rule) with the tag "coding strand: not read". ### 4c. which way the copy grows
> Now the direction. The polymerase can only add each new nucleotide to the **3' end** of the growing mRNA, so the mRNA grows from its 5' end to its 3' end. And because paired strands run in opposite directions, the polymerase reads the template **from its 3' end to its 5' end**.

### 5a. worked example: building the mRNA
> Let's copy one. Here is a template strand, with its 3' end on the left: **T A C, C G A, A T T**. The polymerase starts at the 3' end and moves right. Free RNA nucleotides drift in, and only a matching one stays. T needs **A**. A needs **U**, not T, because this is RNA. C needs **G**. The next three: C with G, G with C, A with U. The last three: A with U, T with A, T with A. Each new nucleotide joins the 3' end of the one before. The mRNA reads **A U G, G C U, U A A**, from its 5' end.

### 5b. the coding strand check
> Now look at the strand the polymerase did not read, the **coding strand**. It reads A T G, G C T, T A A, from its 5' end. Compare it with the mRNA: the same letters, in the same direction, except that **T has become U**. So the mRNA is a copy of the coding strand, built by pairing with the template strand. That is the trap in many exam questions, so always check **which strand** you have been given.

### 6a. the copy leaves; the gene stays
> At the end of the gene, the polymerase lets go, and the new mRNA comes away. Behind the polymerase, the two DNA strands have already **paired up again**, so the gene is unchanged and ready to be copied again. In a cell like ours, the mRNA leaves the nucleus through a **nuclear pore**, a small opening in the nuclear envelope, and travels out to the ribosomes.

### 7. not the same as DNA copying
> Transcription can look like DNA copying. Both open up the DNA, and both build a new strand by **base pairing** on a template. But replication copies **both strands** of all the DNA, into new DNA, before a cell divides. Transcription copies **one strand** of one gene, into RNA with U, whenever the cell needs that gene's product. In one sentence: both use a DNA template, **whereas** only transcription makes RNA, from one strand of one gene.

### 8. read in threes, and why one base matters
> The mRNA is read in groups of three bases, called **codons**. Our mRNA has three: A U G, G C U and U A A. What each codon means is the next video. But one consequence follows already. In a gene such as **CFTR**, the gene linked to cystic fibrosis, if one base in the template strand changes, the matching base in the mRNA changes too, so a **codon changes** before any protein is made.

### 9. memory
> Two things to remember. First, **transcription keeps the language**. DNA and RNA are both written in bases, so it is like copying notes in the same language. Translation, next, changes the language, from bases to amino acids. Second, the trap: mRNA **matches** the coding strand, but it is **built on** the template strand.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- A gene is a section of DNA that codes for a product, usually a polypeptide. Transcription copies one gene into mRNA.
- RNA polymerase binds the promoter, separates the DNA strands and builds mRNA by pairing with the template strand: A with U, T with A, C with G, G with C.
- The template is read 3' to 5'; the mRNA is built 5' to 3'. The mRNA has the same sequence as the coding strand, with U in place of T.
- DNA stays in the nucleus. mRNA leaves through nuclear pores to the ribosomes. Many temporary mRNA copies let one gene make a lot of protein.
- Replication: both strands, all the DNA, makes DNA. Transcription: one strand, one gene, makes RNA.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (16 Oct) against standard texts (Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell; Campbell Biology) and the sources named; Codex or the science check should confirm.
- **Worked example (site).** Template 3' TAC CGA ATT 5'. Pairs: T-A, A-U, C-G; C-G, G-C, A-U; A-U, T-A, T-A. mRNA 5' AUG GCU UAA 3'. Coding strand 5' ATG GCT TAA 3'. Checked all nine positions; the mRNA equals the coding strand with U for T.
- **Attempt 1.** Top (template) 3' TGC AAT CGG 5'. Pairs: T-A, G-C, C-G; A-U, A-U, T-A; C-G, G-C, G-C. mRNA 5' ACG UUA GCC 3'. Bottom (coding) 5' ACG TTA GCC 3' is the complement of the top (checked), and equals the mRNA with U for T. Wrong route (pairing the bottom): 3' UGC AAU CGG 5'. The sequence has no restriction-site palindrome or self-complementary run (checked) so no strand reads the same either way.
- **Scene 8 illustration.** Template 4th base C changed to T gives mRNA G changed to A: codon 2 GCU becomes ACU. Labelled "illustration, not the real CFTR change" (the commonest CFTR variant, F508del, is a three-base deletion, not a substitution).
- **Direction.** RNA polymerase synthesises RNA 5' to 3' by adding nucleotides to the 3'-OH of the growing chain, reading the template 3' to 5'; the RNA is antiparallel to the template and has the coding strand's sequence (Alberts, ch. 6). RNA polymerase needs no primer (not spoken).
- **RNA differences.** Single strand, ribose, uracil pairs with adenine (two hydrogen bonds, as T does). Correct.
- **Promoter.** A DNA sequence just upstream of the gene where RNA polymerase binds (in eukaryotes with general transcription factors; author note). The polymerase separates the strands over a short stretch (about 10 to 20 base pairs; not spoken) and the DNA re-pairs behind it.
- **Export.** In eukaryotes mRNA leaves through nuclear pores; DNA does not. Correct. Processing (cap, tail, splicing) is omitted (Decisions, held back).
- **mRNA is temporary.** Mammalian mRNA half-lives range from minutes to many hours, median around 9 hours in one large mouse-cell study (Schwanhausser et al. 2011, Nature 473:337). The narration says "broken down after a while" and the feedback "over the following hours": consistent.
- **Death cap.** Amanita phalloides contains alpha-amanitin, which binds RNA polymerase II under the bridge helix and blocks its translocation along DNA, stopping mRNA synthesis; the liver is mainly affected because hepatocytes take up the toxin (review: [PMC8230822](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8230822)). In Australia it grows under introduced trees, mainly oaks, and is recorded in the ACT, Victoria and South Australia ([Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria](https://rbg.vic.gov.au/science/herbarium/death-cap); [ACT Government](https://www.environment.act.gov.au/parks-conservation/plants-and-animals/biosecurity/invasive-plants/deathcap-mushrooms)). The stem says "stops RNA polymerase moving along DNA", which matches the mechanism. Cell death in poisoning also involves p53 and apoptosis; the question asks only about protein production, so this stays out. Strictly, alpha-amanitin blocks RNA polymerase II (the polymerase that makes mRNA) strongly, polymerase III weakly and polymerase I not at all; it traps the bridge helix and trigger loop so the enzyme cannot add nucleotides and translocate (Brueckner and Cramer 2008, Nat Struct Mol Biol 15:811, PDB 2VUM). The stem's "stops RNA polymerase moving along DNA" is a fair school form (science check finding 3). Death caps are also reported from Tasmania (Launceston, under oak); "parts of Australia" stays true.
- **NESA feedback** (pack): 2022 Q31(b) noted confusion between transcription and replication and missing uracil; 2024 feedback asks for a similarity and a difference. Scene 7 and the 10d crossed-out chip answer both.

## Memory aid check

- **"Transcription keeps the language, translation changes it" (bank insight, with "C before L").** True: transcription copies nucleic acid to nucleic acid (bases to bases), translation converts a base sequence to an amino acid sequence. The alphabet cue is a mnemonic for order only, and it holds (transcription precedes translation for every gene). Limit: "same language" does not mean the same letters (U replaces T, and the copy is complementary to the template); scene 3 and scene 5b show this before the aid is given, and the aid says "language", not "letters". In bacteria translation can begin while transcription continues; the order still holds for any one stretch of mRNA.
- **"mRNA is a copy of the coding strand, built on the template strand" (bank trap).** True for the transcribed sequence: complementary and antiparallel to the template, the same as the coding strand with U for T. Limit (author note): in eukaryotes the mature mRNA has introns removed and a cap and tail added, so it matches the coding strand's exons only; no question in this video depends on that. It helps a new case: attempt 1's check against the coding strand.
