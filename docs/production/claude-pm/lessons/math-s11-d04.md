### 1. hook: the headline
> Our survey's in. Twelve students charge their phones in their bedrooms, and eight charge them somewhere else, and the bedroom group reported less sleep. Then this headline turns up. Phones in bedrooms steal two hours of sleep! It sounds like exactly what we found. It isn't. Even a fair sample can be let down by how the data were collected, and by what people claim afterwards. ### 2. an observational study just watches
> Look at how our data were collected. We didn't tell anyone where to charge their phone. We just asked. That's an observational study, just watching what happens. Observational studies are useful, but they have a big weakness. The two groups can differ in other ways too. Remember the early bus? In our sample, five of the six bus students charge their phones in their bedrooms. Bus students get up earlier. So the bedroom group might sleep less because of the bus, not the **phone**. Something else could explain the difference. That's why an observational study can show that two things happen together, but it can't show that one causes the **other**.

### 3. a controlled experiment changes one thing
> To test whether the phone itself makes a difference, you'd run a controlled experiment. That means you change one thing on purpose, and keep everything else the same. Take forty volunteers. A random draw puts twenty of them in a group whose phones charge in the kitchen for two weeks, and the other twenty carry on as usual. The random draw is the key. It spreads the bus students, and everything else, across both groups, three bus students in each. If the kitchen group then sleeps more, the phone is the likely **reason**. Experiments have faults too. If people choose their own group, all six bus students might end up together. People might sneak their phones back, or sleep differently because they know they're being watched. And two weeks might be too short, or forty volunteers might not represent Year **11**.

Visual: stage change: a second panel "controlled experiment: change one thing on purpose and keep everything else the same" (violet label, 40 px; hand tag "change one thing on purpose"). ### 4. practical faults: from asking to recording
> Now the practical side. Picture the whole process as a pipeline, from the question, to the people, to the method, to the recording, to the claim. Faults can creep in at every stage. The question might be unclear, like our old overlapping **buttons**. Some people chosen for the sample might not reply. If five of our twenty don't answer, and they're the ones who slept least, the results look better than they **are**. People guess and round. Ask about sleep, and lots of answers come back as whole hours. Someone might type seven point five as seventy-five, and suddenly a student slept seventy-five hours. And timing matters. A survey in exam week, or after a long weekend, describes that week, not a normal one. The fix is to plan for each stage, follow up the people who didn't reply, and check the data before you **use** it.

Visual: stage change (simulation 3): the fault map: five stages left to right as violet-labelled blue boxes "question", "people", "method", "recording", "claim", with a "trust" meter (green bar) above. ### 5. misunderstandings and misrepresentations
> The last stage is the claim, and this is where the headline goes wrong. A misrepresentation presents results so they seem to show more than they do. Steal says the phone causes it, but ours was an observational study. Phones in bedrooms suggests every teenager, but we asked twenty students at one school, on one night. And two hours isn't what our survey found for everyone. Percentages can mislead too. Sixty per cent of students sleep with their phone sounds huge, but it's twelve out of **twenty**. Misunderstandings happen on the other side, when people read a result, or a question, differently from what was meant. Hours slept might be read as hours in bed. Here's an honest version. In our sample of twenty Year 11 students, those who charged their phones in their bedrooms tended to report less sleep. That doesn't show the phone caused **it**.

### 6. a checklist for any data collection
> So whenever you judge a data collection, walk the pipeline. Is the question clear? Did the right people answer, and did enough of them reply? Was it an experiment or an observational study, and does the claim match? Were the answers recorded and checked? And does the claim stay inside what the sample and method can **show**? Every yes makes the results easier to trust.
