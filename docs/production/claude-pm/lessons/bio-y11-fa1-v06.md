## Decisions

- **Main teaching move: you can't measure one cell, so measure the whole view and share it out.** The field of view is a circle of known size once a mini-grid has measured it; the cells across its diameter divide it into equal shares. The surprise is that the magnification never enters the syllabus calculation: the grid sat on the stage and was magnified exactly as a specimen is, so its millimetres are real millimetres (site outline answer: "the grid is a ruler marked in REAL micrometres, not in magnified ones"). Students who keep that cause can tell when to divide by magnification (a magnified image) and when not to (a field measured with the grid).
- **Syllabus method first (route note, root approval).** Scenes 2 to 8 are the field-of-view method: (1) measure the field diameter at low power with the mini-grid; (2) swap in the slide on the same objective and count cells across the diameter; (3) cell size = field diameter ÷ cells across; (4) at higher power, calculate the field from the low-power one; (5) repeat counts, take the mean; (6) compare a variety of specialised cells. The site's image size ÷ magnification appears once, in scene 9, as a cross-check on the cheek cell (55 µm against 56 µm), with the divide-not-multiply trap. The site's other direct method (reading a cell against stage-grid squares of 100 µm) is not shown: it is not the syllabus equation, and a second grid method would compete with the first.
- **Why the higher-power field step is included (scene 6).** The syllabus names the mini-grid and the equation; a 1 mm mini-grid can only be counted at low power (at ×400 the field, 0.45 mm, is narrower than one square). The cheek cell (and most specialised cells) need high power, so students must find the high-power field from the low-power one. This is the standard school method and the core of attempt 1. Cause given in one sentence: ten times the magnification shows a patch one tenth as wide, spread over the same circle. Assumption (author note; science check to confirm): the eyepiece stays the same and field diameter is inversely proportional to the objective's magnification, which holds closely for standard school microscopes.
- **The prediction beat (scene 6).** Before the higher-power field is calculated, students predict what ten times the magnification does to the field. A 2 s thinking hold with a "Predict" chip (not a countdown), as v05 scene 6 and p01 scene 5.
- **Worked numbers (constructed, realistic for a school microscope with a ×10 eyepiece of field number 18).** Field at ×40 (×4 objective): 4.5 mm = 4500 µm (four whole 1 mm squares and about half of the next). Onion epidermis at ×40: 15 cells end to end along the diameter, so 4500 ÷ 15 = 300 µm (site: "an onion epidermal cell can be 200–300 µm long"). Field at ×100: 1800 µm (author note only; not shown). Field at ×400: 4500 × 40 ÷ 400 = 450 µm. Cheek cells at ×400: about 8 across, so 450 ÷ 8 = 56.25 ≈ 56 µm (site: "a human cheek cell about 60 µm"). Extra example counts on other lines: 8, 7.5, 8.5 (mean 8.0). Photomicrograph at ×400: 22 mm = 22 000 µm, ÷ 400 = 55 µm. Red blood cell about 8 µm and nerve cell over 1 m (site's values, quoted, not calculated). Checked under "Numbers to verify".
- **Significant figures (scene 7).** 56.25 is given as "about 56 µm" (2 significant figures), because the count is an estimate to the nearest half cell. The site: "Always state results to an appropriate number of significant figures and include the unit."
- **Reliability (scene 7).** Count cell widths end to end along the diameter (same direction, gaps closed up, part-widths as fractions) in several fields, then take the mean (site: "repeating the measurement on several cells improves the reliability of an estimate"). Validity and accuracy words are not taught here (later practicals and the exam skills series); a necessary condition is kept in learner copy: count along a line through the centre (the diameter).
- **A variety of specialised cells (scene 8; the content point's "a variety").** Onion epidermis (about 300 µm long), cheek cell (about 56 µm), red blood cell (about 8 µm, "measured the same way at higher power"), nerve cell (over 1 m). One function phrase each, said plainly: the red blood cell is small enough to squeeze through the narrowest blood vessels (capillaries; screen tag); a nerve cell carries signals from the spine to the toes. The claim is kept modest ("tends to match the cell's job"). Why shape and size suit exchange (surface area to volume) is a route later home (FA2 ratio and exchange videos) and is not taught.
- **Sense check (scene 10; site outline answer step 5).** The site says "most plant and animal cells are between about 10 and 100 micrometres across", which its own onion (200 to 300 µm) and red blood cell (8 µm) values fall outside. The video says "from a few micrometres to a few hundred" (consistent with all four cells shown) and keeps the site's two diagnostic flags: below 1 µm suggests the magnification was used twice; millimetres suggests a missed conversion.
- **Memory aid (author-written).** "**Ten times the zoom, a tenth of the view.**" Shown after scene 6 explains why the field narrows, then large on the notes screen with the second line "Field ÷ count = one cell." Assumptions checked under "Memory aid check". "Zoom" stands for total magnification; the narration and cards say "magnification" throughout.

### 2. two ideas: the field of view and the micrometre
> Two ideas first. The field of view is the circle of the specimen you can see through the microscope, and its **diameter** is the distance straight across the middle. Cells are measured in micrometres. A micrometre is one thousandth of a millimetre, so one millimetre is a thousand **micrometres**, written µm. To turn millimetres into micrometres, multiply by a **thousand**.

### 5. step 3: calculate the cell size
> Step three: share the field between the cells. The equation is: cell size equals the diameter of the field of view, divided by the number of cells **across** it. So four thousand five hundred micrometres divided by fifteen is three hundred **micrometres**. Notice there's no magnification in this step. The field was measured in real millimetres, so dividing by the magnification as well would make the cell far too **small**.

### 6. higher power, narrower field (a quick prediction)
> Cheek cells are much smaller, so you'd look at them at times four hundred. Quick prediction: that's ten times the magnification of times forty, so what happens to the field of view? It gets ten times **narrower**. You're seeing a smaller patch, spread over the same circle. So the field is four thousand five hundred divided by ten, which is four hundred and fifty **micrometres**. In general, multiply the low power field by the low magnification, then divide by the high **magnification**.

### 13. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Field of view: the circle of specimen you can see. 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometre: one thousandth of a millimetre).
2. Measure the field diameter at low power with a mini-grid (1 mm squares), for example 4.5 mm = 4500 µm at ×40. The grid is magnified like a specimen, so it reads real lengths.
3. At higher power: field = low power field × low magnification ÷ high magnification (×400: 4500 × 40 ÷ 400 = 450 µm).
4. Cell size = diameter of field of view ÷ number of cells across the field of view (onion: 4500 ÷ 15 = 300 µm; cheek: 450 ÷ 8 ≈ 56 µm). Count cell widths end to end across the diameter, repeat in several fields and take the mean; give the unit.
5. From a magnified image only: real size = image size ÷ magnification (divide, don't multiply). Sense check: an answer below 1 µm usually means the magnification was used twice.

Work out the field at higher power (ten times the magnification, a tenth of the view), size an onion epidermis cell and a cheek cell in micrometres, and compare a variety of specialised cells from red blood cells to nerve cells. Then check a result with image size ÷ magnification, and see why you divide, never multiply. Includes two exam-style questions with brief feedback. All questions are written for HSCScience in the HSC style; they are not official NESA questions. Data are illustrative.
