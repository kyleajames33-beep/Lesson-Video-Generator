Author notes (not learner copy):
(a) Acid test: typically 1 mol L⁻¹ hydrochloric acid (or 0.5 to 2 mol L⁻¹, as the school's risk assessment sets), small pieces of similar mass or surface area; concentration not stated in learner copy ("dilute"). Iron reacts slowly (Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂); lead reacts very slowly (insoluble PbCl₂ coating) and is not used by students.
(b) Water: K and Na with cold water are teacher demonstrations behind a screen or footage (site; m1-v15); calcium with cold water is commonly a teacher demonstration (Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂; the water turns slightly cloudy); magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water, faster with hot water, vigorously with steam (Mg + H₂O(g) → MgO + H₂). Science check to confirm the calcium wording against current NSW school guidance.
(c) Oxygen: magnesium ribbon burns with a brilliant white light (never look straight at it, m3-v04); iron filings sprinkled into a Bunsen flame burn as sparks (a common teacher demonstration; the product is mainly Fe₃O₄, not stated); copper blackens without flame (2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO, m3-v04). Zinc powder burns but is not shown.
(d) Displacement grid (expected results): Mg displaces Zn, Fe, Cu (3); Zn displaces Fe, Cu (2); Fe displaces Cu (1); Cu displaces none (0). Iron(II) sulfate solution oxidises in air (yellow-brown); use freshly made solution (not stated in learner copy). Magnesium in some salt solutions also gives a little hydrogen; the deposit is the observation.
(e) Aluminium: the oxide layer (Al₂O₃) is a few nanometres thick and re-forms quickly; in dilute HCl the reaction starts slowly, then speeds up as chloride attacks the layer. m3-v08 owns "surface passivation against underlying tendency" (route).
(f) Secondary source: the standard series is consistent with reduction potentials (site note 1). Calcium and sodium swap in some lists depending on the measure; the school series puts Na above Ca (site), so the video follows it.
(g) A2: Cu + 2Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Ag; the solution turns pale blue slowly; silver grows as grey or shiny crystals. Silver nitrate stains skin (m2-v15's control).
(h) Pronunciations: "zinc two plus", "copper two plus" spoken once each; "X", "Y", "Z" as letters.

## Script and shots

Cue words in **bold** trigger the visual change. Colour key as in Decisions.

### 1. hook: same acid, three metals
> Here are three beakers of the same dilute hydrochloric acid, the same volume, at the same temperature. Drop a clean piece of magnesium into one, and it fizzes hard. Zinc fizzes steadily. Copper does **nothing**. Same acid, so the difference has to come from the metals. How readily a metal reacts is called its reactivity, and observations like these are how chemists put metals in **order**.

Visual: opens on the self-contained frame. On "magnesium" the first beaker cross-fades to `m5x-strong-acid-mg-v1.png`; on "Zinc" the second to `m6-zinc-acid-v1.png`; on "nothing" the third to `m6-copper-acid-v1.png`. Bubble meters rise beside each: tall, medium, zero. Gloss chip "reactivity: how readily a metal reacts". On "order" a ranking strip writes at the right: "acid: Mg > Zn > Cu" (Cu tile grey, tag "no reaction").

### 2. making it fair
> For that order to mean anything, the test has to be fair. Use the same acid concentration and volume, the same temperature, and pieces of about the same size. Rub each metal with sandpaper first, because many metals carry a thin coating of oxide that slows the reaction down. Then the only thing that changes is the **metal**.

Visual: a fair-test card writes beside the beakers: "same: acid concentration, volume, temperature, piece size; surfaces cleaned"; gloss chip "fair test: only one thing changes". On "sandpaper" a small inset shows a dull grey metal surface with a thin coded oxide skin being rubbed away to bright metal (abstract; no hand, no paper plate). On "changes is the metal" the card's "changes: the metal" line lights teal.

### 3. what the bubbles are
> So what's the gas? A metal that reacts with an acid makes a salt and hydrogen. Magnesium plus two hydrochloric acid gives magnesium chloride plus hydrogen. Hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop, and your teacher does that test on a small sample, because hydrogen is flammable. Use dilute acid, small amounts, and safety glasses, in a well-ventilated **room**.

Visual: line "Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)" with atom tiles and a green tally (m3-v02 style); tag "metal + acid → salt + hydrogen". `m6-splint-pop-v1.png` small (grey tag "teacher does the pop test"). On "room" a safety strip: "dilute acid; small amounts; safety glasses; ventilated room; hydrogen is flammable" with `m5x-safety-glasses-v1.png` small.

### 4. iron, and a surprise
> Add iron to the line-up, and it bubbles slowly. Aluminium foil is a surprise. At first it hardly bubbles at all, so keep that in mind. From the acid test, the order is magnesium, zinc, iron, and copper last, because copper makes no **hydrogen**.

Visual: two more beakers join the row: `y11c-iron-acid-v1.png` (bubble meter low) and `y11c-aluminium-acid-v1.png` (bubble meter very low; an amber "?" tag "aluminium: slow start"). On "hydrogen" the acid strip rewrites: "acid: Mg > Zn > Fe > Cu" with Al parked to one side under an amber "?" tile.

### 5. water
> Water is a gentler test. Potassium and sodium react so violently with cold water that your teacher shows them behind a safety screen, or on video, like in the alkali metals video. Calcium sinks and bubbles steadily. Magnesium barely reacts with cold water, though it does react with steam. These reactions make the metal hydroxide and hydrogen. So from water, it's potassium, sodium, calcium, then **magnesium**.

Visual: the acid beakers shrink to a row at the top. The trough set from m1-v15 at one scale: `y11c-trough-k-v1.png` and `y11c-trough-na-v1.png` behind `y11c-safety-screen-v1.png` (grey tag "teacher demonstration or footage"); `y11c-trough-ca-v1.png` (new; a grey granule on the bottom with a steady bubble stream); `y11c-trough-mg-v1.png` (new; a ribbon on the bottom with a few tiny bubbles; tag "faster with hot water or steam"). Lines small: "2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)", "Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)"; tag "metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen". On "magnesium" the strip writes: "water: K > Na > Ca > Mg".

### 6. oxygen
> Oxygen gives a third ranking. Magnesium burns with a brilliant white light. Iron filings sprinkled into a flame burn as bright **sparks**. Copper doesn't burn at all. It just turns black on the surface, as you saw with combustion. So from oxygen, it's magnesium, iron, then **copper**.

Visual: three plates in a row: `c1-burned-magnesium-v1.png` with the coded glare from m3-v04 (tag "never look straight at it"); on "sparks" `y11c-flame-iron-sparks-v1.png` (new; aligned with `m8-flame-blue-v2.png`); `y11c-copper-foil-black-v1.png` (requested in m3-v04). On "copper" the strip writes: "oxygen: Mg > Fe > Cu".

### 7. metal ions: displacement
> The fourth test is the most useful. Put zinc into blue copper sulfate solution. The blue fades, and brown copper coats the **zinc**. Zinc has gone into the solution as zinc ions, and copper ions have come out as copper metal. Now try copper in zinc sulfate solution. Nothing **happens**. A more reactive metal pushes a less reactive metal out of its compound, but never the other way round. That's a displacement reaction.

Visual: board clears to `y11c-cuso4-beaker-zinc-v1.png` (new: blue solution, grey zinc strip); on "coats the zinc" it cross-fades to `y11c-cuso4-beaker-zinc-after-v1.png` (paler blue, the strip coated brown, flakes below). The ion lens opens on the strip's surface: Zn atoms leave as amber Zn²⁺; blue Cu²⁺ arrive and settle as brown Cu atoms. On "copper in zinc sulfate" `y11c-znso4-beaker-copper-v1.png` (new: colourless solution, a copper strip, unchanged) slides in beside; on "happens" a tag "no change" and a chip "mixing always gives a reaction" fades in and is crossed. Gloss chip "displacement reaction: a more reactive metal pushes a less reactive metal out of its compound".

### 8. the equation
> As an equation, zinc plus copper sulfate gives zinc sulfate plus copper. The sulfate ions are spectators, so the net ionic equation is zinc plus copper two plus ions gives zinc two plus ions plus **copper**. Whichever metal ends up as ions is the more reactive one.

Visual: line "Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)"; the full ionic line flies down (m3-v03's token flight) and the SO₄²⁻ pair is struck grey. On "ions plus copper" line 3: "Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)". On "ends up as ions" a pen ring circles "Zn²⁺(aq)" with the tag "more reactive"; the strip writes: "metal ions: Zn > Cu".

### 9. prediction: the displacement grid (countdown, 3 seconds)
> Here's a quick way to rank several metals at once. On a spotting plate, put small pieces of magnesium, zinc, iron and copper into drops of each other's sulfate solutions, and count how many each one reacts with. Quick prediction. How many will copper react **with**?

Visual: `p03-well-plate-white-tile-v1.png` with the coded 4 × 4 block (rows "Mg, Zn, Fe, Cu"; columns "MgSO₄, ZnSO₄, FeSO₄, CuSO₄"; diagonal greyed "same metal"); a tally column "reactions" empty. Grey tag "small volumes; waste as your teacher directs". On "copper react with" the 3 s `Countdown` ring runs over the Cu row.

### 10. counting the grid
> None. Copper can't displace any of them. Iron reacts with one, copper sulfate. Zinc reacts with two, and magnesium reacts with all three. The more solutions a metal reacts with, the more reactive it is. So it's magnesium, zinc, iron, then **copper**.

Visual: the grid fills row by row: Cu row stays clear (tally "0"); Fe row deposits in the CuSO₄ well (tally "1"); Zn row in FeSO₄ and CuSO₄ (tally "2"); Mg row in three wells (tally "3"). On "then copper" the strip writes: "metal ions: Mg > Zn > Fe > Cu".

### 11. one series
> Now put all four tests together. They never disagree, so they combine into one series, called an activity series: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, zinc, iron, copper. And the acid test tells you where hydrogen goes. Every metal that made hydrogen sits above it, and copper sits **below**.

Visual: the series merge: the four strips slide together; shared metals line up; the merged strip writes vertically: "K, Na, Ca, Mg, Zn, Fe, Cu" (teal outlines, tag "our data"); gloss chip "activity series: metals from most to least reactive". On "below" a dashed teal "H" line drops between Fe and Cu (tag "made H₂ with acid: above; didn't: below").

### 12. compare with the reference
> Now compare that with a standard series from a data book, a secondary source. It goes potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, hydrogen, copper, silver, gold. Our order matches. We didn't test lead, silver or gold, because lead is toxic and the others are expensive, so they come from the reference. But aluminium sits above zinc, even though our foil hardly **reacted**. Aluminium is covered by a thin, tough oxide layer that keeps the acid off. Once that layer breaks down, aluminium reacts quickly. So the mismatch came from the surface, not the **metal**.

Visual: the reference compare: the reference series slides in beside ours (grey outlines; gloss chip "secondary source: data collected by others, such as a data book"); matching metals join with ticks. Pb, Ag and Au tiles drop into our strip with grey "from the reference" tags. On "reacted" Al's tile flashes amber with a link to the parked "?" tile; a chip "aluminium is unreactive" fades in and is crossed. ### 13. a way to remember the order

### 19. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Reactivity: how readily a metal reacts. Compare fairly: same reagent, concentration, volume, temperature, piece size; clean surfaces.
2. Metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen (squeaky pop). Metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. Metal + oxygen → metal oxide.
3. Displacement: a metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its ions. Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). The anion is a spectator.
4. Activity series: K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au. Metals above H give H₂ with dilute acid. Aluminium looks less reactive because of its oxide layer.
