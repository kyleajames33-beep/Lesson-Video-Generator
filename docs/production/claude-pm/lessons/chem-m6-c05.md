### 2. inside the two beakers
> Zoom in. In the hydrochloric acid, **every** hydrogen chloride molecule has given its proton to a water molecule, making hydronium ions and chloride ions. In the acetic acid, most molecules are still **whole**. At any moment, only about **one molecule in eighty** has given its proton away. Hydronium ions are what make a solution acidic, so the acetic acid has far **fewer** of them.

Visual: the plates shrink to the top corners (labels and pH chips kept, dimmed). Two particle lenses open beneath them, the same size and the same number of acid particles drawn (about 40 each). Left lens on "every": each HCl releases its teal token onto a neighbouring water, giving hydronium tokens and grey Cl⁻; a counter under the lens reads "split: all". Right lens on "whole": amber-outlined acetic acid molecules stay intact; on "one molecule in eighty" a single molecule hops its token to a water (the drawn count is illustrative; counter reads "split: about 1 in 80"). On "fewer" both lenses' hydronium tokens pulse; left many, right one. Hold about 1 second.

### 3. strong and weak, and what pH shows
> That difference is called **strength**. A **strong acid** ionises completely in water, which means every molecule gives its proton away and forms ions. A **weak acid** ionises only partly. The pH meter sees this. A lower pH means more hydronium ions, and each step of one on the pH scale is **ten times** as many. Two steps would be ten times ten, a **hundred** times. The gap here is almost two steps, so the hydrochloric acid has about **eighty times** as many hydronium ions.

Visual: the lenses stay. On "strong acid" a tag writes under the left lens: "strong acid: ionises completely"; on "weak acid" under the right: "weak acid: ionises partly" (the counters fade). On "lower pH" a short horizontal pH scale draws across the bottom (0 to 14, below 7 tinted acid, 7 marked neutral); the two chips "1.0" and "2.9" drop onto it, and the tags in the lenses dim. On "ten times" the step from 1 to 2 gets a bracket "× 10". On "hundred" the step from 2 to 3 gets a second "× 10" and the two merge into one bracket "× 10 × 10 = × 100" across 1 to 3. On "eighty times" that bracket dims and a bracket from 1.0 to 2.9 reads "about 80 × more H₃O⁺". Bright at the end: the two chips and the 80 × bracket. Hold about 1 second.

### 4. why the weak acid stops early
> Why does acetic acid stop at so few? Its reaction runs **both ways**. Acetic acid molecules give protons to water, and acetate ions take protons **back** from hydronium ions. Very soon both happen at the same rate, so you have a **dynamic equilibrium**, the balance you met in Module 5, with most molecules still whole. Chloride ions almost never take a proton back, so hydrogen chloride goes **all the way**. A later video explains why.

Visual: the left lens and the pH scale dim; the right lens enlarges. On "both ways" one acetic acid molecule hops its teal token to a water (amber arrow), and on "back" a different acetate pulls a token off a hydronium (violet arrow); the two hops repeat at matched pace in different places, and a small balance icon levels with the tag "forward rate = reverse rate". On "dynamic equilibrium" the tag "dynamic equilibrium (Module 5)" writes and the balance tag dims. On "all the way" the left lens returns small: a chloride sits next to a hydronium and nothing hops; tag "Cl⁻: no pull back". Hold about 1 second.

### 5. the arrow says it
> The equation has to say this. For a strong acid, write a **single arrow**: the reaction goes to completion. For a weak acid, write the **double arrow**, because the forward and reverse reactions are both going on. Writing a double arrow for hydrochloric acid would say some molecules stay whole, and that's **wrong**.

Visual: the lenses shrink to thumbnails at the left. The equation strip writes: "HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)" with tag "strong: complete"; on "single arrow" the → pulses teal. Second strip: "CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)" with tag "weak: equilibrium"; on "double arrow" the ⇌ pulses. The first strip's tag dims as the second strip writes, and the second tag dims on "wrong", so the bright items are the two strips and the crossed chip. On "wrong" a chip "HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ ..." fades in under the first strip and is crossed (caption "HCl ionises completely: →"). Hold about 1 second.

A handy line is **So I Brought No Clean Clothes**, and each word gives the key letters of one acid, in order: **sulfuric**, for its first proton, then hydroiodic, hydrobromic, nitric, hydrochloric and perchloric. Treat other acids as weak unless a question tells you otherwise. And watch **hydrofluoric acid**: it looks like hydrochloric acid, but it's weak.

### 7. concentration is a separate dial
> Now the second idea. **Concentration** is how much acid is dissolved in each litre, in moles per litre. It's a separate dial from strength. Add water to hydrochloric acid, and it becomes **dilute**, but every molecule still ionises, so it's a **dilute strong acid**, never a weak one. **Dilute acetic acid**, and a slightly bigger fraction of its molecules ionise, but nowhere near all of them, so it's still a weak acid. That gives **four combinations**: concentrated or dilute, and strong or weak.

### 8. a concentrated weak acid can win
> Here's the surprise. One mole per litre acetic acid has a pH of about **two point four**. Zero point zero zero one zero mole per litre hydrochloric acid has a pH of **three point zero**. To compare strength fairly, test acids at the **same concentration**. And weak doesn't mean **safe**: concentrated acetic acid still burns skin.

### 9. strong and weak bases
> Bases work the same way. A **strong base** such as sodium hydroxide is an ionic solid. When it dissolves, its ions **separate completely**, so every formula unit gives a hydroxide ion. That's called **dissociation**. Ammonia is a **weak base**: as you saw in the models video, only some ammonia molecules take a proton from water. At zero point one zero moles per litre, sodium hydroxide reads pH **thirteen**, and ammonia only about **eleven**. Calcium, strontium and barium hydroxide are strong too, and each formula unit gives **two** hydroxide ions.

### 10. low solubility is not weakness
> Limewater is a saturated calcium hydroxide solution, which means it holds as much as can dissolve. Its pH is lower than that sodium hydroxide solution's. So is calcium hydroxide weak? No. Less than two grams of it **dissolves** in a litre, and the rest sits at the bottom. But every bit that does dissolve splits completely into ions. That's **low solubility**, not weakness. How much dissolves and how completely it splits up are different questions.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Strength: how completely an acid ionises (or a base dissociates or reacts) in water. Strong: completely, single arrow: HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). Weak: partly, a dynamic equilibrium, double arrow: CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq).
2. Concentration: moles dissolved per litre. A dilute strong acid is still strong. pH depends on both strength and concentration, so compare strength at the same concentration (0.10 mol/L: HCl pH 1.0, acetic acid pH 2.9).
3. Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, H₂SO₄ (first H⁺); treat others as weak (HF is weak). Strong bases: Group 1 hydroxides and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂ (two OH⁻ each). NH₃ is a weak base.
4. Low solubility is not weakness: little Ca(OH)₂ dissolves, but all that dissolves dissociates. Weak does not mean safe.

## Numbers to verify (all arithmetic, repeated for Codex)

Checked by hand (19 Oct); Codex to re-check. All at 25 °C, concentration (ideal-solution) model.

- 0.10 mol/L HCl: [H₃O⁺] = 0.10, pH = 1.00.
- 0.10 mol/L CH₃COOH, Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵: x² / (0.10 − x) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, x = 1.33 × 10⁻³, pH 2.88; from pH 2.9: 10^−2.9 = 1.26 × 10⁻³ mol/L, 1.26% ionised (1 in 79); ratio 0.10 ÷ 1.26 × 10⁻³ = 79 ≈ 80; 10^1.9 = 79.4.
- 1.0 mol/L CH₃COOH: x = 4.23 × 10⁻³, pH 2.37 ≈ 2.4; 0.0010 mol/L HCl pH 3.00; ratio 4.23 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 4.2 ≈ 4; acid ratio 1.0 ÷ 0.0010 = 1000.
- Weak-acid fractions for the grid (author note b): 1.0 mol/L 0.42%; 0.0010 mol/L: x² / (0.0010 − x) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, x = 1.255 × 10⁻⁴, 12.5%.
- 0.10 mol/L HNO₂, Ka 4.5 × 10⁻⁴: x = 6.49 × 10⁻³, pH 2.19 ≈ 2.2.
- 0.10 mol/L NaOH: pOH 1.00, pH 13.00. 0.0010 mol/L NaOH: pOH 3.00, pH 11.00. 0.10 mol/L NH₃, Kb 1.8 × 10⁻⁵: [OH⁻] = 1.33 × 10⁻³, pOH 2.88, pH 11.12 ≈ 11.1. Concentration ratio 0.10 ÷ 0.0010 = 100.
- Equations: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻; CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ (C 2/2, H 4 + 2 = 6 / 3 + 3 = 6, O 2 + 1 = 3 / 1 + 2 = 3, charge 0 = 0); HNO₃ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + NO₃⁻; HNO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + NO₂⁻; NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻; NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻; Ca(OH)₂ → Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ (charge 0 = +2 − 2).
