### 2. SDG 10: why inequality itself affects health
> SDG 10 is Reduced Inequalities, within and between countries. In plain words, its targets aim to raise the incomes of the poorest faster than the average, include everyone whatever their age, sex, disability, race or background, and remove discriminatory laws and practices. Why is inequality itself a health issue? Remember the **social gradient**: health improves at every step up the social ladder, not just above a poverty line. People in the middle aren't deprived, yet they're less healthy than those above them, so it isn't only poverty. Lower relative position brings less control and more chronic stress. Very unequal places tend to have weaker shared services and lower trust, which affects everyone. And **discrimination** acts on health through stress, through blocked access, and through services that make people not want to return. Finally, the worst-off group can improve while the gap still widens. SDG 10 says the gap itself matters.

### 3. SDG 11: how places produce health
> SDG 11 is Sustainable Cities and Communities: safe, inclusive, resilient places. It's the most directly health-shaping of the four, because it changes daily conditions without anyone deciding anything about their health. You traced some of these in Year 11. Housing first. Crowding spreads infection, cold and damp cause breathing problems, heat kills, insecurity causes chronic stress, and rent takes money from food and medicine. Transport decides who can reach work, school and care without a car. **Walkability**, being able to reach daily places on foot, produces more activity than any campaign telling people to exercise. Green space supports mental health and cuts heat. So the **built environment**, the human-made surroundings we live in, is one of the few health interventions that works while people aren't thinking about their health. And most of it is decided by local and state government.

### 4. one change through four goals, whole and partial
> Now the proof that the goals lock together. Take one change: affordable, well-insulated housing, built near a school and a bus line. That's SDG 11 directly. It's SDG 3 too: less crowding and infection, less heat and cold, more walking, less stress, and the clinic is on the bus line. It's SDG 4: a stable home near school means fewer school changes, better attendance, and a warm, quiet place to study. And it's SDG 10: lower living costs for low-income families, and less of the clustering that keeps disadvantage in place. Now do it partially. Build the same housing on the edge of town with no transport. The housing target is met, but people can't reach work, school, shops or the clinic without a car many don't have. That can be worse than not building there at all, because disadvantage is now concentrated and fixed in place for decades. Partial implementation isn't partial success.

### 5. where SDG 10 and 11 bite in Australia

For SDG 10, the gaps we've traced: for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, by income, by remoteness, and for people with disability, plus a gradient that runs through almost every health measure. For SDG 11, housing that's unaffordable or insecure, with cheaper housing often furthest from services. Car dependence in outer suburbs and regional towns, which cuts daily activity. ### 6. a method for thinking about a community
> So how do you use the four goals on a real community? In Year 11 you learned to start from a local need, not from the goals, and you built a strategy checklist. Mapping each cause to a goal answers one of its lines: who acts outside health? Its other lines still apply: who it misses, whether the gap narrows, and how you'd measure, by group. These goals add one new line. What does each action depend on? Remember the housing with no bus. Used this way, the goals are a checklist against narrowness. They stop a health plan becoming a list of health services, when most of what decides health is housing, income, education and the street outside.
