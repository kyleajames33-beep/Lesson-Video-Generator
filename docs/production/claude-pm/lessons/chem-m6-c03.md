### 2. why it gets warm (spectators and water)
> First, why does it warm up? In water, hydrochloric acid is completely split into **hydrogen ions** and chloride ions, and sodium hydroxide into sodium ions and **hydroxide ions**. The sodium and chloride ions don't change at all, so they're called **spectator ions**. The only change is a hydrogen ion joining a hydroxide ion to make **water**. The water has less chemical energy than the two ions had, so the difference is **released as heat**, warming the solution.

Visual: the board clears to a large particle lens of the mixed solution. ### 3. exothermic, negative, per mole of water
> A reaction that releases heat is **exothermic**. The chemicals lose energy, so the enthalpy change, delta H, is **negative**. Twice as much of each solution would make twice as much water, and twice as much heat. So to compare experiments, divide by the water formed. The **enthalpy of neutralisation** is the enthalpy change per **mole of water** formed.

### 5. the calorimeter and q = mcΔT
> To measure it, mix the solutions in a lidded polystyrene foam cup, called a **calorimeter**. Foam insulates, so we assume all the heat stays in the solution. Then the heat is q equals m c delta T, from Year 11. **m** is the mass of all the solution, both together, and one millilitre of a dilute solution is about one gram. **c**, the specific heat capacity, is four point one eight joules per gram per degree. **Delta T** is the final minus the initial temperature.

### 7. worked example 1, step 1: the heat

### 8. worked example 1, step 2: the moles of water

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Enthalpy of neutralisation: the enthalpy change per mole of water formed. Exothermic, so ΔH is negative: about −57 kJ/mol for a strong acid with a base fully split into ions (only H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) happens; the other ions are spectators).
2. q = mcΔT: m = mass of all the solution (1 mL ≈ 1 g), c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹, ΔT = T(final) − T(initial).
3. n = cV for H⁺ and OH⁻ (H₂SO₄ gives 2 H⁺). The one that runs out first sets n(H₂O). ΔH = −q ÷ n(H₂O), in kJ/mol, rounded to the data's significant figures.
4. Example: 50.0 mL each of 1.00 mol/L HCl and NaOH, 21.5 → 28.0 °C: q = 2717 J, n(H₂O) = 0.0500 mol, ΔH = −54 kJ/mol (less negative than −57, mainly heat loss).

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the solution warms: H⁺ + OH⁻ forms water with less chemical energy, and the difference heats the solution (2). Why ΔH is negative: the chemicals give heat away (3, 9). Why per mole of water: heat scales with the amount reacting (3, feedback 2). Why about −57 whichever pair: spectators differ, the net reaction does not (4). Why the total mass: all the solution is warmed, including leftover reactant (7, 11). Why the smaller amount sets the water: the other ion has nothing left to react with, and unreacted ions release no heat (10). Why the result is less negative than −57: heat escapes, so ΔT is too small (9). Why doubling volumes leaves ΔT unchanged: twice the heat into twice the mass (feedback 2).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** mass of one solution (7); ΔH positive for a temperature rise (9, 12); dividing by moles of acid (11); dividing by the excess reactant (feedback 1); −57 for every acid and base (4); bigger experiment, bigger rise (feedback 2).
- **Worked-example rule:** task line, equation strip and grouped givens are separate; one stage per line; results appear on the word that says them; only the q and n chips carry forward into step 3; scene 10 and 11 clear the first example entirely. At most three bright text items.
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen HSC-style tasks with every condition visible (all volumes, concentrations, temperatures and both assumptions in A1; the original conditions and the change in A2), 10 seconds each with the invitation to pause, nothing answer-bearing. One crossed mistake each; feedback about 30 and 27 seconds at the measured rate.
- **Avoided double teaching:** q = mcΔT, ΔH and n = cV are Year 11 callbacks with one-line meanings; H₃O⁺ is a tag (C01); acid + hydroxide → salt + water is used, not taught (C02).
- **Out of scope by design:** practical method, risk and error table (chem-m6-p02); weak-acid enthalpy differences (chem-m6-c09); strong and weak as terms (chem-m6-c05); applications (chem-m6-c04).
