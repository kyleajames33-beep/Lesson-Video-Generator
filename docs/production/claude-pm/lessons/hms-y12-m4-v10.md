### 2. skill goes early, and judge it by next week
> Skill work goes early, after the warm-up and before heavy conditioning, and the reason isn't tradition. Learning a movement depends on the quality of each repetition, and quality falls with fatigue. A tired athlete moves slower and less precisely, so late practice can rehearse the compensations fatigue causes, not the technique you want. And a new skill needs most of the athlete's attention, which is limited. In Year 11 you met practice types, and the key finding: practice that looks worse in training often works better in games. So **skill instruction**, explaining, showing and correcting, is judged by what the athlete can do next week under pressure, not by how the drill looked today.

### 3. conditioning, and what its placement costs
> **Conditioning** builds the fitness the sport needs, with the methods and principles from earlier. Inside a session, the question is where it goes, and what that costs. Put it first, and conditioning gets the fresh athlete, but the skill and tactics after it are done tired and learned poorly. That's only worth it when the conditioning itself must be maximal, like top-speed sprinting. Put it last, and skill and tactics get the fresh athlete, while conditioning is done a bit tired. That's the default. Or build it into the skill work, so the athlete practises the skill as tired as a game makes them. A netballer who shoots well rested but misses in the last quarter needs exactly that, but only once the technique is stable. And with a squad, the same conditioning is too easy for some and too hard for others. So keep the structure, and vary the dose.

### 4. strategies and tactics, trained by decisions
> A **strategy** is the overall plan for a contest. A **tactic** is a specific action that carries it out in a given moment. A tennis player's strategy might be attacking the opponent's second serve. The tactic is stepping inside the baseline on that point. Students lose marks by mixing them up. "Our tactic was to play possession all match" describes a strategy. And tactics are trained by decisions, not explanations. A coach can describe a defensive structure in two minutes, but players still can't do it, because the hard part is spotting the cue under pressure and choosing. So tactical blocks use **constraints**: a smaller space, an overload like four against two, points only for a certain move, or a limit on touches, so the decision keeps happening. For one athlete, tactics are rehearsed alone against a model of the opponent. For a team, they need enough players and space, and shared communication.

### 5. athlete reflection and coach evaluation
> The session closes with **athlete reflection** and **coach evaluation**, and they're not the same job. Reflection asks: what happened for me, why, and what will I change? It uses inside information no observer has, how the movement felt, and it builds ownership. It fails when it becomes a ritual, or is just about whether they won. Evaluation asks: did the session achieve its aim, and what should the next one contain? It uses outside evidence, like observation, data and whether the target behaviour happened. It fails when a session is judged by how busy it looked or how hard it felt. That's why v-nine's goal-specific aim matters. Evaluation is a comparison against the aim. One athlete can reflect and be evaluated continuously. A squad needs a deliberate structure, or the quieter players never say anything.
