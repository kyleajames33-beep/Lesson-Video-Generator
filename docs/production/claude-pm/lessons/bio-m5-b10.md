### 1. hook (all the white-eyed flies were male)
> Remember the white-eyed fruit fly that hatched in Morgan's lab in 1910? He crossed it with red-eyed females, and all their offspring had red eyes. So white was recessive. Then he bred those offspring with each other, and white eyes came back, as expected. But there was a twist. Every single white-eyed fly was male. Why would eye colour care whether a fly is male or female?

### 2. sex chromosomes
> The answer is in the sex chromosomes. In fruit flies, as in humans, females have two X chromosomes, and males have one X and one Y. The eye colour gene is on the X, and the Y doesn't carry it. So a female has two copies of the gene, but a male has only one. If that one copy is the white allele, there's no other allele to hide it, so his eyes are white. A gene on a sex chromosome is called **sex-linked**.

Visual: B9's karyotype strip returns small with the X and Y pair now highlighted (tag "sex chromosomes"; grey tag "B9: autosomes"). Two coded panels draw: "female: XX" (two X chips) and "male: XY" (an X chip and a shorter Y chip). On "two copies" the female's X chips take tags "R" and "r" (shown as X^R X^r, tag "red eyes: r is hidden"). On "only one" the male's X takes "r" (X^r Y) and on "no other allele to hide it" a dashed amber ring circles the empty Y slot; `m5x-fly-white-eye-v1.png` slides in beside him. That matches what Morgan saw: no white-eyed daughters, and close to half the sons white-eyed.

### 4. people: haemophilia
> People work the same way. Haemophilia A, where blood doesn't clot properly, is caused by a recessive allele on the X. A carrier mother and an unaffected father have a one in four chance, for each child, of an affected son. But for each son, the chance is one in two. So read the question: all children, or sons? No daughter of theirs is affected, because their father's X carries the normal allele. That's why conditions like this are far more common in males: a male needs one copy, but a female needs two.

### 5. the X-linked pedigree pattern
> In a pedigree, X-linked recessive traits leave a pattern: mostly males are affected, the allele passes through unaffected carrier mothers, and it never passes from father to son. But it doesn't only come from mothers. An affected father passes his X to every daughter, so all his daughters are carriers. And one quick test rules it out: an affected daughter with an unaffected father. That's the family from the last video, so that condition wasn't X-linked recessive.

### 7. incomplete dominance: snapdragons
> Snapdragons show the in-between kind. Cross a red one with a white one, and every offspring is pink. The red allele codes for an enzyme that makes red pigment. The white allele's enzyme doesn't work, like the white snapdragons in the proteins video. One working copy makes less pigment, so the flowers are pink. That's **incomplete dominance**. Cross two pinks, and you get 1 red to 2 pink to 1 white. Red and white come back, so the alleles never blended. Only the colour did.

Visual: row 2 fills: `m5x-snapdragon-red-v1.png` "CR CR", `m5x-snapdragon-pink-v1.png` "CR CW", `m5x-snapdragon-white-v1.png` "CW CW" (a grey tag: "neither allele dominant: one capital letter, two superscripts"). ### 8. co-dominance: roan cattle
> Cattle show the other kind. Cross a red bull with a white cow, and the calves are **roan**. Look closely at a roan coat. It isn't pink. Each hair is either fully red or fully white, mixed together. Both alleles are fully expressed, side by side. That's **co-dominance**. Two roans give 1 red to 2 roan to 1 white, the same ratio as the snapdragons, because in both cases the heterozygote looks different from both homozygotes.

### 9. multiple alleles: ABO blood groups
> Your ABO blood group adds one more twist. In the population, this gene has three alleles, not two. That's **multiple alleles**. But each person still carries only two of them. One allele's enzyme puts A antigens, small sugar markers, on your red blood cells. Another puts B antigens on. The third, small i, makes an enzyme that doesn't work, so it adds neither. A and B are co-dominant: with both, you're type AB. Both are dominant over small i, so type O means two small i alleles.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (17 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Griffiths et al., Introduction to Genetic Analysis), Morgan (1910) and the sources named. Every square and ratio below is also produced at build time by the ported cross function (with `bySex`), and pedigree P4 is checked by `fitsMode`.
- **Morgan 1910.** White-eyed male x red-eyed females gave a red-eyed F1 (1,237 flies, plus 3 white-eyed males that Morgan attributed to further mutation; textbooks report the F1 as all red, and so does the hook; author note). F1 x F1 gave 2,459 red-eyed females, 1,011 red-eyed males and 782 white-eyed males; no white-eyed females in that cross (Morgan, Science 32:120, 1910). The overall red to white ratio was about 4.4 : 1, not 3 : 1 (white-eyed flies survive less well); the video does not claim 3 : 1 for these counts. "Every single white-eyed fly was male": correct for this cross. Morgan obtained white-eyed females in later crosses (white male x F1 female); author note.
- **Sex chromosomes.** Drosophila and human females XX, males XY. The *white* gene is on the Drosophila X with no Y copy; F8 (haemophilia A) and the red and green opsin genes are on the human X. Correct. (Fly sex is set by the X to autosome ratio, not the Y; not spoken.)
- **Morgan cross on the square.** F1 female X^R X^r x F1 male X^R Y: daughters X^R X^R, X^R X^r (all red); sons X^R Y (red), X^r Y (white). Daughters all red; sons 1 : 1. Correct.
- **Haemophilia A.** X-linked recessive, about 1 in 5,000 male births (not spoken). X^H X^h x X^H Y: X^H X^H, X^H X^h, X^H Y, X^h Y. Affected son 1/4 of all children (assuming equal chances of a son or daughter), 1/2 of sons; carrier daughter 1/4 of all children, 1/2 of daughters; no affected daughters. Correct (site short answer 3).
- **Male excess.** Allele frequency q gives affected males about q and affected females about q squared under random mating; q = 1/100 gives 1/100 males and 1/10,000 females. Correct (site L15 aside; pack check).
- **Pedigree P4 (scene 5, X-linked recessive).** I-1 affected male x I-2 unaffected female (X^H X^H); II-1 daughter carrier, II-2 son unaffected, II-3 daughter carrier; II-1 x II-4 (unaffected male X^H Y); III-1 affected son, III-2 unaffected daughter, III-3 affected son. XR fit: I-1 X^h Y, I-2 X^H X^H, II-1 and II-3 X^H X^h (all daughters of an affected father), II-2 X^H Y (his X from his mother), II-4 X^H Y, III-1 and III-3 X^h Y, III-2 X^H X^H or X^H X^h. Consistent; no father-to-son transmission; affected males only. Correct. (P4 would also fit autosomal recessive with II-4 a carrier, which is why scene 5 calls it a pattern to look for, and teaches the one decisive exclusion. G02 handles mode testing.)
- **B9 pedigree P1 recalled.** III-1 is an affected daughter with an unaffected father (II-4); under X-linked recessive she would need X^h from her father, who would then be affected. So P1 is not X-linked recessive. Correct.
- **Father to son.** A father passes his Y to sons, so X-linked alleles never pass father to son. An affected father passes his X to every daughter. Correct (bank insight).
- **Snapdragon incomplete dominance.** *Antirrhinum majus* red x white gives pink F1; pink x pink gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. The heterozygote has one functional allele for an anthocyanin pathway enzyme and makes less pigment (standard textbook explanation, Campbell). "One working copy makes less pigment": correct as stated (not "half", which overstates precision). B7's attempt 2 framed white snapdragons as two copies of an allele with a non-working pigment enzyme; consistent.
- **Roan cattle co-dominance.** Shorthorn red x white gives roan, a coat of intermixed red and white hairs; roan x roan gives 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white. Standard textbook example of co-dominance (each hair is red or white). Correct. (Some texts call roan incomplete dominance at the level of the whole coat; HSC and the site use co-dominance. Author note.)
- **ABO.** Gene *ABO* on chromosome 9; three main alleles IA, IB, i (O). IA encodes a glycosyltransferase that adds N-acetylgalactosamine (A antigen), IB one that adds galactose (B antigen); the common O allele has a frameshift and makes a non-working enzyme, so no A or B sugar is added. "Small sugar markers": correct. IA and IB co-dominant; both dominant over i. Six genotypes (IA IA, IA i, IB IB, IB i, IA IB, ii), four phenotypes. Correct.
- **IA i x IB i.** IA IB, IA i, IB i, ii: AB, A, B, O, each 1/4. Correct (site short answer 3).
- **Typing tile.** Not used in Revision 2.
- **Colour blindness item.** Red-green colour blindness: X-linked recessive. Woman's father X^b Y gives her X^b; she has normal vision, so X^B X^b. x X^B Y: as for haemophilia: 1/4 of all children a colour-blind son, 1/2 of sons, no colour-blind daughters, 1/2 of daughters carriers. Correct.
- **Fractions and percentages.** 1/4 = 25%, 1/2 = 50% throughout.

## Memory aid check

- **"Dad gives his X to daughters and his Y to sons."** (bank insight.) True for XY species with an X-carrying father (all mammals and Drosophila).
