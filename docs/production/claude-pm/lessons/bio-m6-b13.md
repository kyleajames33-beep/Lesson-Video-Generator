> Two genetic techniques. Bt cotton has been grown in Australian cotton fields for about thirty years. A gene drive mosquito, built to fight malaria, has never left a lab cage. How do you **weigh** the benefits of a biotechnology against its risks to biodiversity, and reach a judgement you can defend?

### 2. four questions for every case
> Listing pros and cons isn't weighing. To weigh, put each case through the same four questions, and answer each one with evidence from that case. How big is the benefit, and how strong is the evidence for it? Which level of biodiversity changes, and compared with what? A bigger harvest isn't a level. Can the change be undone if it goes wrong? And who gains, and who carries the risk? Then finish with a verdict that has a condition attached: when is it worth it?

### 3. Bt cotton through the questions
> Start with Bt cotton. The benefit is large and measured: together with better pest management, it's cut insecticide use on Australian cotton by about eighty-five per cent. Compared with sprayed cotton, that can leave more insect species alive in the field, a gain at the **species** level. Australia's native cotton species don't form fertile hybrids with it, so the gene is very unlikely to reach native plants. The main risk is caterpillars evolving resistance, a genetic change in a wild pest population, which growers slow with refuge crops. And if resistance did spread, growers could stop planting it, though they'd spray more again. Growers gain, and so do insects in and around the field.

### 4. the gene drive through the same questions

### 5. can it be undone?
> Notice which question pulls these two apart: can it be undone? A benefit you give up can usually be won back. Stop growing a crop, and you can choose something else next season. But an extinct species can't be brought back, and a drive that has spread through wild mosquitoes could be very hard to remove. So a gain you can reverse and a loss you can't aren't settled just by comparing their sizes. A risk that may be permanent needs much stronger evidence before you accept it.

1. (9a, explain, 3 + 2 marks.) Some mosquito-control programs release large numbers of male *Aedes* mosquitoes that carry a "self-limiting" gene. Female offspring that inherit the gene die before becoming adults. Male offspring that inherit it survive and carry one copy, which each passes to about half of his own offspring, as normal inheritance predicts. (a) Explain why the gene is expected to disappear from the wild population after releases stop. (b) Explain why stopping releases would not remove a gene drive from a population in the same way.
   Model: (a) Once releases stop, no new carriers enter the population. A surviving carrier son passes the gene to only about half of his offspring, so inheritance alone does not increase it. Every female who inherits the gene dies before reproducing, so only male carriers can pass it on, and each generation the carriers leave fewer carrier offspring than non-carriers do. Selection therefore lowers the gene's frequency every generation until it is lost. (b) A gene drive copies itself onto the other chromosome in the cells that make gametes, so a carrier passes it to nearly all of its offspring rather than half. Its frequency rises each generation without further releases, so stopping releases does not remove it. Classic mistake crossed out: "stopping releases stops a gene drive too". Other wrong chains named in the review: "the gene disappears because males die" (the sons survive; daughters die); "the gene mutates away"; "the gene drive also passes to half the offspring" (that is the rule a drive gets around, b11).
2. (9c, evaluate, 6 marks.) In a farming region, farmers grow about twenty different local wheat varieties that have been saved and replanted for generations. A genetically modified drought-tolerant wheat has given higher yields than the local varieties in dry-year field trials. A wild grass that can cross-pollinate with wheat grows along the field edges. It is proposed that the modified wheat replace the local varieties across the region. Evaluate this proposal, weighing its benefits against its effects on biodiversity.
   Model: Benefit: more reliable yields in drought years, supported by field trials (though trial results need confirming across seasons and farms), which supports food security for farmers and the region. Biodiversity, genetic level: replacing about twenty varieties with one narrows the crop's genetic diversity, so the whole region's wheat shares one set of weaknesses to a new disease or pest (the banana problem, b12); local varieties not kept elsewhere may be lost permanently. Gene flow: the tolerance gene could pass by pollen into the wild grass; if drought tolerance helps the grass survive, selection could spread it, changing the wild population's gene pool in a way that is hard to reverse. Reversibility and stakeholders: farmers gain yield but lose seed they could save if the new seed must be bought; the losses (varieties, wild gene pool) may be permanent while the yield gain could be pursued in other ways. Verdict: the proposal as stated (replacing all local varieties) is not justified; growing the modified wheat could be justified to a limited extent, provided local varieties are kept in production on some farms or stored in a seed bank, and the modified wheat is not planted where it can cross with the wild grass. Classic mistake crossed out: "there are pros and cons" (no verdict). Other wrong chains named in the review: "it yields more, so biodiversity improves" (yield is not a level, b12); "it is genetically modified, so it must be rejected" (the effect comes from replacing all varieties and from gene flow, not from the technique alone); a verdict with no condition.
