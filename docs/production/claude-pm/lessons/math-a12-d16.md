- **Anchor: filling a barrel-shaped vase from a tap at a steady rate (painted plates, live).** The vase is narrow at its base, widest in the middle and narrow again at its neck. Water flows in at a steady 0.75 L per minute, so the level rises fast where the vase is narrow and slowly where it is wide. The level-against-time graph therefore bends down while the vase widens (the rising rate is falling) and bends up while it narrows (the rising rate is rising), and it switches bend exactly at the widest part, where the level rises slowest. That is the lesson in one picture: concavity is about how the gradient changes, and an inflection is where the gradient stops falling and starts rising.

- **Learner question kept (route).** "How does the changing gradient reveal concavity and inflection?" The answer, built in order: on d15's join, the curved side has a rising gradient and the straight side a fixed one, so the bend comes from how the gradient changes (hook); the second derivative is the derivative of f′, the rate at which the gradient changes (2); where the gradient falls, the tangent turns clockwise and the curve bends down; where it rises, the tangent turns anticlockwise and the curve bends up; f″ is the gradient of f′, so its sign gives the bend (3); a point of inflection is where the bend switches, which is where f′ stops falling and starts rising, or the reverse (4); f″ = 0 only gives a candidate: the sign must change (5); at a flat point, a curve that bends down is a hilltop and one that bends up is a valley, because the curve sits below or above its flat tangent (6); if f″ = 0 there, the test gives no verdict, so fall back on the signs (7).

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the bend depends on the change in the gradient: the same gradient sits on a bending side and a straight side (1). Why f″ > 0 means concave up: the gradient rises, so the tangent turns anticlockwise and the curve bends up, staying above its tangents; f″ is the gradient of f′ (3). Why an inflection is where f′ turns: the gradient switches from falling to rising there (4). Why f″ = 0 is not enough: f″ can touch zero without changing sign, as for x⁴ (5). Why the second derivative test works: a curve bending down sits below its flat tangent, so the flat point is the highest nearby (6). Why the test fails at f″ = 0: three curves share f′(0) = f″(0) = 0 with three different outcomes (7).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** "f″ = 0 means an inflection" (5; 12, crossed); "f″ > 0 means a maximum" (8; 10, crossed); "f″ = 0 at a stationary point means a horizontal inflection" (7, refuted by x⁴ and −x⁴); "an inflection must be a stationary point" (4); "concavity is about increasing or decreasing" (3: the cubic is concave down on parts where it increases and where it decreases).
- **One idea at a time:** one curve carries scenes 2 to 6, adding one stage per scene (f″, concavity, inflection, sign check, classification); one concavity cell per cue; one flat point per sentence in scene 6; one curve per sentence in scene 7.
- **Video makes it easier:** the student watches the tangent turn clockwise then anticlockwise as the gradient graph falls then rises (3), sees the gradient graph's lowest point line up with the inflection (4), sees x⁴'s strip stay positive across zero (5), sees the curve sit under the flat tangent at the hilltop (6), and compares three flat points that the value f″(0) cannot tell apart (7). Each motion is the evidence for the next sentence.

- **Syllabus coverage:** `ci11df959b` (2; key note 1; A1, A2); `cie880425a` (3; key note 2); `ci1fdde799` (4; key note 3); `ci26282587` (5; 12; key note 3); `cid11e4a03` (6, 7; 10; key note 4; local maximum, local minimum and horizontal point of inflection all shown).
