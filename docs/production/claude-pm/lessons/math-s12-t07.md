### 4. one sine, two angles
> Now a coat hanger. Its wire makes a triangle, and the corner at the hook is wide open. An angle like that is called obtuse, between ninety and a hundred and eighty degrees. Here's the catch. On this half circle, put an arm one metre long at the angle. The arm's height is opposite over a hypotenuse of one, so its height is the **sine**. That works for any angle up to a hundred and eighty degrees. At fifty degrees and at a hundred and thirty degrees, the arm reaches the same height, like mirror **images**, so sine fifty degrees and sine a hundred and thirty degrees are exactly the same number. Try it. Sine a hundred and thirty degrees on your calculator is zero point seven six **six**. So when you use inverse sine, the calculator can only give one answer, and it always gives the acute one, under ninety **degrees**. You won't have to decide which one you need. The question will say if the angle is obtuse, and then you take the calculator's angle away from a hundred and eighty **degrees**.

### 5. worked example: the coat hanger
> Here's an exam-style one. A coat hanger's wire forms triangle A B C. The bottom bar, A B, is forty centimetres, the side B C is twenty-two centimetres, and the angle at A is twenty-five degrees. The angle at C, at the hook, is obtuse. Find it, to the nearest minute. The pair is twenty-two centimetres with the twenty-five degree angle it faces. The angle at C faces the forty centimetre bar. With sines on top, sine C equals forty times sine twenty-five degrees, divided by twenty-two. That's about zero point seven six **eight**. Inverse sine gives fifty point two one degrees, the acute angle. But C is obtuse, so C is a hundred and eighty minus fifty point two one, which is a hundred and twenty-nine point seven nine **degrees**. That's a hundred and twenty-nine degrees, forty-seven minutes. Check, twenty-five plus that is under a hundred and eighty, so the triangle **closes**. Writing fifty degrees, thirteen minutes is the slip. That's the acute angle with the same **sine**.

### 6. when this method fits

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why flip: equal fractions stay equal when all are turned over, and it puts the unknown on top (2). Why inverse sine: the rule gives a sine value, not an angle (3). Why the check works: the shorter side faces the smaller angle (3). Why an obtuse angle has a sine at all: an arm one metre long has height opposite ÷ 1, its sine (4). Why 180° minus: θ and 180° − θ reach the same height, so share a sine, and the calculator returns only the acute one (4). Why the angle-sum check: the angles must add to 180° (5).
