### 2. food first, and the three real reasons
> Start with **food first**. Most needs are met by ordinary food, timed well. A **supplement** is something added on top, and it earns its place only for a reason. There are three. A diagnosed deficiency, like low iron found by a blood test, that food alone is correcting too slowly. A practical problem, like a recovery drink between two matches on one day, when there's no time or appetite for food. Or a specific **ergogenic** effect, meaning performance-boosting, at a dose ordinary food can't deliver, which is where caffeine and creatine come in. Notice what isn't on the list. "Everyone else takes it." "The tub says it helps recovery." And "just in case my diet has gaps", which is insurance against a problem nobody has checked for. So ask two questions. What problem does it solve? And does this athlete have that problem?

### 3. correct a lack; don't add more
> **Micronutrients** are vitamins and minerals. They supply no energy, but the systems that do can't work without them. Iron is part of haemoglobin, so it carries oxygen, and adolescent female athletes and endurance athletes are most at risk of running low. Calcium and vitamin D support bone. Here's the key idea. Correcting a **deficiency**, a real shortfall, improves performance. Adding more to an athlete who isn't deficient doesn't, and too much iron is harmful. So iron should follow a blood test, not a guess. Protein works the same way. It supplies the material for repair and growth, but the training supplies the stimulus. Once intake is adequate, more protein doesn't build more muscle. A powder can help when food can't practically reach that adequate amount, but then it's convenience, not magic.

### 4. caffeine and creatine, the two with real evidence
> The syllabus names caffeine and creatine because they have the strongest evidence. Caffeine acts on the brain. It lowers how hard effort feels and sharpens alertness and reaction time. The work still costs the same, it just feels easier. It helps endurance, repeated sprints and concentration. But it can cause anxiety, a racing heart, an upset stomach, and, taken late, poor sleep, which damages recovery, so it can't replace sleep. Teenagers are generally advised to limit it. Creatine works in the muscle. In Year 11 you met phosphocreatine, the fuel that rebuilds ATP in the first few seconds of a sprint. Creatine enlarges that store, so more maximal work is done before it runs low, and it refills faster between efforts. That suits repeated sprints and strength training. It doesn't help long endurance, where glycogen is the limit, and it adds body mass, mostly water.

### 5. the risks that never appear on the label
> Now the other side, because discuss means weighing. First, **contamination**. A product can contain something not on its label, from a shared factory line or deliberately added. In sport, the athlete is responsible for whatever is in their body, so "it was in my supplement" isn't a defence. That's how athletes fail drug tests without meaning to. Independently batch-tested products reduce the risk, but don't remove it. Second, side effects and interactions with medication. Third, cost, and the bigger cost: attention taken away from sleep, food and training, which matter far more. And watch the **placebo** effect. Believing a product works can genuinely improve performance, so a teammate swearing by it is weak evidence about the product itself.
