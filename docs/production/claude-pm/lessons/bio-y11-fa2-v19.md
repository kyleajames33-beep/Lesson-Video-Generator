### 6. why blood slows in the capillaries
> Slowest. Surprised? Squeezing the end of a hose makes water squirt faster. But one artery doesn't turn into one narrow capillary. It splits into a huge number of capillaries side by side. Add up all their openings, and the total area is far bigger than the artery's. The same volume of blood still passes every second, but it's spread across that much wider space, so it slows right down, to hundreds of times slower than in the big arteries. And slow is useful here, because diffusion takes time.

### 7. veins: wide, thin, and valves
> After the capillaries, the vessels join up again into veins. By now the pressure is low, so a thick wall isn't needed. A vein has a thinner wall and a wide lumen, so blood flows back easily. But low pressure brings a problem. From your legs, blood has to travel up, against gravity. So many veins have valves, flaps that only open one way. ### 11. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it back (A for artery, A for away). The name depends on direction, not oxygen: the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood to the lungs.
2. Pressure is high and pulsing in arteries, falls as they branch, is low in capillaries and lowest in veins. Each wall suits the pressure inside it and the vessel's job.
3. Artery: thick wall of smooth muscle and elastic tissue, so it withstands the surges and recoils to keep blood moving between beats; relatively small lumen; no valves.
4. Capillary: wall one cell thick (short diffusion distance), narrow lumen (red cells in single file), billions of them (huge surface, huge total area, so slow flow gives time for exchange).
5. Vein: thin wall and wide lumen (easy low-pressure return); many have one-way valves that stop backflow as surrounding muscles squeeze blood on. A model shows these relationships, but not the processes, and no model material is one cell thick.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (2 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Capillary wall one cell thick; single file (1, 5).** Capillary walls are a single layer of endothelial cells on a basement membrane; capillary diameter is about 5 to 10 µm and a red cell about 7 to 8 µm across, so red cells pass in single file and often deform. Correct.
- **Cut artery spurts (1).** Arterial bleeding spurts in time with the heartbeat because arterial pressure is high and pulsatile. Correct as a qualitative statement (no distance claimed).
- **Direction definition; pulmonary artery (2, 3, 9c, 9d).** Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins toward it; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs and the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood to the heart. Correct. (The umbilical arteries and vein of a fetus are a second exception; author note only.) The pulmonary artery's wall is thinner than the aorta's (pulmonary pressure is lower) but it is an elastic artery with no valves along it, and its pressure is pulsatile; the model answer says "pulsing, higher pressure of blood leaving the heart", not "high pressure" in the systemic sense. Confirm acceptable.
- **Pressure along the path (3).** Mean pressure falls from the aorta through arteries and arterioles (the largest drop), is lower in capillaries and lowest in veins near the heart; the pulse is damped in arterioles. The trace is a qualitative "illustrative shape". Correct.
- **Artery wall (4).** Arterial walls have a thick tunica media of smooth muscle and elastic fibres; elastic recoil during diastole maintains flow between beats (the Windkessel effect). Correct. "Most of what you see is wall, and the lumen looks small": acceptable for a muscular artery beside a vein of similar size. Arteries have no valves along their length (the semilunar valves are heart valves at their exit; author note).
- **Capillaries and pressure (5).** Capillary pressure is far lower than arterial pressure (about 35 falling to 15 mmHg against about 120 over 80 in large arteries), so a thin wall is not exposed to arterial surges. Correct as worded ("the pressure has dropped, so a thin wall can cope").
- **Total area and speed (6).** Total capillary cross-sectional area is several hundred to about a thousand times the aorta's; mean velocity falls from roughly 20 to 40 cm/s in the aorta to roughly 0.03 to 0.1 cm/s in capillaries, so "hundreds of times slower" is safe (could be more than a thousand). Confirm wording. Flow rate (volume per second) is the same at each level in steady state. Correct.
- **Veins (7).** Low venous pressure; thin walls and wide lumens; one-way valves in many veins, especially in the limbs; the skeletal muscle pump and valves return blood against gravity. Correct ("many veins").
- **Model weights (4, 7).** A 200 g slotted mass on a thick rubber tube and on a soft thin tube: the painted states must show the thick tube barely dented and the soft tube flattened. Plausible for real materials; the plates are illustrations of the test.
- **Model limits (8).** No everyday material approaches one cell (a few micrometres or less) thick; rubber lacks living elastic tissue's properties; a paper flap is stiffer than a venous valve; a static model shows no exchange or recoil. Correct (site wording).
- **Capillary section scale tag (5).** "Drawn about 50 times larger" is relative to the artery and vein sections if they are drawn at a common scale for a medium artery (about 4 mm) and a capillary (about 8 µm), which is actually about 500 times.
