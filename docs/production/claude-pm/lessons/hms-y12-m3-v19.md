### 2. six key features, as design choices
> In Year 11 you met the seventeen **Sustainable Development Goals** as a ring, with health in the middle. They were adopted by United Nations members in 2015, with targets to 2030. Their key features were each chosen to fix a problem with earlier goals. They're **universal**. Every country reports, including Australia, because earlier goals assumed rich countries had arrived, and we haven't, as the gaps by remoteness and for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples show. They're **indivisible**. You can't reach good health without water, food, education, housing and income. They're comprehensive: social, economic and environmental together. They're measurable, with **targets** and indicators, so progress can be checked. They **leave no one behind**: a national average improving while a group falls behind isn't success. And they're time-bound, but not legally binding, which is their biggest weakness. For health, indivisibility matters most. Most of what decides health is decided outside the health system.

### 3. SDG 3: good health and wellbeing
> SDG 3 is Good Health and Wellbeing: healthy lives and wellbeing for everyone, at every age. In plain words, its targets cover five things. Mothers, babies and children surviving. Ending epidemics of infectious disease. Cutting early deaths from chronic disease, and supporting mental health. Reducing harm from alcohol, drugs and road crashes. And universal health coverage, meaning everyone gets essential care and medicines without financial hardship, backed by a funded workforce. Australia does well on the first two. The live issues are chronic disease, mental health, and partial coverage. And notice something. Most SDG 3 targets are outcomes, things that should be true, not actions. The actions are mostly found in the other goals.

### 4. SDG 4, and six ways education produces health
> SDG 4 is Quality Education: inclusive, fair education and lifelong learning for all, with equal access for girls, people with disability and Indigenous peoples. Why is it in a health course? Because education is one of the strongest causes of health, and not just as a stand-in for income. It works six ways. It builds **health literacy**, the skill to find and use health information. It leads to better, more secure work and income. It brings more control over your life, and low control means chronic stress. It changes what's affordable, available and normal, not because educated people are better. It makes it easier to navigate services. And it passes down. A mother's education is one of the strongest predictors of her children's survival and health. So education is a health intervention that works decades early, on several causes at once, and across generations.

### 5. one loop, both directions
> And it runs both ways. Health also produces education. A child who's sick, hungry, or can't see or hear properly, or who's caring for a sick parent, doesn't attend or learn well. Untreated vision and hearing problems are among the most common, and most fixable, barriers to learning. So the two goals form a loop. Better child health means better learning, which means better work and health literacy, which means healthier adults, who raise healthier children. Run it the other way and it's a trap. Poor health interrupts school, which leads to insecure work and worse adult health. But you can intervene anywhere and turn the loop, like with vision and hearing checks or meals at school. That's why the other goals aren't background to health. They're the mechanism.

### 6. what the framework is good for, and what it isn't
> So what are the goals good for? They give health, education, housing and councils a shared language, which helps across the seams. They make it legitimate for a council fixing footpaths to call it health work. Their targets and group reporting make progress checkable. But there's no enforcement. Countries volunteer and report on themselves. Seventeen goals can scatter effort and let people report only what's going well. Some things are badly measured, especially for small groups. And signing up is cheap. The goals don't supply money, workers or political will. So the SDGs are a framework for thinking and reporting, not a program of work. They can show the connections and check progress, but they can't make anyone act.

## Memory aid check

- **"Health and education are one loop."** Assumption: causation runs both ways in the population. For an individual many other causes intervene; the aid describes a population pattern, which is how scene 5 shows it.
