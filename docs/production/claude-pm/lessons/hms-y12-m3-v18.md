### 2. five uses, and why linkage matters
> **Big data** means datasets so large and varied that they show patterns no single record can. It's used five ways. First, and biggest, just knowing what's happening. Every trend in this course comes from linked national data. Second, surveillance, like spotting outbreaks and rare medicine side effects. Third, planning and evaluation. Fourth, prediction: who's at high risk, so attention goes there first. Fifth, research: does a treatment work in real life, not just in a trial? Most of the value comes from **data linkage**, joining records about the same person from different sources. A prescribing record says what was dispensed. Linked to hospital and death records, it says whether the medicine helped or harmed. And that's exactly where the privacy risk is greatest. The benefit and the risk have the same source.

### 3. does it reduce spending?
> Does it reduce spending? Answer honestly. Some savings are real. It avoids repeat tests. It can prevent avoidable admissions, if someone follows up. It reveals low-value care and **unwarranted variation**, the same operation done at very different rates in similar populations. And it targets programs at the people most likely to benefit. But the claim is weakest in four places. Prediction isn't intervention. Knowing who'll be readmitted saves nothing unless something's funded to act. Finding more disease often costs more, though that's a health gain. The data systems themselves are expensive. And some savings just move to another funder. So the honest version is that big data improves the value of spending more reliably than it cuts the total.

### 4. curing and managing disease
> How is it used to cure and manage disease? It finds causes, because rare and small effects only show up across huge numbers of people. It powers **precision medicine**, matching treatment to a person's features, like choosing a cancer drug from the tumour's genes. It speeds research and follows outcomes for years. It shows how treatments work in everyone, including older people and people with several conditions, who trials often leave out. It helps services spot patients drifting out of control before they end up in hospital. One caution. If a dataset under-represents some groups, like rural communities or Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, its conclusions fit them worse. So collecting representative data is an equity step, not paperwork.

### 5a. privacy and confidentiality: the first measures
> So what protects the people inside the data? First, two meanings. **Privacy** is your right to control information about yourself. **Confidentiality** is the duty not to share what was given in trust. Now the measures. Collect only what's needed, and don't keep it forever. **De-identify** it, removing names, but know that's imperfect. **Re-identification** can happen when age, postcode and dates are combined, especially for rare conditions or small towns.

### 5b. the measures that limit who looks, and the trade-off
> Give access for a stated purpose, only the fields needed, with every look logged. Trusted staff isn't a control. Have an independent body approve each new use. Encrypt and monitor it. Make penalties real. And where possible, send the question to the data instead of copying the data out. Stronger privacy means less can be learned, so the line has to be drawn deliberately. One serious breach can cost the public trust that every future use depends on.

### 6. how big data shapes health
> Notice the syllabus says shaping, not just helping. Big data shapes health in four ways. It decides what's visible, because a problem that's measured becomes a policy problem, and splitting data by group is how an inequity becomes something the system must answer for. It shifts care from reacting to anticipating. It changes what treatment means, from the group average to the person. And it moves power to whoever holds and analyses the data. Public bodies with oversight ask the population's questions. Companies ask the questions worth money. So big data is one of the biggest opportunities in health, but it's not automatically a benefit. It depends on representative data, someone funded to act, trusted rules, and attention to who benefits.
