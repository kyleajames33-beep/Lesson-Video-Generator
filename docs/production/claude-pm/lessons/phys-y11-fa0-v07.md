- **Main example (fa1-v15's investigation; no new physics).** Series A (total mass 1.00 kg; F_net 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80 N; mean a 0.21, 0.39, 0.61, 0.79 m s⁻², scatter ±0.05 m s⁻²; best-fit gradient 1.0 kg⁻¹; steepest and shallowest lines 1.08 and 0.90 kg⁻¹, giving 0.93 kg to 1.1 kg; balance 1.00 kg) and series B (F_net 0.60 N; m 1.00, 1.50, 2.00 kg; mean a 0.61, 0.40, 0.29 m s⁻²; a × m 0.61, 0.60, 0.58 N), all from `phys-y11-fa1-v15.md` author note a and scenes 5 to 7, unchanged and tagged "illustrative data (simulated)". v15's own conclusion card is the model answer this video builds towards, so the two videos agree word for word on the limits.
- **The hook (a choice between three claims).** Three cards about series A's graph: (1) "Our results prove that F = ma."; (2) "The trolley sped up more with a bigger force."; (3) "Within the scatter bars, from 0.20 to 0.80 N, the trolley's acceleration was proportional to the net force." 3 s prediction: "Which claim do these data support?" Answer: (3). (1) claims too much: data can support a law over the range tested, not prove it for every case. (2) claims too little: true, but it doesn't state the relationship the graph tests. A coded "claim meter" under the cards ("too little", "fits the evidence", "too much") makes the judgement visible.
- **The argument (`ci9fda93ae`).** Plain meaning: "a scientific argument: a claim, backed by evidence, with reasoning that links them". Built in four coloured slots (the same slot style as fa0-v01's if, then, because): claim (ink), evidence (navy, series A's colour in v15), reasoning (gold, as fa0-v01's because slot: the reasoning is the physics), limits (grey dashed). Reasoning taught as two links: a straight line through the origin is what proportional looks like; the gradient should be 1/m, and the balance agrees (two independent measurements). Limits: the range tested and the friction check's sensitivity (v15's leftover force ≲ 0.02 N at 1.00 kg). "Support, not prove" is said once with its reason.
- **Language and notation (`ci720f6e48`).** A draft sentence is repaired one fault at a time (`SentenceRepair`): "The trolley got faster quicker with more power, 0.8 gave 0.79." becomes "With a net force of 0.80 N, the trolley's mean acceleration was 0.79 m s⁻²." Each fix has its reason: "faster quicker" is acceleration (a precise term); "power" is a different quantity (nomenclature: the agreed name for each quantity); every value gets its unit; units in the standard form (m s⁻², not "m/s/s" or "metres per second per second" in a table); quantity symbols (a, F_net, m) mean the same to every reader. Scientific notation for very large or small values is shown with one value from the falling-ball practical (0.000 65 s → 6.5 × 10⁻⁴ s; tag "for very large or small values"). Significant figures are not re-taught (fa0-v05); the repaired sentence keeps the data's two decimal places, said on screen only.
- **Peer review (`ci9fda93ae`, "recognise the role").** Plain meaning: "peer review: other experts in the same field check a study's method, data and reasoning before it is published". What they check (method sound? data support the claim? anything missing?), what they can do (ask for changes, reject), and what it cannot do (reviewers usually can't repeat the experiment, so errors can still get through; later work can still challenge a result). Prediction (3 s): "Passed peer review: definitely right?" Answer: no; it raises trust, it doesn't guarantee. Classroom version: a peer reads the trolley report and leaves two margin comments, one on the practice ("Was the friction checked for each load?") and one on the conclusion ("Your claim says 'any force', but you tested 0.20 to 0.80 N."); the report is revised and the argument's limits slot updates. This covers "evaluation of scientific practices ... or conclusions"; "solutions" is named on screen in the plain-meaning chip only (open decision 3).
- **Media (`cic4ffcedd`).** Four tiles, each with the job it does for this argument: written report (full method and every number, so someone can check or repeat it); visual (the graph shows the evidence at a glance); digital (a slide or short clip can show the trolley moving and the graph building); oral (a talk can explain and take questions, but listeners can't reread, so one clear graph, not a table of twelve numbers). Rule: choose the medium that carries the evidence the argument needs. No speaker is drawn: the oral tile is a slide with a coded sound waveform.
- **Audience and purpose (`cicc30bb9d`).** One result, two documents side by side (`AudienceSwitch`): a lab report for the teacher (purpose: show the whole investigation for assessment; full terms, units, uncertainties) and a Year 8 class poster (purpose: explain one finding; fewer numbers, plainer sentences, one graph). The same series A graph sits on both, unchanged (evidence kept). The term "net force" stays on the poster with its plain meaning beside it ("the total push or pull left over"), because swapping in a wrong everyday word would teach a wrong idea. Rule: change the words, the detail and the medium for the audience; never change the evidence, and keep the correct terms, explained if needed. No class or teacher is drawn; audiences are tags on the documents ("for: your teacher", "for: a Year 8 class").

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why "prove" is wrong: data cover only the range and conditions tested (2). Why claim 2 is weak: it doesn't state the relationship the graph tests (2). Why state reasoning: a reader can check a claim only through the link between the evidence and the claim (3). Why limits: the data say nothing beyond the range tested (3). Why precise terms and units: every reader takes the same meaning, so the claim can be checked (4). Why peer review raises trust: independent experts catch what the writer missed (5, 6). Why it can't guarantee: reviewers rarely repeat the work (6). Why the medium matters: each carries different parts of the argument (7). Why words change with the audience but evidence doesn't: the reader's knowledge and purpose differ, the result doesn't (8).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** results prove a law (1, 2; A1 crossed); a true but vague statement is a good conclusion (2); everyday words are fine in a report (4; A2); peer review means the result is definitely right (5, 6); any medium will do (7); simplify the science, not the words, for a younger audience (8; A2 crossed).
- **One idea at a time:** which claim (1, 2), argument parts (2, 3), words (4), peer review (5, 6), media (7), audience (8), all together (13).
