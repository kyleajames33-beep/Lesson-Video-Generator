- **Language and notation (`ci720f6e48`).** A draft sentence is repaired one fault at a time (`SentenceRepair`): "The trolley got faster quicker with more power, 0.8 gave 0.79." becomes "With a net force of 0.80 N, the trolley's mean acceleration was 0.79 m s⁻²." Each fix has its reason: "faster quicker" is acceleration (a precise term); "power" is a different quantity (nomenclature: the agreed name for each quantity); every value gets its unit; units in the standard form (m s⁻², not "m/s/s" or "metres per second per second" in a table); quantity symbols (a, F_net, m) mean the same to every reader. Scientific notation for very large or small values is shown with one value from the falling-ball practical (0.000 65 s → 6.5 × 10⁻⁴ s; tag "for very large or small values"). - **Peer review (`ci9fda93ae`, "recognise the role").** Plain meaning: "peer review: other experts in the same field check a study's method, data and reasoning before it is published". What they check (method sound? data support the claim? anything missing?), what they can do (ask for changes, reject), and what it cannot do (reviewers usually can't repeat the experiment, so errors can still get through; later work can still challenge a result). Prediction (3 s): "Passed peer review: definitely right?" Answer: no; it raises trust, it doesn't guarantee. Classroom version: a peer reads the trolley report and leaves two margin comments, one on the practice ("Was the friction checked for each load?") and one on the conclusion ("Your claim says 'any force', but you tested 0.20 to 0.80 N."); the report is revised and the argument's limits slot updates. This covers "evaluation of scientific practices ... or conclusions"; "solutions" is named on screen in the plain-meaning chip only (open decision 3).

### 1. hook (which claim?)
> Before we use that law the other way round, there's one more job: telling other people what the trolley data showed. Here's the graph, and three ways students wrote it up. One: our results prove that F equals m a. Two: the trolley sped up more with a bigger force. Three: within the scatter bars, from nought point two to nought point eight newtons, the acceleration was proportional to the net force. Which claim do these data actually **support**?

Visual: the first sentence is the separate bridge take, over fa1-v15's last frame (see "Opening frame"). On "telling other people" the crate and thumbnails slide off right and v15's series A graph (`DataGraph`: navy points with scatter bars, the ink best-fit line through the origin; axes "acceleration (m s⁻²)" up, "net force (N)" across; tag "illustrative data (simulated)") grows to the left 55 per cent; the heading writes: "Communicating: from results to an argument". Three claim cards slide into the right column as spoken: "1 Our results prove that F = ma.", "2 The trolley sped up more with a bigger force.", "3 Within the scatter bars, from 0.20 to 0.80 N, the trolley's acceleration was proportional to the net force." On "support" a 3-second countdown ring.

### 2. an argument: claim and evidence
> The third. The first claims too much. Data can support a law over the range you tested, but they can't prove it for every case. The second claims too little. It's true, but it doesn't say proportional, which is what the graph tests. So a scientific argument is a claim, backed by evidence, with reasoning that links them. Here, the claim is the third card. The evidence is the graph. One straight line through the origin passes through every scatter **bar**.

Visual: `ClaimCompare`: a claim meter writes under the cards, "too little", "fits the evidence", "too much". On "The third" card 3's marker slides to "fits the evidence" and glows. On "claims too much" card 1's marker slides to "too much", its word "prove" underlines, tag "support, not prove"; card 1 takes a muted red scribble cross. On "claims too little" card 2's marker slides to "too little", tag "doesn't state the relationship"; it greys. On "scientific argument" a gloss "argument: a claim, backed by evidence, with reasoning that links them". `ArgumentSlots` open in the right column: on "the claim" card 3 slides into the ink "claim" slot; on "The evidence is the graph" the navy "evidence" slot fills "series A: one straight line through the origin passes through every scatter bar", with a leader line to the graph, where the line and each bar tick in turn on "every scatter bar".

### 3. reasoning and limits

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why "prove" is wrong: data cover only the range and conditions tested (2). Why claim 2 is weak: it doesn't state the relationship the graph tests (2). Why state reasoning: a reader can check a claim only through the link between the evidence and the claim (3). Why limits: the data say nothing beyond the range tested (3). Why precise terms and units: every reader takes the same meaning, so the claim can be checked (4). Why peer review raises trust: independent experts catch what the writer missed (5, 6).
