### 2. what a ratio is
> A ratio compares two or more amounts measured in the same unit. We write it with a colon, and we say "to". Here we want cordial to water. But the cordial is in millilitres and the water is in litres, so first, make the units match. One litre is one thousand millilitres. So cordial to water is two hundred and fifty to one **thousand**. Order matters. Water to cordial would be one thousand to two hundred and **fifty**, a different ratio. So write the amounts in the order the question names them.

### 3. simplest form
> Two hundred and fifty to one thousand is correct, but it's clumsy. A ratio stays the same if you multiply or divide every term by the same number, because every amount grows or shrinks together. The biggest number that divides every term is called the highest common factor. Here it's two hundred and **fifty**. Two hundred and fifty divided by two hundred and fifty is one, and one thousand divided by two hundred and fifty is **four**. So the ratio is one to four. That's its simplest form, because no number bigger than one divides both terms. Check by multiplying back. One lot of two hundred and fifty, and four lots of two hundred and fifty, give the amounts we started with. One to four, two to eight, and two hundred and fifty to one thousand are called equivalent ratios. They all describe the same **mix**.

### 4. ratio and fraction
> Now the question we started with. One to four means one part cordial for every four parts water. Picture the jug in equal parts. That's one plus four, five parts **altogether**. Cordial is one of those five parts, so cordial is one fifth of the **jug**. That's the link. A ratio compares the parts with each other, and a fraction compares one part with the whole. In a ratio a to b, there are a plus b parts, so the first amount is a over a plus b of the **whole**. The classic slip is to say one quarter. One quarter compares the cordial with the water, not with the whole **jug**.

### 5. big numbers, in steps
> Simplest form works for big numbers too, and you don't have to spot the highest common factor straight away. A phone screen is one thousand nine hundred and twenty pixels across, and one thousand and eighty down. A pixel is a tiny dot of light. Divide both by ten, and it's a hundred and ninety-two to a hundred and **eight**. Both are even, so halve twice, and it's forty-eight to twenty-**seven**. Both are in the three times table, so divide by three, sixteen to **nine**. Nothing else divides both, so that's simplest form. It's why shops call it a sixteen to nine screen.

### 6. worked example: a footy season
> Here's an exam-style one. A local footy team played forty-two games last season. It won eighteen and lost twenty-four, with no draws. Write the ratio of wins to losses in simplest form, and find the fraction of games the team won. Wins come first, because the question says wins to losses. That's eighteen to twenty-**four**. The highest common factor is six, so divide both by six, and it's three to **four**. Check, three sixes are eighteen, and four sixes are twenty-four. For the fraction, three plus four is seven parts, and wins are three of them. So the team won three sevenths of its **games**, which matches eighteen out of forty-two. Saying three quarters is the slip. Three quarters compares wins with losses, not with all the **games**.

## Teaching checks

Why five parts: the terms count equal parts, so they add to the whole (4). Why one quarter is wrong here: it compares part with part (4).
