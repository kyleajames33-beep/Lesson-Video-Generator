> In 1985, British officials doubted that a boy returning from Ghana was really his mother's son. A geneticist, Alec Jeffreys, had just found a way to compare people's DNA as patterns of bands. ### 2. two tools, two questions
> There are two main ways to get that kind of evidence. DNA sequencing reads the exact order of bases, the A's, T's, C's and G's, along a stretch of DNA. DNA profiling doesn't read the order at all. It compares a few chosen places where people's DNA tends to differ. So one reads every letter, and the other checks a handful of markers. Which one you use depends on the question you're asking.

### 3. how sequencing works
> Sequencing never reads a whole chromosome in one go. First, the DNA is copied and broken into short fragments. A machine reads each fragment one base at a time, because each kind of base is tagged with its own coloured dye. The copies were broken at different places, so the fragments overlap. Software lines up those overlaps, like matching the edges of torn strips, and rebuilds the whole sequence.

### 4. sequencing a family, and a population

That's the recessive pattern, now seen in the DNA itself. Sequence thousands of people this way, and you get the allele's frequency across a whole population.

### 5. short tandem repeats
> Today, profiling usually looks at markers called short tandem repeats. Each one is a short run of bases, like G, A, T, A, repeated over and over, side by side. The number of repeats varies from person to person. One copy of a marker might have eight repeats, and the other copy eleven. You carry two copies of every marker, one from each parent, so your repeat numbers are inherited, just like alleles.

### 6. separating repeats in a gel
> To compare lengths, labs copy each marker many times, then run the copies through a gel. DNA is negatively charged, so an electric current pulls it towards the positive end. The gel is a jelly full of tiny pores. Every piece carries the same charge for its length, so the pores decide the speed: shorter pieces slip through more easily, so they travel further. Each length ends up as a band, and a person's set of bands is their profile.

Visual: `m5x-gel-tank-v1.png` sits at the upper right as context (no bands drawn on the plate; grey tag "gel tank"). On "copy each marker many times" the 8-block and 11-block strips multiply into small stacks (grey tag "PCR: Module 6"). The coded `Gel` slab draws large and front-on: wells along the top, the top edge tagged "−  (black lead)", the bottom edge "+  (red lead)". ### 7. reading inheritance from bands
> Here's how profiles show inheritance. At every marker, a child has one copy from the mother and one from the father. So every band in the child's profile must match a band in one parent or the other. Here, the child's eight came from the mother, and the twelve came from the father. So if a child has a band that neither the mother nor a man has, that man can be ruled out as the father.

### 8. why a match needs many markers
> A match is weaker evidence than a mismatch, because lots of people share any one band pattern. Say each marker's pattern is shared by one person in ten. Matching one marker means very little. But if the markers are inherited separately, the chances multiply. Three markers give one in a thousand, and ten give one in ten billion. That's why real profiles compare many markers, and even then, a match is strong evidence, not proof.

### 9a. choosing the tool
> So which tool fits which question? If you need a particular base, like a disease allele or a snip, sequence it. Profiling can't see it, because it only measures repeat lengths, mostly in DNA that doesn't code for a protein. If you need to know who's related to whom, profiling is quick and cheap across many individuals. That's how conservation programs check relatedness in captive animals before choosing mates.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- **Sequencing.** Methods read short fragments (Sanger reads up to about 1,000 bases; common short-read methods about 100 to 300), each base type detected by its own fluorescent dye label (some current instruments use two colours, or non-optical detection; the narration says "tagged with its own coloured dye", true of the common four-dye methods students meet), and reads are assembled or aligned by overlap with software. "Never reads a whole chromosome in one go": correct for the methods students meet (long-read methods read tens of thousands of bases, still far short of a chromosome). Correct as stated.
- **Heterozygous trace.** In dye-based sequencing of a heterozygote, two overlapping peaks appear at the variant position. Correct.
- **Sickle family (scene 4).** I-1 A/T, I-2 A/T (unaffected carriers); II-1 T/T (affected daughter), II-2 A/A, II-3 A/T. Autosomal recessive (HBB on chromosome 11); fits AR (`fitsMode`). The coding-strand base at codon 6 middle position is A (GAG) in the normal allele and T (GTG) in the sickle allele. Correct (B7 convention).
- **STR.** Short tandem repeats: units of 2 to 6 bases (forensic markers mostly 4-base units, e.g. TH01 is a TCAT repeat; GATA-type units are common, e.g. D7S820 and D13S317 are GATA or TATC repeats). The example "G, A, T, A" is a typical tetranucleotide unit. Repeat numbers vary between people (often about 5 to 30 for common markers). Two copies per autosomal marker, one inherited from each parent. Correct. Most forensic STRs lie in non-coding DNA. Correct.
- **Gel.** DNA's phosphate backbone is negatively charged at the buffer pH, so it migrates to the positive electrode (anode); in agarose, shorter fragments migrate further, distance roughly linear in the logarithm of length over the useful range. The bank's "red lead = positive" follows the usual lead colours. Correct. Capillary electrophoresis is the modern forensic method (grey tag). Correct.
- **Band lengths.** Fragment = flank + 4 x repeats; with flank 100 bases, 8 repeats = 132, 11 = 144, 12 = 148 bases. Differences of 4 bases are resolvable in high-resolution systems; the coded gel exaggerates spacing for legibility (author note; a real agarose gel would not separate 4-base differences cleanly).
