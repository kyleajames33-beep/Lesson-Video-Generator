- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Margarine: invented in 1869 (from beef fat); catalytic hydrogenation of vegetable oils (nickel catalyst, about 150 to 200 °C, hydrogen under pressure) was patented in the early 1900s and was the usual way to harden oils for margarine through much of the twentieth century. Partial hydrogenation also forms trans fats, and many current spreads use blending or interesterification instead. The hook says only "for much of the last century, that's how margarine was made" and that hydrogen adds "across some of those double bonds". Fats in oil are triglycerides with long C=C-containing chains; "long molecules contain carbon to carbon double bonds" is accurate at this level. (b) Markovnikov's rule: when HX or H₂O adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the major product usually has the H on the double-bond carbon that already has more H atoms (propene + HBr gives mainly 2-bromopropane; but-1-ene + H₂O gives mainly butan-2-ol). Not required by the 2017 syllabus and not taught; site lesson 6 marks it as enrichment. Scene 8 names both possible products and says nothing about amounts; feedback 2 relies only on "one possible product against two". In practice, acid-catalysed hydration of but-1-ene and 2-methylpropene gives almost only the Markovnikov alcohol; attempt 2 therefore says "only one alcohol product is possible" (Revision 2), so a student who knows the rule cannot answer but-1-ene correctly. (c) Hydrogenation: nickel is typically used at about 150 to 200 °C (site), often under pressure in industry; palladium or platinum work at room temperature. The arrow reads "Ni catalyst, about 150 °C". (d) Bromine water: in aqueous solution the main organic product is usually 2-bromoethanol (a bromohydrin) because water is in excess; the HSC-standard equation is ethene + Br₂ → 1,2-dibromoethane, which the video writes for bromine itself. Scene 5 says "When an alkene adds that bromine, the colour disappears", which is true for either product. (e) Hydrohalogenation: HBr and HCl add to alkenes at room temperature without a catalyst (site lesson 6 card 3); in the gas phase or in a non-aqueous solvent. (f) Hydration: industrial direct hydration uses phosphoric acid on a solid silica support, about 300 °C and about 6 to 7 MPa (60 to 70 atm), with steam; C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ C₂H₅OH(g) (two moles of gas to one, so high pressure favours ethanol; ethanol is a gas at 300 °C). The conversion per pass is low and unreacted ethene is recycled. Hydration is exothermic, so 300 °C is a rate and yield compromise (Module 5); scene 7 says "for a fast enough rate". Dilute aqueous H₂SO₄ is also used in the laboratory. Globally, most ethanol (mainly fuel ethanol) is made by fermentation; the video says "one industrial route to ethanol". (g) Feedback 2: the C₄H₈ alkenes are but-1-ene, but-2-ene and 2-methylpropene (but-2-ene exists as cis and trans forms, which are not in the 2017 syllabus and both give butan-2-ol). The C₄H₈ cycloalkanes are cyclobutane and methylcyclopropane; HSC treatment is that cycloalkanes do not decolourise bromine water (methylcyclopropane's strained ring can react with bromine slowly; "rapidly" in the question excludes it). Butan-2-ol is formed as a racemic mixture of two enantiomers, which counts as one alcohol at this level (stereoisomers are not in the syllabus). (h) Equations are written with full or condensed structural formulas; HSC questions that say "using structural formulae" accept condensed structural formulas such as CH₃CHClCH₂Cl. The video draws full structural formulas on the board and writes the condensed form in the answer chip. ### 2. why an alkene can add and an alkane can't
> Start with why. In an alkane, every carbon already has four single bonds, so there's no spare bond for a new atom. It's **saturated**. An alkene's double bond is different. As you saw in the properties video, its **second** bond is the weaker one, and its electrons sit above and below the carbons, where other molecules can reach them. That second bond can **break**. Then each carbon has one spare bond, and a new atom can join each one.
