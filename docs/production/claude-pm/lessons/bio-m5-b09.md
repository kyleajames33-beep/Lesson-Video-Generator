Out of 1,064 plants in the next generation, 277 were short. So how can two tall plants have a short offspring? And how can a family tree tell us the same kind of thing about people?

### 2. why one allele can hide another
> Remember, genes code for proteins, and many proteins are enzymes. The tall allele codes for an enzyme that makes a growth hormone. The short allele's version barely works. A plant with one of each still makes enough hormone, so it grows tall. That's what **dominant** means: the allele shows even with only one copy. The short allele is **recessive**: it shows only with two copies.

Visual: the cross board's left side. B8's grey tag "which version shows: next video" slides in and is struck through, replaced by "now". On "genes code for proteins" a small gene card with an arrow to a coded enzyme blob (grey tag "B7"). On "makes a growth hormone" the enzyme blob turns small coded hormone dots out into a stem outline, which lengthens. On "barely works" a second enzyme blob with a slightly misshapen pocket makes one hormone dot (tag "barely works"). On "one of each" a stem outline with one working and one weak enzyme still fills with hormone dots and grows tall; `m5x-pea-tall-v1.png` stands beside it. On "dominant" a chip "T" (capital, large serif) writes "dominant: shows with one copy". On "recessive" a chip "t" writes "recessive: shows only with two copies", and `m5x-pea-short-v1.png` stands beside a "t t" card. A grey tag: "genotype: the alleles; phenotype: what you see (B7)". ### 3. what goes into a gamete
> Two of the same allele is called **homozygous**, and one of each is **heterozygous**. So which alleles go into a gamete? In meiosis, the two chromosomes of each pair separate, so each gamete gets just one allele of each gene. A heterozygous plant makes two kinds of gamete: half carry the tall allele, and half carry the short one. That explains Mendel's first cross. A tall plant with two tall alleles, crossed with a short plant, gives offspring that all get one of each. They're all tall, but every one carries the short allele.

Visual: on "homozygous" three allele cards line up under three plates: "TT" (tall, tag "homozygous"), "Tt" (tall, tag "heterozygous" on its cue), "tt" (short, tag "homozygous"). Then the "Tt" card opens into B5b's plum and teal single chromosome plates, a "T" chip on the plum and a "t" chip on the teal (grey tag "B5b"). On "separate" the two chromosomes slide apart into two gamete discs (no stage drawing), each keeping one chip: "T" (plum edge) and "t" (teal edge). On "half carry" each disc gets "1/2" (computed). On "Mendel's first cross" the board shows "TT x tt": the TT parent sends only "T" discs, the tt parent only "t" discs; on "all get one of each" four offspring cards "Tt" drop in under four small tall plates; on "every one carries the short allele" each card's "t" chip gets a small amber ring (tag "hidden, not lost"). Hold one second.

### 4. the Punnett square
> A **Punnett square** lays out every way the gametes can meet. One parent's gametes go across the top, the other's down the side. Each box is one equally likely fertilisation. Fill them in: one box with two tall alleles, two heterozygous boxes, and one with two short alleles. That's a genotype ratio of 1 to 2 to 1. Three of the four boxes have a tall allele, so the phenotype ratio is 3 tall to 1 short. Mendel's 787 to 277 is very close.

Visual: `PunnettGrid` draws on the right: "Tt x Tt" above it. On "across the top" the plum-edged discs "T" and "t" slide along the top; on "down the side" the teal-edged discs slide down the side. On "Each box" one box pulses with two thin lines from its row and column disc (tag "one fertilisation, chance 1/4"). On "Fill them in" the boxes fill one per cue, each chip flying in from its disc: "TT", "Tt", "Tt", "tt". On "1 to 2 to 1" `RatioStrip` row 1 writes "genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt" (computed). On "Three of the four boxes" small tall plates pop into three boxes and a short plate into one; row 2 writes "phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 short" (computed by merging). ### 5. a chance for each offspring
> But 3 to 1 is a chance for each offspring, not a promise for every group of four. Each seed has a one in four chance of being short, whatever happened to the seed before it. Plant just four seeds from this cross, and you'll get exactly one short plant only about 42 per cent of the time. With hundreds of seeds, like Mendel had, the counts settle close to the ratio.

Visual: the grid shrinks to the corner with a tag "1/4 short, for each seed". `FamilyReplay`: rows of four seed plates (`m5x-pea-yellow-round-v1.png` used as a neutral seed token, with a small plant icon that grows tall or short above each) draw one row at a time, outcomes from a seeded random with p = 1/4: row 1 "0 short", row 2 "2 short", row 3 "1 short", row 4 "1 short", row 5 "0 short". ### 6. the test cross
> So how can you tell whether a tall plant is homozygous or heterozygous, when they look the same? Cross it with a short plant, which can only give the short allele. This is a **test cross**. A heterozygous tall plant gives each offspring a half chance of being short. So if even one offspring is short, the tall parent must carry the short allele. If they're all tall, it's probably homozygous, and more offspring make that more certain.

### 7. reading a pedigree
> We can't run test crosses on people, so instead we read family trees called **pedigrees**. Squares are males, circles are females, and a shaded symbol means the person has the trait. A line joins two parents, and a line down from it leads to their children. Each generation gets a Roman numeral, and each person a number, so you can point to exactly who you mean. In this video, the genes are all on **autosomes**, the chromosomes that aren't sex chromosomes.

### 8. a recessive pedigree
> Here's a family with a recessive condition, such as cystic fibrosis. In generation one, an affected father has two unaffected children. Every child of his got one recessive allele from him, so his daughter carries it, hidden. In generation three, it reappears: two unaffected parents have an affected daughter. That's the giveaway. If the parents don't show it, but their child does, each parent must carry one hidden recessive allele. People like this are called **carriers**. So recessive traits can skip a generation.

### 9. a dominant pedigree
> Now a dominant condition, such as Huntington's disease. Look for clues: it usually shows up in every generation, and every affected child has an affected parent. An affected, heterozygous parent and an unaffected parent give each child a half chance, the same square as the test cross. The giveaway here is the opposite: two affected parents with an unaffected child. Each parent must carry a hidden recessive allele, so the condition must be dominant. And dominant doesn't mean common. Huntington's is dominant, but rare.

### 10. the giveaways, and how to use them
> Here's how to remember them. Hidden in the parents: recessive. Hidden in the child: dominant. In an answer, name the people. For example: individuals 3 and 4 in generation two are unaffected, but their daughter, individual 1 in generation three, is affected, so the condition is recessive. Then write their genotypes, and use a Punnett square for any probability.

Visual: two big handwritten lines write on, each with a tiny trio icon beside it: "Hidden in the parents: recessive" (two open symbols, filled child) and "Hidden in the child: dominant" (two filled symbols, open child). ### 11b. feedback, item one

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (17 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Griffiths et al., Introduction to Genetic Analysis), Mendel (1866) and the sources named. Every ratio below is also produced at build time by the ported cross function, and every pedigree is checked by `fitsMode`.
- **Mendel's stem length F2.** 787 long (tall) and 277 short; total 1,064; 787 / 277 = 2.84 : 1. Short share 277 / 1,064 = 26.0%. F1 from tall x short all tall. Correct (Mendel 1866, experiment 7).
- **Tall and short peas, protein reason.** Mendel's *Le* gene encodes gibberellin 3-beta-hydroxylase, which makes the active growth hormone GA1; the *le* allele carries a single amino acid change (Ala to Thr) that reduces activity strongly (Lester et al. 1997; Martin et al. 1997). Le is dominant: one working copy makes enough GA1 for a tall plant. "The short allele's version barely works": correct.
- **Tt x Tt.** Boxes TT, Tt, Tt, tt; genotypes 1 : 2 : 1; phenotypes 3 : 1. Correct.
- **Exactly one short in four seeds**, p = 1/4: 4 x (1/4) x (3/4)^3 = 108/256 = 27/64 = 42.19%, "about 42 percent". Full distribution: 0 short 81/256 = 31.6%; 1 short 42.2%; 2 short 54/256 = 21.1%; 3 short 12/256 = 4.7%; 4 short 1/256 = 0.4%; sum 256/256. Correct (matches the site simulator and the pack).
- **Seed as a separate chance.** Each seed in a pod comes from its own fertilisation (B2b, B8). Correct. Peas self-pollinate naturally; Mendel let F1 plants self. Correct.
- **Test cross.** TT x tt: all Tt (tall). Tt x tt: 1 Tt : 1 tt. One recessive offspring proves the tested parent carries t; all dominant offspring is likely but not proof (bank wording). Correct.
- **Round and wrinkled.** Round (R) is dominant to wrinkled (r) (Mendel; *SBEI*). Seed shape is a trait of the embryo, so seeds produced by a cross show the offspring's phenotype. Rr x rr: 1 : 1. 52 : 48 is within normal chance variation for n = 100 (chi-squared 0.16, p about 0.69; not spoken). Correct.
- **Pedigree P1 (scene 8, autosomal recessive).** I-1 affected male x I-2 unaffected female; II-2 unaffected son, II-3 unaffected daughter; II-3 x II-4 (unaffected male, married in); III-1 affected daughter, III-2 unaffected son. AR fit: I-1 aa, I-2 AA (or Aa), II-2 Aa, II-3 Aa (got a from I-1), II-4 Aa (III-1 needs an a from him), III-1 aa, III-2 AA or Aa. Consistent. AD fit fails: III-1 affected with two unaffected parents (aa x aa cannot give A_). So P1 is AR only (under the stated assumptions). III-1 is an affected daughter with an unaffected father, which B10 uses to rule out X-linked recessive. Correct.
- **Pedigree P2 (scene 9, autosomal dominant).** I-1 affected male x I-2 unaffected female; II-1 affected daughter, II-2 unaffected son, II-3 affected son; II-1 x II-4 (unaffected male); III-1 affected son, III-2 unaffected daughter. AD fit: I-1 Aa, I-2 aa, II-1 Aa, II-2 aa, II-3 Aa, II-4 aa, III-1 Aa, III-2 aa. Consistent. AR fit is also possible in principle (I-2 and II-4 would both have to be carriers), which is why scene 9 teaches the decisive pattern on the separate inset trio (two affected parents, unaffected child), where AR fails. Scene 9's spoken claims (Revision 2: "Look for clues: it usually shows up in every generation, and every affected child has an affected parent") describe the pattern as clues; the narration does not claim P2 alone proves dominance. The reviewer's brute-force check found P2 also fits X-linked recessive (I-2 and II-4 as carriers or normal as needed), which does not matter here because the video states that its genes are autosomal, and B10 teaches X linkage.
- **Inset trio (scene 9).** Two affected parents, unaffected child: AR impossible (aa x aa gives only aa), AD requires both Aa. Correct.
- **Pedigree P3 (attempt 1).** I-1 affected male x I-2 affected female; II-1 unaffected daughter, II-2 affected son, II-3 affected daughter; II-3 x II-4 (unaffected male, married in); III-1 affected son, III-2 unaffected daughter.
