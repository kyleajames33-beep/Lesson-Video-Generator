### 2. drawing y = kx
> Two points fix a straight line, and for direct variation, one of them is free. It's the origin, because zero Australian dollars is zero New Zealand dollars. So you only need one more point. Pick an easy input, well away from the origin, because a longer line is easier to rule accurately. Three hundred Australian dollars works. One point one times three hundred is three hundred and thirty, so plot three hundred, three hundred and **thirty**. Then add a check point. A hundred Australian dollars gives a hundred and ten, and that should sit on the same **line**. Rule from the origin through both points. If the check point misses, there's a slip somewhere, so find it before you use the **graph**.

### 5. worked example: k from a graph
> Here's the method on its own, going backwards from a graph. Remember last video's car? Here's its **graph**, a straight line from the origin through five hours, three hundred kilometres. Find k from the graph alone, and use the graph to find how long the car takes to travel two hundred and ten kilometres. Step one, k is the gradient, rise over run from the origin. Three hundred divided by five is **sixty**, so d equals sixty t. Step two, read the graph. Start at two hundred and ten on the distance axis, go across to the line, then down. It reads three and a half **hours**. Step three, check with the equation. Two hundred and ten divided by sixty is three point **five**. The slip is reading k as three hundred, the height of the point. You have to divide by the run, **five**.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why only one more point: direct variation always passes through the origin (2). Why a check point: every point on the line fits y = kx, so a miss shows a slip (2). Why the reading direction matters: each point pairs an input with an output, so start on the given number's axis (3). Why steeper is dearer: k is dollars per gram (4).
