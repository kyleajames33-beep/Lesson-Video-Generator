### 2. two processes, two lists
> Air. Soil normally has tiny air spaces between its grains, and flooding fills them with water. That's what waterlogged means. To see why it matters, remember that a plant runs two processes. Photosynthesis uses light energy, carbon dioxide and water to make glucose and oxygen. Respiration uses glucose and oxygen to release energy for the cell. So a plant has two sets of requirements, and they're not the same list.

### 3. gases, day and night

Every living cell respires, day and night. In bright light, photosynthesis outpaces respiration, so a leaf takes in carbon dioxide overall and gives out oxygen. In the dark, only respiration runs, so the plant takes in oxygen. Gases pass through stomata, tiny pores, mostly on the underside of the leaf.

### 4. roots need soil air
> Now the roots. They're underground, so they can't photosynthesise, and very little oxygen reaches them from the leaves. So root cells take their oxygen from the air spaces in the soil. Flood the soil, and that oxygen has to diffuse through water instead, about ten thousand times more slowly than through air. The root cells run short of oxygen, so aerobic respiration slows, and they make far less ATP.

### 5. wilting in water
> And roots need that ATP. Remember, root cells pump mineral ions in, against their concentration gradient, and that takes energy. Without enough ATP, the pumps slow down, the root cells are damaged and start to die, and damaged roots take up far less water. Meanwhile, the leaves keep losing water. So the plant wilts, standing in water. That's why overwatering can kill a pot plant.

### 7. water's four jobs
> What about water? It's more than a reactant in photosynthesis. Water dissolves mineral ions and sugars and carries them around the plant. It fills the cells and presses them firm, so soft leaves and stems stand up. And as it evaporates from the leaves, it cools them. In fact, most of the water a plant takes up evaporates from its leaves, and only a tiny share is used in photosynthesis.

### 13. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Gases: carbon dioxide for photosynthesis (carbon for glucose); oxygen for respiration, in every living cell, day and night. Bright light (photosynthesis faster than respiration): net CO₂ in, O₂ out. Dark: only respiration, so O₂ in, CO₂ out. Gases pass through stomata (pores, mostly under the leaf) and root surfaces.
- Roots get oxygen from soil air spaces. Waterlogging fills them, so root cells get less oxygen, make less ATP, pump in fewer ions, are damaged and take up less water: the plant wilts.
- Dark: no photosynthesis, so no new glucose; respiration spends the stores.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (2 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Soil air and waterlogging (scenes 2, 4, 5).** Well-drained soil holds air in pores between particles; flooding fills them with water. The diffusion coefficient of oxygen is about 0.2 cm² s⁻¹ in air and about 2 × 10⁻⁵ cm² s⁻¹ in water, so diffusion in water is about 10,000 times slower ("about ten thousand times more slowly"). Root cells become hypoxic, switch to anaerobic respiration (less ATP), active ion uptake falls, root cells are damaged or die, and root water uptake falls (reduced hydraulic conductance, aquaporin closure with cytoplasmic acidosis), so shoots wilt despite saturated soil. "very little oxygen reaches them from the leaves": in most dryland crops internal oxygen transport to roots is small without aerenchyma. Correct.
- **Gas exchange (scene 3).** Photosynthesis needs CO₂ (the carbon in glucose); respiration needs O₂, continuously in living cells. In bright light, photosynthesis exceeds respiration: net CO₂ uptake and O₂ release; in darkness only respiration: net O₂ uptake, CO₂ release. Stomata are mostly on the lower epidermis in many dicot leaves ("mostly on the underside"). Correct.
- **Darkness (scene 6).** No photosynthesis without light; respiration continues using stored sugars and starch; prolonged darkness depletes carbohydrate reserves and the plant can starve. Correct.
- **Water (scene 7).** Reactant in photosynthesis; solvent and transport medium for ions and sugars; turgor for support; evaporative cooling. About 97 to 99 per cent of the water absorbed by roots is lost by transpiration, and a small remainder is retained or used in metabolism. Revision 2: the science check found no source for a fixed photosynthesis share, so the narration says "only a tiny share is used in photosynthesis" and the screen shows no number.
- **Elements (scenes 8, 9).** Glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ contains only C, H and O. Proteins contain nitrogen (and some sulfur, not mentioned); DNA contains nitrogen and phosphorus; ATP contains phosphorus; chlorophyll contains nitrogen and a central magnesium atom ("Magnesium sits at the centre of every chlorophyll molecule"). Plants take up nitrogen mainly as nitrate (also ammonium, not mentioned) and phosphorus as phosphate. Potassium ions move into and out of guard cells to open and close stomata. Correct.
- **Fertiliser (scene 9).** N-P-K figures on Australian labels give the percentages of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. Example digits "10 : 4 : 6" on screen only, tagged "example only". Correct.
- **Attempt 1.** Magnesium deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis in older leaves first (magnesium is phloem-mobile and remobilised to young leaves); less chlorophyll reduces light absorption and photosynthesis; reduced growth. Correct.
- **Attempt 2.** Rice develops aerenchyma (continuous gas spaces) in roots and stems that conduct oxygen from shoots to roots, allowing aerobic respiration in flooded soil; most dryland crops lack extensive aerenchyma and suffer root hypoxia when flooded for days. Correct.
