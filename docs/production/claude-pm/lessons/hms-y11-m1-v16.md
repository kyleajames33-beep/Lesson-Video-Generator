### 2. the Ottawa Charter's five action areas
> The **Ottawa Charter**, from nineteen eighty-six, is the framework Australian health promotion is built around. Its five action areas aren't five kinds of campaign. They're five levels where you can act, each answering a question. Build healthy public policy: what are the rules and prices? Create supportive environments: what are the settings people live, learn and play in? Strengthen community action: who decides? Develop personal skills: what do people know and can do? And reorient health services: what is the health system for?

### 3. reorient health services, precisely
> Reorienting health services is the one students get wrong. It doesn't mean building more hospitals. It means changing what the system is for and how it works: funding prevention, moving services into the community, and redesigning them so the people with most need can actually use them. A youth health service with no appointment, no referral, no cost, and a waiting room that doesn't feel like a hospital, is reorienting a service. A bigger emergency department isn't.

### 4. three strategies: how, not where
> Alongside the action areas, the Charter names three strategies. The action areas are where you act. The strategies are how. **Enable** means giving people the resources and opportunity to reach their potential, like free transport where there's no bus. **Mediate** means reconciling competing interests, like a council's revenue, residents' sleep and alcohol harm. **Advocate** means making health a public priority. Mediation explains why health promotion is slow. Almost every structural change has someone who loses by it.

### 7. partnership, and who holds the power
> Community action is the row most often claimed and least often done. Remember the participation ladder: informed, consulted, partner, and lead, where the community has control. Consultation asks. Control decides. Real **partnership**, shared decisions and resources, changes outcomes for three reasons. Relevance: a community knows its own barriers, so it solves the real problem, not the visible one. Trust and reach: young people who avoid institutions still talk to someone they know. And durability: a program a community owns keeps going when the funding round ends.

## Practice question and feedback

1. (11a, 3 marks.) Helmet law.
   Model: healthy public policy (1 mark); two actions in other areas, each correctly placed and youth-specific (for example safe separated cycleways (supportive environments); school bike-safety and helmet-fitting sessions (personal skills); a youth group co-planning routes (community action); helmets provided free through school health services (reorient or enable)) (2 marks). Classic mistake crossed: advocacy named as an action area.
