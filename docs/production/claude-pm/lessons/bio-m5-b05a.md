### 1. hook (a graze heals)
> Scrape your knee, and within days new skin has filled the gap. Each new skin cell needs the same instructions as the cells beside it, 46 chromosomes, the DNA packages. Copying DNA is only half the job, because the copies still have to be shared out. How does a cell divide so that both new cells keep every chromosome, an exact copy of the parent's?

### 2a. chromosomes, sets and pairs
> Your DNA is packed into structures called chromosomes, each one, before copying, a single long DNA molecule wound up with proteins. A human body cell has 46 of them, in two sets of 23, one set from each parent. A set is one of each kind of chromosome. So every kind appears twice, apart from X and Y in males, and the two of one kind, one from each parent, are called a homologous pair.

Visual: the camera settles on a nucleus. A row of 23 short bars in plum draws on, then a second row of 23 in teal beneath it, with the tags "set from one parent" and "set from the other parent" and a counter that ticks to 23 on each row and then to "46 chromosomes". A small grey tag beside the rows reads "in males, X and Y are different and only partly match (23rd pair)" (screen only; the narration says "apart from X and Y in males"). On "A set is one of each kind" a bracket sweeps along the plum row. The board then compresses to a small corner icon and the model cell fades in (introduced fully in 2b); the model's four bars slide to the centre: long plum, short plum, long teal, short teal. On "every kind appears twice" a scribble circle lands on the two long bars and a bracket joins them with the tag "homologous pair: a matching pair, one from each parent"; then the same for the two short bars. A one-second hold on the paired bars.

### 2b. diploid, haploid and the model cell
> A cell with two sets of chromosomes is called diploid. A cell with one set, like a human sperm or egg, is haploid. To keep the picture clear, we'll follow a model cell with just four chromosomes, two sets of two.

Visual: two cell outlines side by side: the left shows both plum and teal bars with the tag "two sets: diploid", the right shows only plum bars with the tag "one set: haploid (a sperm or egg)". ### 2c. why copy first
> Here is the problem. A chromosome can't be cut in half, because each half would carry only half of its instructions. So if a cell just shared out its chromosomes, each new cell would get only half of them, and a skin cell needs both sets. The DNA has to be copied first, as you saw in the DNA copying video.

Visual: inside the model cell the four bars sit in the nucleus. On "can't be cut in half" a ghost cut line crosses the long plum bar; it splits into two grey halves, each with a flat grey cross and the tag "half the instructions each". On "just shared out its chromosomes" the four bars are shared two and two between two ghost cells, each cell tagged "only half the chromosomes: a skin cell needs both sets" with a grey cross (no pair-by-pair labels). On "has to be copied first" the ghost cells fade and a copy icon (two overlapping page outlines) lands beside the nucleus. A one-second hold.

### 2d. what the copy looks like, and how to count
> After copying, each chromosome is made of two identical copies, joined at a point called the centromere. Each copy is called a chromatid, and the two together are sister chromatids. It is still one chromosome, so to count chromosomes, count the centromeres, not the chromatids.

Visual: the four bars each duplicate with a soft pulse into two identical long threads side by side (long doubled threads, an elongated X, same colour) and snap together at a cream centromere disc. A scribble circle lands on one centromere with the tag "centromere", then the two threads light in turn with the tags "chromatid" and "chromatid", and a bracket reads "sister chromatids: identical copies". On "count the centromeres" a pen taps each disc in turn with a ticking counter to 4, and the tag "4 centromeres = 4 chromosomes (8 chromatids)" writes on in amber. The four doubled threads hold for one second. (The copies are drawn long and thin until prophase; the compact X shape appears only after the coil in 4a.)

### 3. when the copying happens (interphase)
> When does the copying happen? In interphase, the long stage between divisions. It is not a rest. The cell grows and makes what it needs, and in a part called S phase it copies all of its DNA. So by the time mitosis starts, every chromosome already has two chromatids. Mitosis is the much shorter stage that shares those copies out. DNA is never copied during mitosis itself.

Visual: the cycle ring (`CycleRing`) draws on in the centre: a long arc "interphase" (about nine tenths of the ring), a short arc "mitosis" and a very short arc "cytokinesis", with a travelling dot (TravelingDotOnPath) that circles the ring. The model cell sits small inside the ring and changes with the dot: at the start of the interphase arc it shows four single threads; on "copies all of its DNA" the dot passes a marked sub-arc "S phase: DNA copied" and the four threads become four long doubled threads (the copies side by side, joined at the disc) with a small tick. On "not a rest" three tiny icons pulse along the arc (growth arrows, a gear, a tick) labelled "grows, makes what it needs". On "every chromosome already has two chromatids" a scribble circle lands on one doubled thread. On "much shorter stage" the mitosis arc lights and the tag "shares the copies out" writes on. On "never copied during mitosis" a flat grey cross lands on the mitosis arc with "no copying here". ### 4a. prophase
> Mitosis has four steps. In prophase, the long, thin chromosomes coil up into short, thick X shapes, which makes them easier to move, and the nuclear envelope, the membrane around the nucleus, breaks down. Spindle fibres, thin protein threads, grow from the two ends of the cell.

Visual: the model cell board returns large; a step chip "1 prophase" lands in the stage strip. The four long doubled threads shrink and thicken as a coil animation (about two seconds) into compact X shapes on "coil up", with a tag "coils up: easier to move". The dashed nuclear envelope breaks into dashes and fades on "breaks down", with a scribble circle and the tag "nuclear envelope" and a small grey tag "animal and plant cells". Two small spindle poles appear at the left and right ends of the cell and fibres draw out from them on "grow from the two ends". A one-second hold.

### 4a2. metaphase
> In metaphase, fibres from the two ends attach to every chromosome at its centromere, one end to each chromatid, and the chromosomes line up across the middle. Each chromosome lines up on its own, not next to its homologous partner.

Visual: the chip "2 metaphase" lands. Fibres from the left pole attach to the left chromatid of each X and fibres from the right pole to the right chromatid, each attachment ticking at the centromere (a scribble circle on one centromere with the tag "fibres from opposite ends, one end to each chromatid"). A one-second hold on the aligned cell.

### 4b. anaphase
> In anaphase, the centromeres split and the fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids to opposite ends of the cell. As soon as a chromatid is separated, it is called a chromosome. So for a short time the whole cell holds twice as many chromosomes as before. But look at each end: it holds one copy of each of the four chromosomes the cell started with. Each end has both sets again.

Visual: chip "3 anaphase" lands. On "the centromeres split" each cream disc halves with a tiny pop, the X shapes open into two single bars, and on "the fibres shorten" the fibres draw in and the two groups travel apart, each bar leading with its centromere (about three seconds). A coded counter at the lower right ticks to "8 chromosomes" on "twice as many", then splits to "4 + 4" as the groups settle. A scribble circle lands on the left group, then the right group, on "each end", with the tags "a copy of each of the four chromosomes" and "4 chromosomes: the same four as the parent". Each end shows long plum, short plum, long teal and short teal, so both homologues are visibly at both ends.

### 4c. telophase and cytokinesis
> In telophase, the chromosomes reach the ends and uncoil into long thin threads again, and a new nuclear envelope forms around each group. Cytokinesis, the splitting of the cytoplasm, often begins while the nuclei form. In an animal cell, the membrane pinches in. Now there are two cells, each with both sets.

Visual: chip "4 telophase" lands. ### 5. tracking the numbers
> Let's track the numbers in our model cell, which starts with four chromosomes. After copying, it still has four chromosomes, because we count centromeres, but now eight chromatids. In anaphase, each chromatid becomes a chromosome, so the cell briefly holds eight, four at each end. After the cell divides, each new cell holds four. A human cell goes the same way: 46 chromosomes, and after copying 92 chromatids, because twice 46 is 92. In anaphase there are 92 chromosomes, 46 at each end, then 46 in each new cell. The number is never halved. The copying doubled the DNA first, and the division shared it back out.

### 6a. why the two new cells are identical
> Why are the two new cells identical? Because sister chromatids are copies of the same chromosome. The fibres pull the two chromatids of every chromosome to opposite ends, so each new cell gets a copy of every chromosome the parent had, both sets. The cell also checks that every chromosome is attached from both ends before anaphase starts.

Visual: the model cell returns at metaphase, large, with the tracker parked small at the lower right. On "copies of the same chromosome" the two chromatids of the long plum X light together and a bracket with "=" appears between them. On "the two chromatids of every chromosome to opposite ends" arrows run from each X to opposite poles and a mini-split shows one copy of each bar landing in each end with ticks; a bracket on one end reads "both long bars and both short bars are here" so the student sees both homologues arriving at each end. One X has a fibre missing from the right side: the gate stays shut, a small clock turns and the tag "waits" shows; the missing fibre attaches, the gate lifts and the tag "attached from both ends: go". The cell then proceeds to anaphase.

### 6b. accurate, not perfect
> Mitosis is accurate, not perfect. A mistake made while copying the DNA is a mutation. It ends up in one of the two new cells, and then in every cell that divides from that one. Rarely, the two chromatids of one chromosome fail to separate, so one new cell gets an extra chromosome and the other is one short. Checks catch many of these, but not all.

Visual: a short strip of the model cell. On "a mistake made while copying" a tiny base letter flips on one chromatid (a pulsing grey tag "mutation: a change in the base order") and the marked chromatid is one of the two copies of that chromosome. On "It ends up in one of the two new cells" only one of the two daughters carries the marked bar; the other is clean. On "every cell that divides from that one" that daughter divides and the mark is carried to its two cells only, with the tag "one new cell, then its descendants". Then on "fails to separate" a replay of anaphase has the long plum X not opening: both chromatids travel to the right, leaving the left end with three bars and the right with five, with the tags "3 chromosomes" and "5 chromosomes" and a grey caution mark (no cross, no red). A coded checkpoint magnifier sweeps across and flags the problem on "Checks catch many of these", then the flag fades half-way on "but not all". A one-second hold.

### 6c. what mitosis is used for
> Because every new cell gets the same chromosomes, mitosis is how an organism grows from one cell, how a graze heals, and how worn-out skin and gut lining are replaced. It is also how yeast and an amoeba make identical offspring. Bacteria have no nucleus, so they split by fission without mitosis.

Visual: four small coded icons come up in a row, each with a short tag and a miniature of the model cell dividing in its corner: "growth" (a tiny embryo ball growing), "repair" (the hook's skin patch, closed), "replacement" (a skin surface shedding its outer layer with new cells rising), "making identical offspring" (a yeast cell with a bud, the amoeba from the single-celled video recalled small, and a cutting with roots), the last with the tag "nucleus divides by mitosis". ### 8a. feedback, item one
> One. After copying, the cell still has 8 chromosomes, because the copies stay joined at their centromeres, but it now has 16 chromatids. ### 10. key notes (quiet, about 75 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- A chromosome is DNA wound with proteins: one long DNA molecule before copying, two identical ones (sister chromatids) after. It is still one chromosome.
- A set is one of each kind of chromosome. Diploid: two sets, one from each parent (human body cell: 46). Haploid: one set (gamete: 23).
- DNA is copied in interphase (S phase), before mitosis. Count centromeres, not chromatids.
- Prophase: chromosomes coil, the nuclear envelope breaks down (animal and plant cells), the spindle forms. Metaphase: chromosomes line up in the middle, each on its own. Anaphase: centromeres split, sister chromatids are pulled apart and each is now a chromosome. Telophase: new nuclei form. Cytokinesis: the cytoplasm splits.
- Each new cell gets a copy of every chromosome the parent had (both sets), so, barring errors, it matches the parent. Human: 46, then 92 chromatids, 92 chromosomes in anaphase, 46 in each new cell. Not halved.
- To explain: copying makes sister chromatids, fibres from opposite ends attach, a checkpoint confirms this, they are pulled apart.
- Uses: growth, repair, replacing worn-out cells, asexual reproduction (yeast, cuttings). A copying mistake is a mutation in one new cell and its descendants. Rarely sister chromatids fail to separate: one cell gains a chromosome, one loses one.
- Order: I P M A T (interphase comes before mitosis).

## Memory aid and assumptions

Aids: **"Count the centromeres, not the chromatids"** (bank, "Sister chromatids vs homologous chromosomes", `module5-memory-aids-2026-10-11.md`) and **IPMAT** (bank, "IPMAT for mitosis").
Assumptions checked:
- IPMAT. The letters give the order only; the keyword under each letter carries the meaning, so the aid is shown after the stages are explained (scene 9), not before. Interphase is not part of mitosis (the bank notes this): the learner copy says aloud "I is interphase, which comes before mitosis and is where the DNA is copied. Then the four steps", and the screen tag repeats it, so a listener does not learn five stages of mitosis. Cytokinesis has no letter and is not a fifth stage of mitosis; it begins during late anaphase or telophase and is not after the T in every cell, so the spoken "Then cytokinesis splits the cell" is a simplification that the notes qualify ("follows telophase" is replaced by "then" on screen; scene 4c says it often begins while the nuclei form). The aid says nothing about prometaphase, which the HSC course does not name; a student who meets it later is not contradicted. The keyword is "coils" (what 4a said), which covers the chromosomes only; the nuclear envelope breakdown and the spindle are separate events named in 4a.
- "Count the centromeres, not the chromatids." ("Arms" was dropped: it means one chromatid here but, in textbook diagrams, an X has four arms, two per chromatid, so counting arms of six X shapes gives 24, not 12.) Holds when each chromosome is drawn with one centromere, which is every diagram the lesson uses. It also works for anaphase pictures (each separated chromatid has its own centromere). It fails if a diagram hides a centromere, if a fragment has none, or if chromosomes overlap; none of the lesson's pictures do this. It does not tell a student whether a chromosome is replicated: the notes say "two chromatids joined at a centromere".
- "Mitosis keeps the number" is the headline, but the learner copy never says "keeps the diploid number": it says "the same chromosome number as the parent cell" so it stays true for a haploid parent cell. The closing line to meiosis ("one set, not two") is true for gamete formation in most animals and plants.
- The "sharing" metaphor (copy, share, split) was taught in the single-celled video and is recalled in scene 3. It maps copy to S phase, share to mitosis and split to cytokinesis. It breaks for bacteria, where the sharing is not mitosis; scene 6c says so.
