### 1. hook (one powder, two jobs)
> Sprinkle baking soda into hydrochloric acid, and it **fizzes**, and the solution becomes less acidic. Now sprinkle it into sodium hydroxide solution. **No fizz** this time, but the solution becomes **less basic**. The same white powder calms an acid and calms a base. So how can one substance act as a base in one reaction, and as an **acid** in another?

### 2. run one equation backwards
> Start with an equation you already know: ammonia in water. Water gives a proton to ammonia, making ammonium and hydroxide. Now run it **backwards**. Ammonium can give that proton back, so it's an **acid**. Hydroxide can take it, so it's a **base**. Every acid-base reaction makes a new acid and a new base, ready to swap the proton **back**. The proton keeps swapping both ways, which is why this equation has the double arrow.

Visual: the board clears; `m5x-ammonia-bottle-v1.png` small at the right. The strip writes "NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)"; the C01 hop plays once (water amber, ammonia violet). On "backwards" the teal token hops back from NH₄⁺ to OH⁻ along the same path, dashed. On "acid" NH₄⁺ gets an amber outline; on "base" OH⁻ gets a violet outline. On "back" both hops loop slowly, forward and reverse, with a tag on the ⇌ "proton can go either way". Hold about 1 second.

### 3. conjugate pairs
> Each new species is linked to the one it came from. When an acid gives away its proton, what's left is its **conjugate base**. When a base takes a proton, it becomes its **conjugate acid**. Conjugate means joined. So ammonium and ammonia are one **conjugate pair**, and water and hydroxide are the other. Partners differ by **exactly one proton**, so the charge changes by one, and they sit on **opposite sides** of the equation.

Visual: the strip stays; the loop stops. On "conjugate base" an amber bracket draws above the strip from H₂O to OH⁻, tagged "acid → conjugate base (−H⁺)"; on "conjugate acid" a violet bracket draws below from NH₃ to NH₄⁺, tagged "base → conjugate acid (+H⁺)". On "conjugate pair" the two tags shrink to pair labels "pair: H₂O / OH⁻" and "pair: NH₄⁺ / NH₃". On "exactly one proton" the H counts and charges of each pair light in turn (H₂O to OH⁻: 2 H to 1 H, 0 to −1; NH₃ to NH₄⁺: 3 H to 4 H, 0 to +1). On "opposite sides" each bracket's two ends pulse across the arrow. Hold about 1 second.

### 4. worked example: follow the proton
> Try a harder one: hydrogen phosphate in water. H P O four two minus, plus water, gives H two P O four minus, plus hydroxide. Follow the proton. Water **loses** one and becomes hydroxide, so water is the acid, and hydroxide is its conjugate base. Hydrogen phosphate **gains** one, so it's the base, and dihydrogen phosphate is its conjugate acid. Always one proton per pair: the conjugate base of H two S O four is **H S O four minus**, not sulfate.

### 5. conjugate strength explains the arrows
> Pairs also explain the arrows from the strength video. Look at the conjugate bases. Chloride, from hydrogen chloride, has almost **no pull** on a proton, so it almost never takes one back. That's why the reaction goes all the way, with a single arrow. Acetate, from acetic acid, has a **real pull**, so it keeps taking protons back from hydronium, and you get the double arrow. So the stronger the acid, the **weaker** its conjugate base.

### 6. amphiprotic: baking soda explained
> Now the baking soda. The ion that does the work is **hydrogen carbonate**, H C O three minus. In the acid, it **takes** a proton from hydronium, making carbonic acid, which breaks down into water and the carbon dioxide you saw fizzing. In the sodium hydroxide, it **gives** a proton to hydroxide, making carbonate and water, so there's no gas. A species that can give or take a proton, depending on its partner, is **amphiprotic**. Amphi means both, like an amphibian, at home on land and in water.

### 7. spotting one: the middle of a proton chain
> How do you spot one? Line the species up in a **proton chain**, each one proton apart. Carbonic acid, hydrogen carbonate, carbonate. Phosphoric acid loses its three protons one at a time: dihydrogen phosphate, hydrogen phosphate, then phosphate. The ends do one job: phosphoric acid can only give, and phosphate can only take. ### 9. water's own role

But it's a tiny equilibrium: only about **two water molecules in a billion** have given a proton away at any moment. And because each transfer makes one of each, they're always in **equal numbers**. Equal amounts of hydronium and hydroxide is what **neutral** means, and the next video links that to pH seven.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Conjugate pair: two species on opposite sides of an equation that differ by exactly one H⁺. Acid − H⁺ = conjugate base; base + H⁺ = conjugate acid (charge changes by 1). NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻: pairs NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻. One H⁺ at a time: H₂SO₄/HSO₄⁻, not H₂SO₄/SO₄²⁻.
2. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base: Cl⁻ does not take H⁺ back (→); CH₃COO⁻ does (⇌).
3. Amphiprotic: can donate or accept a proton, depending on its partner (the middle of a proton chain): H₂O, HCO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻.
4. NaHCO₃(s) → Na⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq). As an acid: HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H₃O⁺. As a base: HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ + OH⁻.
5. Water self-ionises to a tiny extent: H₂O + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. Neutral means equal amounts of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻.
