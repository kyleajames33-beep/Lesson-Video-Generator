### 1. hook: one tendency behind every test
> Last time, every metal that reacted in our tests did the same thing. It turned into positive ions, and to do that, it lost electrons. So a reactive metal is one that gives its outer electrons away **easily**. Potassium gives them up in an instant, while gold hangs on to them. But why should one metal let go more easily than another? A big part of the answer is inside the **atom**.

### 2. down a group: further out, easier off
> You've met part of the answer with the alkali metals. Going down Group one, from lithium to sodium to potassium, each atom has one more shell, so the outer electron sits further from the **nucleus**. The core charge stays the same, so that distant electron is held less tightly. The first ionisation energy, the energy to pull it off, falls from five hundred and twenty, to four hundred and ninety-six, to four hundred and nineteen kilojoules per **mole**. Less energy needed means the metal loses that electron more readily, so reactivity rises down the **group**.

### 3. across a period: a tighter grip
> Now go across Period three: sodium, magnesium, aluminium. Sodium fizzes hard in cold water, magnesium barely reacts, and aluminium shows no change at **all**, partly because of a surface layer we'll come back to. Across the period, each atom has one more proton, but its outer electrons stay in the same shell. So the core charge rises, from one, to two, to three, and the atom gets **smaller**. Both changes pull the outer electrons in tighter, so they're harder to **remove**.

### 5. the whole job, not the first step
> Sodium loses one electron, magnesium loses two, and aluminium loses three. Each extra electron comes off a smaller, more positive ion, so it costs more. Add up the whole job, and sodium needs four hundred and ninety-six kilojoules per mole, magnesium about two thousand two hundred, and aluminium over five **thousand**. The whole job gets harder across the period, and that's a big part of why reactivity falls from sodium to **aluminium**.

### 6. electronegativity tells the same story
> The syllabus names one more property, electronegativity. That's how strongly an atom pulls on the electrons it shares in a bond. Line up the metals in the activity series, and their electronegativity climbs as you go down the list, from potassium at zero point eight two, up to gold at two point five **four**. An atom with a weak pull on shared electrons also holds its own outer electrons weakly. Both numbers come from the same thing underneath, how tightly the nucleus grips its outer **electrons**.

### 7. the chain, and a trap
> So here's the chain to use in an answer. A larger atom, or a lower core charge, means a weaker grip on the outer electrons. That gives a lower ionisation energy and a lower electronegativity, so the metal loses electrons more easily, and it's more **reactive**. In short, weaker grip, easier loss, more **reactive**. Notice what the chain doesn't mention. It isn't about how many electrons a metal has. Gold has seventy-nine electrons, potassium has only nineteen, and gold is far less **reactive**.
