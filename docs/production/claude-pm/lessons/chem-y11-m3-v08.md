### 1. hook: one tendency behind every test
> Last time, every metal in our series did the same thing in every test. It turned into positive ions, and to do that, it lost electrons. So a reactive metal is one that gives its outer electrons away **easily**. Potassium gives them up in an instant, while gold hangs on to them. But why should one metal let go more easily than another? The answer is inside the **atom**.

Visual: opens on m3-v07's final frame. On "lost electrons" the tag "M → Mⁿ⁺ + electrons" pulses. On "easily" the Next chip clears and the amber chip "easier to remove electrons?" rewrites as "reactive = loses outer electrons easily" (teal). On "Potassium" the K tile glows; on "gold" the Au tile glows grey. On "atom" the periodic outline shrinks to the top right and the board centre clears.

### 2. down a group: further out, easier off
> You've met part of the answer with the alkali metals. Going down Group one, from lithium to sodium to potassium, each atom has one more shell, so the outer electron sits further from the **nucleus**. The core charge stays the same, so that distant electron is held less tightly. The first ionisation energy, the energy to pull it off, falls from five hundred and twenty, to four hundred and ninety-six, to four hundred and nineteen kilojoules per **mole**. Less energy needed means the metal loses that electron more readily, so reactivity rises down the **group**.

Visual: the shell row: Li, Na, K shell diagrams at true relative size, each outer electron orange; on "nucleus" radius arrows draw from nucleus to outer electron (tag "atomic radius"). Core-charge badges "+1" on all three (grey tag "same core charge"). On "mole" m1-v14's bars rise under each atom: "520", "496", "419 kJ mol⁻¹" (gloss chip "first ionisation energy: energy to pull the outer electron off"). On "group" an arrow "more reactive" points down the row; tag "as in alkali metals video".

### 3. across a period: a tighter grip
> Now go across Period three: sodium, magnesium, aluminium. Sodium fizzes hard in cold water, magnesium barely reacts, and aluminium shows no change at **all**, partly because of a surface layer we'll come back to. Across the period, each atom has one more proton, but its outer electrons stay in the same shell. So the core charge rises, from one, to two, to three, and the atom gets **smaller**. Both changes pull the outer electrons in tighter, so they're harder to **remove**.

### 5. the whole job, not the first step
> Sodium loses one electron, magnesium loses two, and aluminium loses three. Each extra electron comes off a smaller, more positive ion, so it costs more. Add up the whole job, and sodium needs four hundred and ninety-six kilojoules per mole, magnesium about two thousand two hundred, and aluminium over five **thousand**. The whole job gets harder across the period, and that's why reactivity falls from sodium to **aluminium**.

### 6. electronegativity tells the same story
> The syllabus names one more property, electronegativity. That's how strongly an atom pulls on the electrons it shares in a bond. Line up the metals in the activity series, and their electronegativity climbs as you go down the list, from potassium at zero point eight two, up to gold at two point five **four**. An atom with a weak pull on shared electrons also holds its own outer electrons weakly. Both numbers come from the same thing underneath, how tightly the nucleus grips its outer **electrons**.

### 7. the chain, and a trap
> So here's the chain to use in an answer. A larger atom, or a lower core charge, means a weaker grip on the outer electrons. That gives a lower ionisation energy and a lower electronegativity, so the metal loses electrons more easily, and it's more **reactive**. In short, weaker grip, easier loss, more **reactive**. Notice what the chain doesn't mention. It isn't about how many electrons a metal has. Gold has seventy-nine electrons, potassium has only nineteen, and gold is far less **reactive**.

### 8. where trends stop
> These trends explain the patterns down a group and across a period. But when you compare metals from different parts of the table, like iron and copper, the grip isn't the whole story. What happens to the ions once they're in water matters too. That's why the activity series is built from measured reactions, not worked out from atoms **alone**.

### 9. aluminium's disguise
> Now back to aluminium. Its place in the series says it should be more reactive than zinc, yet the foil hardly reacted in acid. Aluminium reacts with oxygen in the air so readily that its surface is instantly covered by a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide. That layer sticks tightly and keeps water and acid away from the metal. Protection by an oxide layer like this is called **passivation**. It's why aluminium window frames and drink cans last for **years**.

### 10. same metal, new surface
> Here's a demonstration your teacher may show. Aluminium foil sits in blue copper sulfate solution, and almost nothing **happens**. Then some sodium chloride, ordinary salt, is stirred in. Chloride ions break up the oxide layer, and soon the foil fizzes, the mixture gets hot, and brown copper **forms**. Same metal, same copper ions. Only the surface changed, so the slow start was never low **reactivity**.

### 11. tendency against what you see
> So keep two ideas apart. A metal's underlying tendency to lose electrons comes from its atoms, and it sets the metal's place in the series. What you see in a test also depends on the surface, the size of the piece and the conditions. Sodium's dull tarnish slowed its start in water, and aluminium's oxide hides its tendency far more **completely**.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. A metal reacts by losing its outer electrons. The weaker the nucleus's grip on them, the more reactive the metal.
2. Down a group: larger atom, same core charge, lower ionisation energy, more reactive. Across a period: higher core charge, smaller atom, more energy to form the ion, less reactive (Na > Mg > Al).
3. Lower electronegativity goes with higher reactivity (K 0.82 to Au 2.54): both show a weak grip.
4. Passivation: a thin, tough oxide layer (Al₂O₃) keeps reagents off aluminium, so it looks less reactive than its place in the series.
