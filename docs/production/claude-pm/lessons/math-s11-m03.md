### 1. hook: four payments, no interest
> Four hundred and eighty dollars for these headphones, or four payments of a hundred and twenty. Take them home today, and pay no interest. Four times a hundred and twenty is exactly four hundred and eighty, so it costs nothing **extra**. That's the promise of buy now, pay later. You take the item today, and pay for it in equal parts. It sounds free. But the companies that run these plans make money somehow. So what does buy now, pay later really **cost**?

Visual: opens on m02's final frame exactly. On "pay no interest" a grey chip "0% interest" writes under the card. On "extra" row "4 × 120 = 480" (teal, 44 px) and a grey chip "nothing extra?" with "?" ringed amber. On "equal parts" grey chip "buy now, pay later: take it today, pay in equal parts". On "make money somehow" a small grey "$?" chip floats above the card. On "cost" heading "Buy now, pay later: the real cost" writes on.

### 2. how a plan works
> Here's how a typical plan works. You pay the first instalment today, a hundred and twenty dollars. An instalment is one of the equal payments. Then the plan takes the next three every two weeks, from your card or bank account. So it's paid off in six **weeks**. Watch what you still owe. Four eighty, three sixty, two forty, one twenty, **nothing**. The instalments add back to exactly the price, and there's no interest. It's still a kind of credit, because you get the item before you've paid for it. So who pays the provider? Mostly the shop. It pays the provider a small cut of every sale, because people buy more when they don't have to pay all at once. That's worth knowing. These plans are built to make spending **easy**.

### 3. where the cost hides: fees
> The plan can still cost you, through fees. Some plans charge an account fee, a fee for each month you owe them money. Our example plan charges six dollars a month. This plan is open across two months, so that's twelve dollars, and the total cost is four hundred and ninety-**two**. Other plans charge nothing extra if you pay on time. But almost all of them charge a late fee, a fee each time a payment is missed. In our example plan, it's ten dollars. Miss one payment, and the total becomes five hundred and **two**. So the total cost is the price, plus any account fees, plus any late fees. Fees differ between plans, and they can change, so read the plan before you **tap**. And "no interest" doesn't mean no extra cost. Fees aren't interest, but you still pay **them**.

### 4. how big is the extra?
> Is twelve dollars a lot? Compare it with the price, just like a percentage change. Twelve divided by four eighty is two and a half per **cent**. With the late fee, the extra is twenty-two dollars, and twenty-two divided by four eighty is about four point six per **cent**. One missed payment nearly doubled the extra **cost**. That's why paying on time is the whole **game**. On a plan with no account fee, paying on time really does cost nothing extra.

### 5. a flat fee bites a small purchase
> Now try the same plan on a sixty-dollar t-shirt. Four payments of fifteen dollars. The account fees are still twelve dollars, because they're per month, not per dollar. So the extra is twelve on sixty, twenty per **cent**. Miss one payment, and it's twenty-two dollars extra on a sixty-dollar shirt, nearly thirty-seven per **cent**. The fees don't shrink when the price does. So the same plan is a much worse deal on small **purchases**. On a twenty-dollar phone case, one ten-dollar late fee is half the price **again**.

### 6. several plans at once
> One plan on its own can look easy. The trouble often starts with several. Say you've got three plans running at once: the headphones at a hundred and twenty a fortnight, shoes at forty-five, and a concert ticket at thirty. That's a hundred and ninety-five dollars every two **weeks**. If you only have a hundred and fifty spare each fortnight, after rent and food, the payments don't fit. Something gets missed, and the late fees **start**. Since June twenty twenty-five, these providers need a credit licence and have to check that a plan suits you, and missed payments can show on your credit report. But the simplest check is yours. Add up every plan's payment, and compare it with what you've got **spare**.
