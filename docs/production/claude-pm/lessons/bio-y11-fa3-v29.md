## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (3 Nov). The science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Magpies eat beetles (1, 2).** Australian magpies (Gymnorhina tibicen) forage on the ground for insects including beetles and their larvae. Correct.
- **Ticks on kangaroos, feeding for days (3).** Ixodid ticks attach and feed on blood for several days; kangaroos and wallabies are common hosts. Correct.
- **Grass and dandelions compete for water, light and nutrients (5).** As v25. Correct as a general statement.
- **Eucalypt allelopathy (6).** Several eucalypt species (for example river red gum and some others) have leaf litter and leachates that inhibit germination and growth of understorey species in experiments; field bareness also reflects shade, litter depth and water competition. "Certain eucalypts" follows site b1. Revision 2 (brief 163): the bare patch is no longer the evidence; scene 6 names the controlled comparison (leaf-soaked water against plain water) and uses the softer "slow or stop".
- **Native bee pollination (7).** Many native bees feed on nectar and pollen and transfer pollen between flowers of one species. "Drinks its nectar" is correct; most bees also collect pollen as food (not spoken).
- **Epiphytic orchids (8).** Epiphytic orchids anchor on bark and take water and minerals from rain, air and debris, not from the host's tissues. Correct.
- **Cuckoo brood parasitism (9, 10).** Site SA Q2 (b): "the host is harmed because it raises another species' young while its own chicks starve". In many cuckoo species the chick ejects host eggs or chicks; "its own chicks starve" follows the site and is true for some species. Science check to confirm, or fallback "and loses its own chicks" (shorter).
- **Remora and shark (13a).** Usually classed as commensalism; some sources note possible small drag costs. The site's model accepts +/0. Correct at Year 11 level.
- **Kangaroos competing within a mob in drought (5).** Site b2's example. Correct. "Usually fiercer" follows b2 (intraspecific competition is generally the stronger, because niches overlap completely); science check to confirm the hedge.
- **Mycorrhizae (11).** Ectomycorrhizal fungi on eucalypts exchange host sugars for water and mineral nutrients (especially phosphorus). The mutualism-parasitism continuum (the fungus can become a net cost to the host under some conditions, for example high nutrient supply or stress) is established in the literature; site b1 states it. "Some of these fungi take more than they give" is hedged. Science check to confirm.
- **Lichen (13a).** Fungus and alga (or cyanobacterium) mutualism. Correct.
- **Mistletoe (13c).** Australian mistletoes (Loranthaceae) are hemiparasites: they photosynthesise but take water and minerals from host xylem through a haustorium. "Draws water and dissolved nutrients from the tree's xylem" is correct; the hemiparasite detail is an author note only.
