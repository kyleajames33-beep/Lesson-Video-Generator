## Decisions

- **Anchor: the disagreement (d11's frame).** The mean says 7.2, the median and mode say 7.5. Eleven of the twenty students slept more than the mean. The video's question is which number to report, and when the answer would change.
- **Drag simulation (visual surprise).** Drag the 3 h dot to 7 h: the mean triangle slides to 7.4 while the median line doesn't move. Drag it to 1 h: the mean drops; the median still stays. The median "stays with the middle"; the mean "follows the extremes".
- **Merits, one measure per scene.** Mean: uses every value, so it's sensitive to them all; best for roughly symmetric data with no extreme values; the only one that recovers a total (mean × how many). Median: not pulled by extreme values; best for skewed data (house prices, incomes, screen time); it ignores how extreme the extremes are. Mode: the only measure for nominal categories; the most popular size or item; there may be none, or several, and it need not be near the middle.
- **Shape and centre (Year 12 z01's wording).** Symmetric: the mean and median are close. Positively skewed: the mean is pulled towards the tail, above the median (screen time 218.8 against 200). Negatively skewed: the mean is below the median (sleep 7.2 against 7.5).
- **Real-world uses that fit or mislead.** Fit: median house prices in news reports; the mode for stocking sizes; a mean battery life from a symmetric test of one phone model. Mislead: a café's "average pay" dragged up by the manager; a "mean shoe size of 8.7" (no such shoe); comparing one group's mean with another group's median (site lesson07, trap 01).
- **Plain meanings at first use.** Merit ("a strength, a reason to use it"); extreme value ("a value far from the rest"; the word "outlier" is used once here and defined by rule in d18); resistant ("not pulled much by extreme values").

### 1. hook: which one is typical?
> The mean says seven point two hours. The median and the mode both say seven and a half. Eleven of our twenty students slept more than the mean. So if a newspaper asked how much a typical Year 11 student sleeps, which number would you give? Each measure has strengths, and each can mislead. The right choice depends on the data, and on what the reader needs to know. So when is the mean, median or mode the right measure of **centre**?

### 2. the mean follows the extremes
> Start with the mean. Its strength is that it uses every value. That's also its weakness, because every value includes the extreme ones. Watch the three hour night. Drag it up to seven hours, and the mean climbs to seven point four. Drag it down to one hour, and the mean falls to seven point one. The median doesn't move at all. It only cares which value sits in the middle, not how far away the extremes are. So the mean follows the extremes, and the median stays with the **middle**. The mean is the right choice when the data are roughly symmetric, with no extreme values, or when you need a total, because mean times how many gives the total **back**. Saying the mean is always best, because it uses every value, is a **slip**.

### 3. the median for skewed data
> The median's strength is the opposite. It isn't pulled by extreme values, so it's the better choice when data are skewed. Screen time is positively skewed. Its mean is about two hundred and nineteen minutes, but its median is two hundred, because the few heavy users at the top pull the mean up. House prices are the classic case. In a street of seven houses, six sell for between six hundred thousand and seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars, and one mansion sells for two and a half million. The mean is about nine hundred and twenty-nine thousand, more than all six ordinary houses. The median is six hundred and eighty thousand, a typical **house**. That's why news reports quote the median house price. The median's weakness is that it ignores how extreme the extremes are.

### 4. the mode for categories and popularity
> The mode is the only measure that works for every kind of category. A club ordering hoodies wants to know which size to stock most of. Last season it sold eight small, fifteen medium, twelve large and five extra large. A mean size makes no sense, and even with numbered sizes, a mean shoe size of eight point seven fits nobody. The mode, medium, is exactly what the club **needs**. Sizes do have an order, so a median size exists, but it still wouldn't tell the club what sells most. The mode's weaknesses are that there may be no mode, or several, and the most common value isn't always near the middle.

### 5. shape decides
> Shape tells you which to choose, and why. When data are symmetric, the mean and the median sit close together, so either works, and the mean is usually given. Stretch a tail out to the right, and the mean is pulled towards the tail, above the median. Stretch it left, and the mean falls below the median, just like our sleep data, seven point two against seven point five. If you're not sure of the shape, compare the mean and the median. A big gap between them is a warning. So skewed data, or data with extreme values, call for the median. Symmetric data suit the **mean**.

Visual: stage change (simulation 2): d09's shape slider (grey chip "d09: the tail tells the skew") now with a teal triangle and a violet line under the outline. Symmetric: the two markers sit together (hand tag "symmetric? mean"). On "mean" (bold) a summary strip: "symmetric: mean ≈ median | positive: mean > median | negative: mean < median".

### 6. uses that fit, and uses that mislead
> Out in the real world, measures of centre are used well and badly. Fits. A real estate report quotes the median house price, because prices are skewed. Fits. A shop stocks the mode, the most popular size. Fits. A phone review quotes the mean battery life from a hundred test runs that were roughly symmetric. Misleads. A café advertises an average pay of over a thousand dollars a week, when most staff earn under seven hundred, because the manager's pay drags the mean up. Misleads. Comparing one group's mean with another group's median, which isn't a fair comparison. Whenever you choose, say why, using the shape of the **data**.
