## Script and shots

### 1. hook (coral and seal)
> On a few nights each year, many of the corals on the Great Barrier Reef spawn together. They release bundles that float up and break apart, setting free millions of tiny eggs and clouds of sperm in the open sea. Now think about a harbour seal on a rocky shore. In one year she makes a single egg, and months later, a single pup. So why would one animal release millions of eggs, while the other gets by with one? By the end, you'll be able to explain it in an exam answer.

Visual: painted coral spawning plate; the pink and cream bundles lift off the polyps and rise on "float up" (coded bundle particles drift up from the plate), then on "break apart" each bundle splits into separate pink egg dots and smaller pale sperm dots, and on "millions of tiny eggs" the freed egg cloud swells. On "harbour seal" a push-through transition to the seal and pup plate; on "a single egg" one dot, on "a single pup" the pup highlights with a scribble circle. Heading writes on: "Why do these two animals need such different numbers of eggs?"

### 2. fertilisation and the meeting problem
> Start with the job both animals are doing: getting a sperm to meet an egg. A sperm cell and an egg cell are gametes. Last time you saw that a gamete carries one set of chromosomes, so one set plus one set makes a full two sets. Put simply, each gamete carries half of the instructions for building a new animal. When a sperm joins an egg, that's called **fertilisation**. It makes one new cell, the **zygote**, with the full set of instructions, and it will grow into the new animal. The hard part is getting the two to meet. An egg can't move. A sperm can only swim a short way. And both dry out quickly. Animals solve this in two main ways, depending on where the gametes meet.

Visual: coded schematic cells on a plain board. On "one set of chromosomes" each gamete shows a single chromosome strip (colour-coded sperm and egg), and "one set + one set = two sets" writes on as a B1 callback chip. On "half of the instructions" each strip fills half of an instruction bar. On "joins" the sperm travels in and the halves complete into one full bar inside a single circle, labelled "zygote" on that word. On "can't move" the egg gets a small pin icon, on "short way" a short swim path draws with a scribble limit, on "dry out" a drying-droplet doodle. On "two main ways" the board splits into two columns headed "outside the body" and "inside the female" (empty, filled by the next scenes).

### 3a. external fertilisation (where, and why water)
> The first way is **external fertilisation**. The sperm and egg meet outside the bodies of both parents, and it usually happens in water. Water matters for two reasons. It stops the gametes drying out, and sperm need it to swim. Corals release their gametes into the sea. Frogs do it in a pond, and salmon do it in a river, where the female lays her eggs in the gravel and the male releases sperm over them.

Visual: the board shows only the water reasons first. On "water matters for two reasons" it reduces to a droplet (no drying) and a swimming sperm icon (needs water), each drawn as its reason is spoken. Then three plates in turn, one animal at a time, each with its own named-part cue. Coral: spawn cloud on "sea". Frog pond plate (existing): shown as a wide shot of the pond with the spawn mass on "pond" (the push to the clasping pair is held for 3b). Salmon plate: egg dots appear in the gravel on "lays her eggs in the gravel", a sperm trail from the male on "releases sperm over them". Three small place chips (sea, pond, river) collect on the rail.

### 3b. external fertilisation (meeting in the same place and time)
> But water alone isn't enough. The gametes have to be in the same place at the same time, or they never meet. Many corals solve this by spawning together on the same few nights, so the gametes are crowded into one patch of sea. Salmon follow the scent of their home river, so they gather in the same place, and they return at the same time of year. Frogs gather in the same pond, and the male **clasps** the female, so the sperm are released right over the eggs.

Visual: three plates again, one at a time, with the rail chips from 3a. Coral: a moon icon and "same few nights" chip on "same few nights", the cloud crowds into one patch. Salmon: on "scent" a coded scent-wave line runs from a river-mouth icon to the fish, and the fish converge on one gravel bed on "same place"; a calendar chip on "same time of year". Frog: the camera pushes to the clasping pair on "clasps", then the coded sperm trail lands on the spawn on "right over the eggs".

### 4. external fertilisation (the odds)
> Even so, the odds are poor, and there are two kinds of loss. First, many eggs and sperm are lost before they meet. Currents sweep gametes away, hungry animals eat them, and the sperm are spread so thin in all that water that many never find an egg. Second, most of the eggs that are fertilised, and the young that grow from them, are eaten or die before they grow up. A female Chinook salmon lays around four thousand to fourteen thousand eggs. With that many, a few still make it. So the rule is: water, timing and huge numbers.

Visual: a coded field of hundreds of gamete dots over the water, labelled "loss 1: gametes swept away or eaten before they meet". On "sweep" the dots shear sideways with a current arrow; on "eat" small fish silhouettes remove dots; on "spread so thin" the dots fade apart. The dots that do pair up become small egg icons, labelled "loss 2: fertilised eggs and young are eaten or die before growing up", and most of those fade over the next beat (no fertilisation percentage is shown). Counter chip "4,000 to 14,000 eggs" ticks on at the number. On "a few still make it" only a handful stay lit and gain a scribble circle. Rule line writes on: "water + timing + huge numbers".

### 5. internal fertilisation
> The second way is **internal fertilisation**. The male places sperm inside the female's body, and the egg is fertilised there. The gametes now meet in a moist, protected space, so they can't dry out and they can't be eaten. The sperm only has to swim a short distance. Almost every egg is fertilised, so she needs far fewer eggs. Males still make plenty of sperm. The number that matters is eggs. This is the way mammals, birds and reptiles do it, and it lets them breed on dry land, far from standing water. Some animals that live in the sea, like sharks, use it too. The cost is that the parents usually have to find each other and mate, and that takes time and energy.

Visual: split-screen answer to scene 4. The lizard plate (female bearded dragon, cut-away inside showing the reproductive tract) sits on dry sand. A coded sperm path draws inside the female on "inside the female's body", ending at the egg with a short arrow; a scribble circle on "short distance". Beside it the dot field from scene 4 dims, with the chip "far fewer eggs". On "Males still make plenty of sperm" a small sperm cloud appears beside a male silhouette and stays, with the note "eggs are what differ". A small shark icon pops on "like sharks". On "find each other" two animal silhouettes approach and meet (the cost).

### 6. the trap: where they meet versus where it grows
> Here's a trap. A hen lays an egg with a hard shell, outside her body. So is that external fertilisation? No. The sperm has to reach the egg inside the hen, before the shell forms around it. Fertilisation is about where the sperm and egg meet, not where the young one grows. So a hen fertilises internally, then lays the egg, and the chick develops outside her. A seal does both inside her, and a frog does both outside the body. Two different questions, two different answers.

Visual: the hen cut-away plate (existing): a coded sperm travels up the oviduct to the yolk on "has to reach the egg inside the hen"; the shell draws on after it with a "then the shell forms" note on "before the shell forms". Then a grid writes on, columns "Where do the gametes meet?" and "Where does the young grow?", rows hen, seal, frog. Each row fills on its animal name using small plate crops (hen and nest, seal and pup, frog spawn): hen = inside / outside the body; seal = inside / inside; frog = outside / outside. The hen row is circled in amber as the trap.

### 7. why numbers and care trade off
> Every parent has a limited supply of energy and time. In mammals and birds, the young begin their life inside or beside the mother, so she can protect and feed them. Protected young survive more often, so each one is worth more of her energy. A salmon spends hers on thousands of eggs and then has nothing left to give her young. A seal spends hers on a single pup, which she carries through months of pregnancy, then feeds with milk and guards for a few weeks. That time, energy and protection a parent gives to help its young survive is called **parental investment**. So the usual pattern is: many eggs and little care, or few young and a lot of care. Some reptiles bury their eggs and leave, so this is a trend, not a rule. External fertilisation suits animals that live and breed in water.

Visual: a short cause strip first: "mammals and birds: inside or beside mother" icon, arrow to "protected young survive more", arrow to "worth more energy", drawn on those phrases. Then a seesaw balance (coded, hand-drawn) with a fixed "energy and time" bar splitting between "making eggs" and "caring for young". Salmon plate left (bar mostly on eggs: thousands of dots), seal plate right (bar mostly on care: one pup). On "limited supply" the bar draws; on "thousands of eggs" the left side fills; on "nothing left to give her young" a crossed-out parent icon appears beside the salmon; on "single pup" the right side fills. The seesaw tilts on "or". "usual pattern" carries a small "usual, not always" tag. On "Some reptiles bury their eggs and leave" a small buried-egg doodle with a parent walking away appears beside the seesaw, labelled "trend, not a rule". On "suits animals that live and breed in water" a tick over the water icon.

### 8. worked-explain (building an HSC answer)
> Let's build a model answer. The question: explain why fish that fertilise externally release far more eggs than mammals that fertilise internally. The command word explain means give reasons, joined by because. First, the cause. Fish release far more eggs because many eggs and sperm in water are swept away or eaten, and most of the young that form are eaten or die before they grow up. Next, the effect. Fish cope because huge numbers of eggs make up for these losses, so some young still survive. Then the contrast. Mammals need far fewer eggs because the sperm is placed inside the female, where it is protected, so almost every egg is fertilised. And they have only a few young because each one gets a lot of parental investment. Finish with a link: a parent has limited energy, so it makes many eggs with little care, or few eggs with a lot of care.

Visual: the question card writes on top, "explain" scribble-underlined with margin note "reasons, linked with because". The answer builds as a chain of cards, one per stage, each a full written sentence the student could copy, landing on its cue word. CAUSE: "Fish release far more eggs because many eggs and sperm in water are swept away or eaten, and most of the young that form are eaten or die before they grow up." EFFECT: "Fish cope because huge numbers of eggs make up for these losses, so some young still survive." CONTRAST: "Mammals need far fewer eggs because sperm is placed inside the female, where it is protected, so almost every egg is fertilised. They have only a few young because each gets a lot of parental investment." LINK: "A parent has limited energy, so it makes many eggs with little care, or few eggs with a lot of care." The word "because" is circled on the cause, effect and contrast cards as each lands, and the linking word ("so", "or") is underlined in a second colour. Each card carries the mini plate icon (salmon, seal). Earlier cards shrink to the top rail and stay.

### 8b. a bad year (recovery)
> That pattern has a consequence. Imagine a storm year that kills nine in every ten of the young alive, for a seal colony and for a cod population. Cod fertilise externally and breed year after year, and a large female cod can lay millions of eggs. In both groups the adults **survive** and breed again next year, so the question is how many young each adult adds. Before the storm, ten seal mothers have ten living pups, and each large female cod's eggs have already become thousands of living young fish. After the storm, the seals have one pup left for the ten mothers, and each cod has hundreds of young fish left. Next breeding season, each seal mother adds only about one pup, so the colony **rebuilds slowly**. Each cod adds millions of eggs again, so many more young fish can follow, and the population can **rebuild faster**, as long as enough adults survive.

Visual: the seesaw from scene 7 returns small at the top. Two rows: seal colony (ten seal icons, ten pup dots) and cod population (coded fish icons, a cluster of young-fish dots per cod labelled "thousands of living young each", kept apart from the egg cloud). On "storm year that kills nine in every ten of the young alive" both rows lose nine in ten living young (red slashes, labelled "extra storm loss"), equal for both, leaving "1 pup for 10 mothers" and "hundreds of young fish per cod"; the adults stay lit on "survive". On "how many young each adult adds" an "adds next breeding season" chip appears per row: seal "about 1 pup", cod "millions of eggs again". On "rebuilds slowly" the seal row's refill dial creeps; on "rebuild faster" the cod row's dial fills quickly. "as long as enough adults survive" appears as a small amber note.

### 12. key notes (quiet)
- Fertilisation is the joining of a sperm and an egg to make a zygote, the first cell of a new individual.
- External fertilisation happens outside the bodies, usually in water (for example corals, frogs, salmon). It needs water, good timing and huge numbers of eggs, and the parents usually give little care.
- Internal fertilisation happens inside the female. It protects the gametes, so far fewer eggs are needed (males still make plenty of sperm). It suits life on land. Mammals and birds usually have few young and give more parental investment.
- Fertilisation is where the gametes meet, not where the young develop (a hen fertilises internally, then lays the egg). Few young per parent means slow recovery after a bad year, because each adult adds only a few young.
- Memory aid (large): External: water and numbers. Internal: a mate and protection.

## Memory aid and assumptions

Aid: **"External: water and numbers. Internal: a mate and protection."** (bank) plus the trap line "where they meet, not where it grows".
Assumptions checked:
- "Numbers" means eggs, and is a trend, not a rule. Most external fertilisers release many gametes, but the learner copy never says internal animals always have few young. The care link is restricted to mammals and birds ("usually"), and scene 7 says some reptiles bury their eggs and leave, so it is a trend, not a rule. "Water and numbers" leaves out timing; scene 11 says "and good timing" aloud.
- "Water" holds for the named animals. Internal fertilisation also occurs in water (sharks, whales, dolphins), so the script says so in scene 5 and the aid never claims internal is land-only.
- "A mate" covers the behaviour and effort of mating. It is not a claim that every internal fertiliser needs a long courtship.
- The trap line is correct for birds and reptiles (internal fertilisation, shell added afterwards). It is not offered for amphibians.
- Neither strategy is called better. The aid pairs each with its conditions.
