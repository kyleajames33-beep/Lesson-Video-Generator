## Decisions

- **Main teaching move: same depth, different share.** In the same conditions and time, the colour change reaches about the same mean depth from each face of every cube. That depth is most of a small cube but only a shell of a large one. The video makes students predict this from SA:V first (scene 3), then shows it in the cut cubes (scenes 6 and 7). The key result is said in plain words, "the same depth in the same time" and a bigger share of the small cube changed, which is also the site's Band 6 precision point ("The acid does not diffuse faster into the small cube"). Revision 2 does not infer an equal instantaneous speed from one endpoint (science check item 4).
- **The investigation (site Activity 1 and short answer 2).** Pink phenolphthalein agar, prepared by the teacher with a little dilute sodium hydroxide, cut by the teacher as a demonstration (FA2 root decision 29) into cubes of side 1, 2 and 3 cm and placed in dilute hydrochloric acid for 10 minutes. Phenolphthalein is pink in alkali; as the acid moves in and neutralises the alkali, the pink fades. The colour-change boundary is what is measured; it is an indicator endpoint, not proof that acid has gone no further or that the colourless gel is acidic (science check item 2). The independent variable is cube size (and so SA:V); the dependent variable is the percentage of each cube's volume that turned colourless in 10 minutes; the site's dye-into-cubes figure and Predict walk-through use the same set-up.
- **Prediction first (syllabus verb "predict").** Scene 3 states the hypothesis before any method, in the site's walk-through form: "If SA:V is higher, then a greater percentage of the cube will change colour in 10 minutes, because there is more surface for each bit of inside and the centre is closer." The prediction beat (3 s, root decision 7) asks which cube will have the biggest share changed.
- **What to measure (scene 5 and 6).** Students measure the width of the pink core on the cut face (easier and more accurate than the thin colourless rim), then work out the depth ((side − core) ÷ 2) and the percentage of the volume changed: whole volume minus core volume, as a percentage of the whole. One worked example (the 2 cm cube) on the calculation card, the other rows quietly on the table (AGENTS rule: one reasoning stage at a time).
- **Illustrative data (scene 7).** Three cubes of each size. Core widths: 1 cm cubes 2, 2, 2 mm (mean 2 mm); 2 cm cubes 12, 13, 11 mm (mean 12 mm); 3 cm cubes 22, 21, 23 mm (mean 22 mm). Depths 4, 4, 4 mm; 4, 3.5, 4.5 mm; 4, 4.5, 3.5 mm: about 4 mm on average in each group. Percentage changed, computed from the mean core widths: 99%, 78%, 61% (labelled "from mean core width"). Grey tag "illustrative data" on every number. A depth of about 4 mm in 10 minutes is plausible for school concentrations (Numbers to verify).
- **Timing the end (scene 5).** At the end of the 10 minutes the teacher lifts the cubes out with forceps, rinses off the surface acid, blots them and cuts them straight away, with the same timing for every cube. Rinsing removes external acid but does not instantly stop diffusion or neutralisation inside the gel (science check item 3), so the control is standard timing and prompt measurement. Attempt 2 does not test it (it stays in teaching and notes).
- **Into and out of (scene 9).** The content point says "into and out of". The video adds one spoken sentence for the reverse version (soaked coloured cubes placed in water lose the biggest share of their colour first from the small cubes), shown on screen as a small inset, not run as a second method.
- **Consistency, validity and improvements (scene 8).** Consistency (FA1 v21's "reliable: consistent when repeated" as a grey gloss): the three cubes of each size agree in this illustrative set; three repeats alone do not establish reliability. Validity: controlling everything but size supports this test; the model limits stay (science check item 5). Improvements: cut cubes accurately (a 2 cm cube cut 1 mm too long on every side has about 16% more volume); measure the core on a photo with a ruler in view (the pink edge can be blurry); add a larger cube to extend the pattern.
- **Model limits (scene 9).** The agar is not alive, has no membrane and real cells are about a thousand times smaller. The model shows the geometry, not the biology of the membrane. Revision 2 cuts the diffusion-time clause (science check item 6).
- **Risk table (scene 10).** Three spoken rows, each hazard, harm and control: dilute hydrochloric acid (irritates eyes and skin: safety glasses, rinse splashes with plenty of water); scalpel (cuts: only the teacher uses it, as a demonstration, cutting down onto a tile; FA2 root decision 29); indicator agar (contains phenolphthalein: never taste it; dispose of it as the teacher directs). Teacher-note rows, not shown: preparing the agar (the phenolphthalein indicator solution in ethanol is highly flammable and a suspected carcinogen; the teacher prepares and adds it, with no ignition sources; brief 107 check); the dilute sodium hydroxide used to make the agar alkaline; disposal of acid and agar; glass beakers. Shown table: only the row being spoken; earlier rows collapse to one-word grey chips (v05's and v21's rule). The school's current risk assessment and the product SDS decide handling, disposal and who cuts; the footer says so. The acid stays at the dilute 0.1 mol L⁻¹ value and is not increased without the local assessment.

### 2. the cubes are a model
> Each cube is a model of a cell, something simpler that behaves in the same way. The jelly is the cell's inside, its faces are the surface, and the acid plays the part of a material moving in by diffusion. As acid soaks in, it makes the pink fade. So after a set time, the colourless part shows how much of each **cell** has changed.

Visual: step rail lights "1 model". ### 5. the method
> Here's the method, as a teacher demonstration. Your teacher cuts the three cubes with a scalpel, checks each side with a ruler, and covers each cube with a hundred millilitres of acid in its own beaker. You start the **timer**. Then you measure the pink core, the centre that hasn't changed **colour**.

## Safety facts check (self-check, 2 Nov; science check to confirm)

- **Dilute hydrochloric acid, 0.1 mol L⁻¹.** At this concentration it is a low-hazard irritant to eyes and skin (more concentrated solutions are corrosive). Controls: safety glasses; rinse splashes with plenty of water; wipe spills. Revision 2 keeps 0.1 mol L⁻¹ (FA2 root decision 29); a 0.1 mol/L teaching method exists, but it is not NSW approval, so the footer points to the school's risk assessment and the product SDS. Do not increase the concentration without the local assessment.
- **Scalpel (Revision 2, FA2 root decision 29).** Student scalpel use with phenolphthalein agar was not cleared from an accessible current NSW entry (science check blocker), so the teacher cuts the cubes as a demonstration, cutting down onto a white tile with the blade covered when not in use. Students time the colour change and record. The school's current risk assessment and the product SDS decide handling, disposal and who cuts.
- **Indicator agar (phenolphthalein).** The indicator solution (phenolphthalein in ethanol) is highly flammable and is classified as possibly causing cancer (for one current school product, H225, H319, H350; codex-check 107 and brief 107). The teacher prepares the agar and adds the indicator and the dilute sodium hydroxide; students handle only set agar, where the indicator is very dilute. Learner controls: never taste the agar; dispose of it as the teacher directs. Revision 2: the teacher handles the agar (cutting, lifting out, rinsing); students do not handle it. If a school's assessment does not allow the indicator agar at all, the alternative is plain agar with food colouring soaked in water (the "outwards" version in scene 9), and the design changes the indicator lines (root decision).
- **Glass beakers.** Breakage; teacher note only.
- **Teacher notes (not in learner copy).** Agar preparation (heating, the indicator in ethanol with no ignition sources, the sodium hydroxide); disposal of acid down the sink with plenty of water if permitted locally; agar into the general waste as directed.

## Memory aid check

- **"Same depth, different share."** (Claude's.) Assumptions: every cube is in the same acid at the same temperature for the same time, so the colour-change boundary reaches about the same mean depth from every face, whatever the cube's size (true while the boundaries from opposite faces have not met; in the 1 cm cube they nearly meet, which is why it is 99% and not 100%). Revision 2: "depth" means the measured colour-change depth, about 4 mm on average; it is not used to claim an equal instantaneous speed. The same depth is a larger percentage of a smaller cube, which is the SA:V effect. Limits: the depth is not the same if conditions differ (attempt 2 shows this); in real cells the "depth" idea becomes the diffusion distance to the centre (v02). Said in scene 7 after the results are explained; supports new cases (A1: 2 mm into both cubes, different shares).
