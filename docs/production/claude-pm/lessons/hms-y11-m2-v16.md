### 2. six things learners bring
> Instruction doesn't land on a blank slate. It's filtered through what the learner already brings, and the syllabus names six things. Personality shapes how someone handles risk and being watched. Heredity sets body size and some abilities. Confidence decides whether they commit to an attempt. Prior experience can carry over, or get in the way, like a tennis serve interfering with a volleyball serve. **Ability**, a stable capacity like reaction time, is mostly inherited. And motivation decides how much attentive practice happens at all. A coach can't change heredity or ability, but confidence and motivation are very changeable, and they decide how much practice the rest ever get. And ability isn't **skill**. Skill is learned, and practice is what builds it.

### 3. three stages, and one exchange
> Every learner passes through three stages. Describe them by the problem the learner is solving. In the **cognitive** stage, it's what do I do? Errors are big, and attention is completely used up by the movement. In the **associative** stage, it's how do I do it better? Errors shrink, and some attention starts to free up. In the **autonomous** stage, it's what do I do with it? The movement runs almost by itself, and attention is free for the game. That exchange explains everything else. **Attention** is limited. A beginner can't watch an opponent, because the movement takes it all. And stages belong to skills, not people. You can be autonomous at a forehand and cognitive at a backhand volley on the same day.

### 4. placing a learner from evidence
> So how do you tell which stage someone's at? Not by how long they've played. Years of inattentive practice can leave someone associative. Use evidence. First, spare attention: ask them to call out a number mid-movement. If the skill falls apart, it isn't autonomous yet. Second, the error-detection test: after an attempt, ask how it was, before you say anything. A cognitive learner doesn't know. An associative learner knows it was wrong, but not why. An autonomous performer tells you what went wrong, and has usually already fixed it. Third, pressure. A player who's great at training and poor in matches is often associative, not lacking confidence. The fix is more quality practice, not a talk about belief.

### 5. coaching that reverses
> Now the puzzle. Coaching at each stage differs in kind, not just amount. A beginner needs a clear model, one or two cues, and frequent feedback, because they can't spot their own errors. An expert needs sparing feedback, because they spot errors better than you can. And where you point attention matters at every stage. Learners usually do better attending to the movement's effect, like the ball's path, than to their own body parts. A single body cue can still help a beginner with one key point. Her attention is on her elbow anyway, and the cue gives it something correct to attend to. But the nine-year shooter's movement runs automatically. "Watch your elbow" drags conscious control back onto it, and the conscious route is slower and clumsier than the automatic one. Say instead: aim for the back of the rim. Same intention, attention on the effect. So save technical changes for training, not the middle of a match.

### 6. recreational and elite athletes
> The process is the same for recreational and elite athletes. What differs is where they sit and what limits them. Recreational learners are mostly cognitive or associative, and their limit is how much they practise. They need clear models, encouraging feedback and early success, or they drop out before the skill becomes fun. Elite athletes are autonomous in their core skills, and their limit is the quality of practice. They need sparing, precise feedback and pressured, game-like work. And when an elite athlete deliberately changes a technique, performance usually drops first, because the automatic pattern has to be rebuilt from an earlier stage. So plan changes for the off-season, and don't abandon one after two bad weeks.
