### 2. three categories: what does it act on?
> The syllabus splits technology into three, and they act on different things. **Training innovations** change how training is delivered: virtual reality game scenarios, altitude or heat chambers, video analysis of an opponent, devices that resist or assist movement. They let athletes practise things that would be impossible, unsafe or too costly for real. **Equipment advances** act on the performance itself: shoes, track and pool surfaces, swimwear, bikes, protective gear. They change the mechanics directly. That includes assistive devices, like a running blade or an audible ball, which can decide whether someone competes at all. And **recording and monitoring**, like GPS units, heart rate monitors and video, act on the information coaches decide with. Monitoring doesn't make anyone faster. It improves decisions, and better decisions improve performance.

### 3. equipment, and where the line is drawn
> Equipment works through the biomechanics of v-seventeen. A shoe or track that returns more of the energy stored at each foot strike lowers the cost of the same speed: efficiency. Swimwear, bike position and clothing cut drag, which matters more the faster you go. Assistive devices improve force application, and above all, access. But equipment is also where sport draws a line, and the line is hard to place, because every generation's gear improves on the last. Governing bodies have restricted equipment when they judged it gave too big an edge. Three questions usually decide it. How big is the advantage? Is it available to everyone, or only to well-funded athletes? And does it change what the sport is testing? If the equipment supplies the performance instead of the athlete, people call it **technological doping**.

### 4. monitoring: more data isn't more information
> Monitoring changed training more than anything else, because it made invisible things countable. A GPS unit records distance, high-speed running and accelerations. Over weeks, that becomes a load chart: daily bars that bounce around, and a trend underneath. But four limits matter. First, validity and reliability, from v-two. Each device works out its numbers its own way, so two brands can give different values for one session. Compare within one system, and trust the trend. Second, it measures what's easy. Distance is easy to count. Decision-making and how an athlete feels are not, and what gets measured gets managed, so training drifts toward the numbers. Third, numbers shouldn't override what a coach can see. And fourth, privacy. Location and body data are personal, so for school-age athletes, consent and who holds the data matter.

### 5. access: a multiplier, not a substitute

### 6. did the technology cause it?
> The verb is examine, and the hardest judgement is cause. Back to the runner. Four things changed together, and one of them, another year of training, is easy to forget. Different evidence answers different questions. One athlete improving shows they improved, not why. Many athletes improving after a technology spreads shows something changed, but the best-funded athletes adopt first, so that's not proof. A controlled comparison of the same athletes with and without it shows the effect's size, the strongest evidence. And a lab measure of energy cost shows the mechanism is real, not that it wins races. So an examine answer runs: the category, the mechanism, the evidence, the limits including access, then a judgement: for whom, when, and instead of what.
