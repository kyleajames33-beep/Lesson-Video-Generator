### 2a. what DNA is made of (nucleotides)
> DNA is a long chain of small units called nucleotides. Each has three parts: a sugar called deoxyribose, a phosphate group, which holds phosphorus and oxygen atoms, and a nitrogenous base, the part that carries the letter. The sugars and phosphates link in a backbone, and the bases hang off it, in four kinds: A, T, C and G. The order of those letters is the information a cell stores.

### 2b. two strands and the pairing rule
> DNA has two strands twisted around each other, a shape called a double helix. The backbones run on the outside, and the bases point inward and meet, but not at random. A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G, because only those pairs fit together, like jigsaw pieces. This is complementary base pairing.

### 2c. direction (antiparallel)

### 3. why one strand is a template
> Because A only pairs with T, and C only with G, one strand fixes the order of the other. If one strand reads A, T, C, G, its partner has to read T, A, G, C. So each strand holds the information to rebuild its partner. Used like this, it is called a template, a pattern to copy from. And because hydrogen bonds are weak, the strands can be pulled apart without breaking either backbone, which strong covalent bonds, where atoms share electrons, hold together.

Visual: the ladder board shows a four-rung ladder, A-T, T-A, C-G, G-C. The top row of tiles pulses; the bottom row is erased on "one strand fixes the order of the other", leaving blank slots. ### 4a. the copying steps
> First, an enzyme, a protein that speeds up a reaction, called helicase unwinds the helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds, at a point called the replication fork. Each original strand is now a template. Second, loose nucleotides in the cell pair with the exposed bases, A with T and C with G. Third, an enzyme called DNA polymerase joins each new nucleotide to the last with covalent bonds, building the new backbone.

### 4b. half old, half new
> Now we have two new DNA molecules, called daughter molecules, each with one original strand and one new strand. That is why this is called semi-conservative replication. Semi means half, and conservative means kept. It could have gone another way: one molecule could keep both original strands, and the other be all new. It does not. The original is shared out, one strand to each. Half old, half new.

### 6a. when copying goes wrong: errors and repair
> Copying is accurate, but not perfect. Occasionally a wrong nucleotide is added. DNA polymerase checks each base and can back up to remove a mistake, and repair proteins check the new strand afterwards, so almost every mistake is fixed. One that survives is a mutation, a change in the base order.

Visual: a short ladder with a wrong tile (G opposite T) snapping in, glowing grey, with the tag "mismatch" and a scribble circle. A coded polymerase icon backs up one step and the wrong tile pops out ("proofreading: checks and removes"), the correct A drops in with a tick; then a second wrong tile slips past, and a coded repair icon sweeps along the strand and removes it ("repair proteins: check afterwards"). A one-second hold.

### 6b. when copying goes wrong: effects and inheritance
> Many mutations have no effect, some harm the cell, and a few are useful. A mutation passes to the cell that receives the changed DNA, and to every cell that divides from that cell. In a bacterium that splits in two, only one of the two new cells gets the changed copy. ### 10. key notes (quiet, about 75 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- A DNA nucleotide has three parts: a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base (A, T, C or G). Strong covalent bonds join the sugars and phosphates into each backbone, and the bases point inward.
- In DNA, A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and C pairs with G (three). This is complementary base pairing. The two strands are antiparallel: one runs 5' to 3' and its partner runs 3' to 5'.
- Replication: helicase separates the strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds. Each original strand is a template. Loose nucleotides pair with the exposed bases, and DNA polymerase joins them into a new strand with covalent bonds.
- Semi-conservative replication: each new DNA molecule has one strand it was copied from and one new strand. The first original strands are never copied twice; they are shared out. To explain accurate copying: weak hydrogen bonds let the strands separate and act as templates, the pairing rule decides each new base, and each daughter keeps an old strand.
- Accuracy comes from the pairing rule. Proofreading and repair fix most mistakes. A mistake that remains is a mutation. It passes to the cell that receives the changed copy and to every cell that divides from it, and in an organism that reproduces sexually it reaches offspring only through a cell that makes gametes.
- To trace a copy: pair A with T and C with G, label old and new, and run the new strand opposite its template. Aids: Apple in the Tree, Car in the Garage (in DNA). Half old, half new (old = the strand it was copied from).
