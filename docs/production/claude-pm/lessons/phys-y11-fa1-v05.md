## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "How do instantaneous and average speed and velocity differ?" The answer, built in order: both averages divide by the same time, but speed puts the distance on top and velocity puts the displacement on top, so a trip that reverses or returns gives two different averages (hook, 2 to 4); an instantaneous value describes one moment, which is what a speedometer shows (5); it is measured over a time interval so short that the speed hardly changes in it (6, 7); instantaneous velocity is that speed plus the direction of motion at that moment, so it can change even when the speed doesn't (5, 8).
- **The hook surprise (site hook, kept with its numbers).** A car laps a 2.0 km track in 80 s and finishes where it started: average speed 25 m/s, average velocity zero. ### 2. two averages, one time
> Both averages divide by the same time, eighty seconds. The difference is what goes on top. Average speed uses the distance, the whole length of road the car covers. So it's two thousand metres over eighty seconds, twenty-five metres per **second**. Average velocity uses the displacement, the straight arrow from start to finish. As the car comes back around, that arrow shrinks, and at the finish it's **gone**. That's why speed is a scalar, just a size, and velocity is a vector, a size with a **direction**. In symbols, v av equals delta s over delta t, where delta means "change in", so delta s is the change in **position**.

Visual: the lap replays slowly (about 6 s), synchronised. An amber trace grows behind the car with the odometer readout counting "distance 0 to 2000 m"; at the same time a navy arrow from the start line to the car stretches as the car goes up the far straight and shrinks as it comes back; the clock runs "0 to 80 s". On the third sentence the right column writes "average speed = total distance ÷ Δt"; on "second" "= 2000 m ÷ 80 s = 25 m s⁻¹" (the odometer readout glows). On "gone" the arrow shrinks to a dot on the start line, scribble-circled, displacement readout "0 m". Right column: "average velocity = Δs⃗ ÷ Δt = 0 ÷ 80 s = 0". On "direction" two small tags: "scalar: size only" beside speed, "vector: size and direction" beside velocity. On the last sentences the formula writes large in the right column: "v⃗av = Δs⃗/Δt", with grey glosses written under its parts on "position": "Δs⃗: displacement (change in position)", "Δt: time taken".

### 5. instantaneous speed and velocity
> So what does a speedometer show? Not either average. It shows how fast the car is going right now, at one instant. That's called instantaneous **speed**. The car speeds up, holds steady, slows to a stop to turn around, then speeds up again heading **west**. The reading changes all the time, and it's zero at the **turn**. The average, ten metres per second, is one number for the whole trip. Instantaneous velocity is that speed, plus the direction the car is moving at that instant. Heading east at twelve point five, the velocity is plus twelve point five metres per second. Heading west at ten, the speedometer still reads ten, but the velocity is minus **ten**.

### 6. measuring at one instant: shrink the interval
> But how do you measure speed at one instant, when an instant has no time in it? You measure over a very short interval instead. Here's a car speeding up. At three seconds, its speedometer reads six metres per **second**. Over the next two seconds, it covers sixteen metres, so its average is eight metres per second. Too high, because the car kept speeding up during those two **seconds**. Over the next one second, seven. Over a tenth of a second, six point **one**. Over a hundredth, six point oh **one**. As the interval shrinks, the average closes in on six, the instantaneous **speed**.

### 7. a light gate does it in the lab
> In a school lab, a light gate does this for you. A light gate shines a beam across a gap and times how long something blocks it. Here a card, ten point oh centimetres long, rides on a **trolley**. As it passes, it blocks the beam for point oh five oh **seconds**. The trolley moves one card length in that time. So its speed is point one oh oh metres over point oh five oh seconds, two point oh oh metres per **second**. Strictly, that's an average. But over such a short time the speed barely changes, so it's a good measure of the instantaneous speed. Add the direction the trolley is moving, and you have its instantaneous **velocity**.

### 8. steady speed, turning velocity
> Now back to the race track. Say the car holds twenty-five metres per second the whole way **round**. Here, and only here, the speedometer matches the average speed, because the speed never changes. ### 15. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Average speed = total distance ÷ time taken (a scalar). Average velocity v⃗av = Δs⃗/Δt, displacement ÷ time taken (a vector: give a direction or a sign).
2. They have the same size only for straight-line motion with no turning back. A trip that ends where it started has an average velocity of zero.
3. Instantaneous speed is the speed at one moment (a speedometer). Instantaneous velocity is that speed plus the direction of motion at that moment.
4. Measure an instantaneous value over a very short interval, for example speed = card length ÷ time the light gate's beam is blocked. Average speed is not the mean of the leg speeds.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the two averages differ: same time underneath, different top line; the distance only grows while the displacement cancels on the way back (2, 4, shown with the trace and arrow side by side). Why they match only without reversal (4, inset). Why a speedometer is not an average: it changes from moment to moment (5). Why a velocity can be negative when a speed is not: the direction convention (5). Why an instantaneous value is measured over a short interval: an instant has no time in it, and a shorter interval gives the speed less time to change (6, 7).
