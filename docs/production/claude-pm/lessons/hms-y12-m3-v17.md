### 2. what digital health is for
> **Digital health** means using information and communication technology to deliver, coordinate and record health care. It isn't really a technology. It's a response to a problem you've met before. Health in Australia is split across governments and sectors, so your information ends up in pieces: at the GP, the specialist, the pathology lab, the pharmacy, two hospitals and an aged care service. None of them can see the others' pieces. So tests get repeated because nobody can see the last result. Medicines get prescribed without the full list. Care gets dropped at the seams. And you become the one carrying the information, which fails exactly when you're unconscious, confused or unwell. Digital health tries to make a split system behave, from your side, like one system.

### 4. to what extent has it worked?
> So, to what extent has it worked? Judge it three ways. Does information follow the patient? Do clinicians actually use it? And has care improved? Registers work, telehealth stuck after the pandemic, and e-prescribing and messaging cut errors quietly. And the shared record exists for most people. But coverage is not use. A record nobody opens adds nothing. Uploading is uneven, so the summary has gaps, and an incomplete record can be worse than none, because it may be trusted as complete. Systems built separately still can't share information well, which is called poor **interoperability**. Slow searching puts busy clinicians off, and early privacy worries dented trust. So here's the graded judgement. It's succeeded where the job was narrow and had one owner, and only partly where it had to connect organisations never designed to share. The infrastructure is built. Real connection is partial.

### 5. challenges and opportunities, for individuals and organisations
> The syllabus asks about individuals and organisations, so answer for both. For individuals, the opportunities are seeing your own information, fewer repeated tests, safer care in an emergency, and telehealth for people limited by distance, mobility, work or caring. The challenges are privacy, especially for sensitive information, **digital exclusion** by age, income, remoteness, disability or language, and records read without context that worry rather than inform. For organisations, the opportunities are safer prescribing, better coordination at the seams, data for planning, and a workforce that reaches further. The challenges are the cost and disruption of setting it up, time in short consultations, keeping data secure, and making systems talk. And here's the catch. The costs fall on providers, but the benefits fall on the system and the patient. When that happens, adoption is slow, however good the idea.

### 6. what would make it work
> So what would make it work? Five conditions, and none is a new technology. Sharing has to be the default for every key document, not just some test results, because voluntary uploading guarantees gaps. Using it has to be faster than not using it. Common standards have to be built and enforced, so systems share meaning, not just documents. Trust has to be kept, with clear access rules, visible logs and real consequences. And people who can't go digital need another way in, or every gain goes to those who already had the most. Digital health has connected exactly as much as a deliberately split system lets it.
