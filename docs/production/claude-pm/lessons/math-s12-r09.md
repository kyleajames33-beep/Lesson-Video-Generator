### 2. per hour: a unit rate
> Pay for each hour is a rate. A rate compares two different kinds of quantity, here dollars and hours. The word per means for each, so dollars per hour means dollars for each hour. To find the pay for one hour, share the pay equally across the hours, because every hour of the shift is paid the same. Three hundred and thirty-six dollars across eight hours is forty-two dollars per **hour**. Two hundred and seventy dollars across six hours is forty-five dollars per **hour**. An amount for one of the second unit, like this, is called a unit rate. So Job B pays three dollars more for every hour you **work**.

### 3. a fair test: the same hours

### 4. ratio or rate?
> You've met ratios already. Cordial to water, one to four, compares millilitres with millilitres. The units are the same, so they cancel, and a ratio has no units. A rate compares different units, like dollars and hours, so the units can't cancel. They stay in the answer, and they tell you what the number means. Forty-five on its own means nothing. Forty-five dollars per hour is a **wage**. So here's a quick test. Can the units cancel? Then it's a ratio. Do they stay? Then it's a **rate**.

### 6. worked example: which printer is faster?
> Here's a comparison where the units save you. Printer A prints four hundred and twenty pages in seven minutes. Printer B prints a thousand pages in twenty minutes. Which printer is faster? For A, four hundred and twenty pages divided by seven minutes is sixty pages per **minute**. For B, a thousand divided by twenty is fifty pages per **minute**. So printer A is faster, by ten pages every minute. Now the slip. Divide the other way, minutes by pages, and B gets zero point zero two, while A gets zero point zero one seven, so B looks bigger. But those are minutes per page, the time for each page, so bigger means **slower**. Read the units before you decide which number wins.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why totals mislead: more hours make a bigger total (1). Why divide: sharing the pay equally across the hours gives one hour's pay (2). **Attempt 1 (scene 8; original, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A café job pays $171.60 for a 6.5-hour shift. A supermarket job pays $214.40 for an 8-hour shift. **Attempt 2 (scene 10; original, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "A netball goal shooter scores 48 goals from 60 attempts across 16 games. (a) Write the number of goals per game as a unit rate. Model answer: (a) 48 ÷ 16 = 3 goals per game. (b) 48 : 60 = 4 : 5. (c) Goals per game compares two different kinds of quantity (goals and games), so it has units; goals to attempts compares shots with shots, the same unit, so the units cancel and it has none. Classic mistake (crossed): "goals : games = 3 : 1".

## Memory aid check

- **"Same units: ratio. Different units: rate."** with key line **"Per means for each one: divide. Compare per one of the same unit."** Original. Meaning, as said in scene 7: when both quantities are measured in the same unit, the units cancel and the comparison is a ratio with no units; when they are different, the units stay and the comparison is a rate; a unit rate is found by dividing to get the amount for one of the second unit, and two rates are compared only when both are per one of the same unit. Assumptions checked: "same units" means the same kind of quantity in the same unit (millilitres with millilitres; shots with shots); quantities in different units of the same kind (cm and m) must be converted before a ratio is simplified, which r01 covers; "divide" gives the unit rate whichever way round, and the units then say which number is better (scene 6). It helps a new case: 3 L of paint for 24 m² is 8 m² per litre, a rate, because litres and square metres can't cancel.
- **Considered, not used:** "Rates have units, ratios don't" (true, but it doesn't give the test or the divide step).
