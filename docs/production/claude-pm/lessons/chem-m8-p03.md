## Decisions

- **Main question:** *Every well with silver nitrate went cloudy, even the one with no unknown in it. How do you plan, run and record qualitative tests so that each identity you report would stand up, and do it safely?* Heading on screen: "Unknown ions practical: would your answer stand up?"
- **Why a hook about a cloudy blank:** the dotpoint says "conduct qualitative investigations", and the route start says "set up controls". A control that catches a false positive shows why controls exist better than a definition. The cause is concrete and true for most Australian tap water (dissolved chloride, author note b), and it gives students a habit (distilled water rinse) with a reason. The same student and the same unknown C carry the whole worked investigation, so the hook pays off in scene 6 ("No chloride after all").
- **Positive and negative controls, named once each.** Site card 5 says "Use fresh portions, blanks and at least one confirmatory test". The video names the blank (negative control) and the known (positive control) with plain meanings, and turns them into one row per reagent: blank, known, unknown. This row is the lesson's memory aid (scene 11; author-written, see Memory aid check).
- **Variables for a qualitative investigation (s07 language).** Independent: which solution is in the well (blank, known or unknown). Dependent: what you observe. Controlled: drops of solution and of reagent (three each), the same reagent bottle, the wait before reading (read at once, and again after a minute), the background (black card for white solids, white tile for colours), distilled water for rinsing. This gives the s07 words a real meaning here without pretending the investigation has a numerical dependent variable.
- **Accuracy for an identity.** s07 defines accuracy as "how close is the result to the true value". For a qualitative result the true value is the true identity, so the video says "Here, the result is an identity, so accurate means it's the true one", and names how a student gets close (matching the known side by side) and how it is checked (the teacher's key at the end). Validity is about the method detecting only the ion it targets (acid choice, fresh portions, distilled water, a clear blank). Reliability is about repeats with fresh portions and another group's agreement.
- **The worked unknown is magnesium sulfate (unknown C).** It uses every step of the cation flowchart (no chloride solid, no sulfate solid, a white hydroxide that stays in excess, then a flame to rule out concentrated calcium), the blank flame and a known magnesium well, then the anion flowchart (no fizz, a white barium solid in acid, a clear acidified silver well). It differs from c03a's copper hook and c03b's copper sulfate closure, and it is low hazard. Concentrations: unknowns 0.1 mol/L (author note a); the acidified silver well gives no silver sulfate (author note c).
- **"One cation and one anion per bottle" is stated in the aim.** It lets the anion sequence stop once sulfate is found, and it is the condition c03a and c03b's flowcharts assume (author note f). Mixtures stay out of scope.
- **Limit kept in learner copy (necessary condition).** "No visible change means not detected": a very dilute ion can slip past (c03a's dilute lead and calcium caveats). Leaving it out would teach that "no precipitate" proves absence. One sentence in scene 8 and key note 4.
- **Lead in the risk table.** The syllabus list includes Pb²⁺, so a teacher's set of unknowns may include a lead salt. Lead(II) nitrate carries H360Df (may damage the unborn child; suspected of damaging fertility). The design takes the safest stated control, matching the p02 root decision for barium: the teacher prepares and dispenses the dilute lead and barium solutions; students use them by the drop on the well plate, with gloves and glasses; lead and barium waste goes to the labelled heavy-metal waste container. The site's Safety note omits lead (site correction 1).
- **Silver waste.** Silver residues go to the labelled silver waste container (silver is often recovered); lead and barium to heavy-metal waste; nothing down the sink. The route review's wording ("lead and silver waste into heavy-metal containers") is met: where a school keeps one heavy-metal container, silver goes there too (risk table footnote).

  (b) Tap water chloride: Australian drinking water typically carries tens of milligrams of chloride per litre (the ADWG aesthetic guideline value for chloride is 250 mg/L). A residual film of tap water in a well, with drops of 0.1 mol/L AgNO₃, has an ion product far above Ksp(AgCl) = 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ (for example 0.05 × 5 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵), so a faint white opalescence can appear. The narration says "a little chloride" and "faint white cloud" and does not claim every tap gives one.
  (c) Unknown C (MgSO₄, 0.1 mol/L) with silver: in the acidified well (3 drops sample, 3 drops 2 mol/L HNO₃, 3 drops 0.1 mol/L AgNO₃), [Ag⁺] ≈ 0.033 mol/L and total sulfate ≈ 0.033 mol/L, but at [H⁺] ≈ 0.67 mol/L most sulfate is HSO₄⁻ (Ka2 ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻²; free SO₄²⁻ ≈ 0.033 × 0.010 ÷ 0.68 ≈ 4.9 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L), so Q(Ag₂SO₄) ≈ 0.033² × 4.9 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 5.3 × 10⁻⁷, below Ksp(Ag₂SO₄) ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁵: no precipitate. Unacidified, Q ≈ (0.05)² × 0.05 = 1.25 × 10⁻⁴ could exceed it, which is a second reason (beyond carbonate) that the silver test is acidified. Not said.
  (d) Unknown C's cation results: no PbCl₂ or AgCl (no Ag⁺, Pb²⁺); MgSO₄ soluble (no sulfate solid); Mg²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Mg(OH)₂(s), white, insoluble in excess NaOH; no visible flame colour from a magnesium salt (c03a author note d); a concentrated Ca²⁺ sample would also give a white hydroxide, which the brick-red flame would show. Known: magnesium nitrate solution, 0.1 mol/L, run in the same rows.
  (e) Unknown E (A2): CaCl₂, 0.1 mol/L. With 1 mol/L H₂SO₄ mixed 1 : 1: [Ca²⁺] = 0.05; most sulfate is HSO₄⁻ at [H⁺] ≈ 0.55, so free SO₄²⁻ ≈ 0.5 × 0.010 ÷ 0.56 ≈ 8.9 × 10⁻³; Q ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ > Ksp(CaSO₄) 4.93 × 10⁻⁵: a white precipitate, often slow to appear (supersaturation), hence "formed slowly" on the card. With 1 mol/L NaOH 1 : 1, Q(Ca(OH)₂) = 0.05 × 0.5² = 1.25 × 10⁻² > 5.02 × 10⁻⁶: a white solid forms, light and slow to settle ("faint"); the card keeps it so the student must use the flame, as in c03a scene 8. Ca²⁺ with dilute NH₃ after AgCl: no Ca(OH)₂ (ammonia gives too little OH⁻), so the AgCl dissolving is clean.

### 1. hook (the well that shouldn't go cloudy)
> Here's a result that nearly fooled a student. She's testing unknown C for chloride. She acidifies a few drops with nitric acid, adds silver nitrate, and a faint white cloud appears. Chloride? But in the next well, she'd put plain water and the same reagents, and that well went cloudy **too**. If the water does it, the cloud can't prove anything about the unknown. She'd rinsed the plate with tap water, and tap water carries a little **chloride**. That one extra well saved her conclusion.

### 3. blank, known, unknown
> Now the controls. A blank, or negative control, is distilled water with the reagent. It should show no change. If it does, the reagent or the equipment is adding something, like that tap water. A known, or positive control, is a solution you know holds the ion, with the same reagent. It shows the test works today, and what a positive looks like on your plate. So each reagent gets one row: blank, known, **unknown**. And rinse everything with distilled water, never tap **water**.

- **Site lesson 3 Safety note** (quoted under Decisions, correction 1): glasses and gloves; silver nitrate stains and is corrosive; barium salts toxic; ammonia fumes and ventilation; small quantities; silver, barium and heavy-metal residues to labelled waste, not the sink. The risk table follows it and adds lead (correction 1).
- **Silver nitrate:** solid H272, H290, H314, H410; stains skin brown-black (the silver is reduced to fine silver in the skin; the stain fades over days as the skin renews). The row uses the dilute solution with its harms stated plainly, as in p02.
- **Lead(II) nitrate:** supplier SDSs (for example Sigma-Aldrich, lead(II) nitrate) list Repr. 1A H360Df, Acute Tox. 4 H302 and H332, Eye Dam. 1 H318, STOT RE 2 H373, Aquatic H410, Ox. Sol. 2 H272. Science check to confirm, and to note if NSW schools restrict lead nitrate further (if so, the row reads "if your school permits a lead unknown").
- **Barium chloride:** as p02 (H301, H332; teacher dispenses).
- **Corrosives:** the classifications for 1 mol/L NaOH and 2 mol/L HNO₃ depend on concentration limits (NaOH ≥ 2% Skin Corr. 1A or 1B; HNO₃ ≥ 5% Skin Corr. 1A); learner copy says "can burn eyes and skin".
