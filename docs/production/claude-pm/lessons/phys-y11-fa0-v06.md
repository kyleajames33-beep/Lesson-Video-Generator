## Decisions

- **Learner question (placeholder rewritten; open decision 2).** Route placeholder: "How can I problem-solving in a defensible physics investigation?" Rewritten as one plain causal question: "How can a physics model solve a real problem, and what should change when new evidence doesn't fit?" The answer, built in order: a real problem becomes solvable when we split it into parts the physics can describe and state what we assume (2); the model's equations give numbers, and the numbers show a cause the eye doesn't expect: ten more km h⁻¹ at the start means reaching the ball at about 40 km h⁻¹ instead of stopping (3, 4); the same model says what causes what, so it predicts the effect of speed, reaction time and grip (5); a model is trusted only after it is tested, so a bench investigation tests its braking part (6); new evidence that doesn't fit is evaluated: it may expose a flaw in the investigation rather than the model, and the investigation is modified (7, 8); that is why scientific knowledge is testable (it predicts things that could fail) and contestable (better evidence can change it) (9); a routine pulls it together (10).
- **The hook (a real problem with a surprise).** A coded road strip with a school-zone sign "40" and a coded zebra crossing; a ball (`phys-y11-cricket-ball-v1.png`, requested in v29) rolls onto the road 26 m ahead. At 40 km h⁻¹ the car stops just short; at 50 km h⁻¹, how fast is it still going at the ball? 3 s prediction, chips "about 10 km h⁻¹", "about 25 km h⁻¹", "about 40 km h⁻¹". Answer (scene 4): about 40 km h⁻¹ (39.5). The surprise is the point: the reaction distance uses up most of the gap before braking starts. "26 m" is chosen so the 40 km h⁻¹ car stops 0.5 m short under the stated assumptions (author note a); everything is tagged "model prediction: stated assumptions".
- **The model (`cibc9c30ac`).** Plain meaning (as fa0-v04): "model: a simplified description, often an equation, that predicts what happens". Two parts, each from physics already taught: reaction distance = speed × reaction time (constant velocity, v05); braking distance from v² = u² + 2as with v = 0, so s = u² ÷ 2a (v09; a here is the size of the deceleration), recalled as "the braking distance goes with speed squared, as we saw with kinetic energy" (v26). Assumptions stated on screen and said: reaction time 1.5 s; steady deceleration 7.0 m s⁻² on a dry road; level, straight road. Values are typical and tagged "assumed (illustrative)": a reaction time of about 1.5 s and hard dry-road braking of about 7 m s⁻² are common textbook values (author note a). "Assumption: something the model takes to be true, so the answer depends on it" (plain meaning).
- **Solving (`cibe345fb1`).** 40 km h⁻¹ = 11.1 m s⁻¹ (÷ 3.6, fa0-v04's conversion recalled as "divide by three point six"); reaction 16.7 m; braking 8.8 m; total 25.5 m. 50 km h⁻¹ = 13.9 m s⁻¹; reaction 20.8 m; only 5.2 m left before the ball; v² = 13.9² − 2 × 7.0 × 5.2 gives v = 11.0 m s⁻¹, about 40 km h⁻¹. The full 50 km h⁻¹ stopping distance (34.6 m) is shown on screen only. Calculation layout: task strip, equation strip, grouped givens with units, one result line per cue (owner calculation rule).
- **Cause and effect (`cibc9c30ac`).** A coded cause-and-effect chain, built one link at a time: speed → reaction distance (∝ v) and braking distance (∝ v²); reaction time → reaction distance only; grip → deceleration → braking distance. Two predictions from the model: a distracted driver at 40 km h⁻¹ with a 2.5 s reaction time travels 11 m further before braking (total 36.6 m, past the ball); on a wet road (deceleration 4.0 m s⁻², tagged "assumed"), the braking distance at 40 km h⁻¹ is 15.4 m, nearly double. "Explain phenomena": why wet-road and distraction warnings exist follows from which part each cause changes.
- **Testing the model (bench investigation).** The braking part predicts s ∝ v² at a steady deceleration. Test: v23's spring launcher (`phys-y11-spring-launcher-v1.png`) pushes v19's wooden block (`phys-y11-wooden-block-v1.png`, with a coded 2.00 cm card on top) through a light gate (`phys-y11-light-gate-v1.png`) to the data logger (`phys-y11-data-logger-v1.png`); the block slides to rest; the slide distance from the gate is read on a metre rule (`phys-y11-metre-rule-v1.png`). Five launcher settings, speeds 0.40 to 1.20 m s⁻¹. Day 1 points lie close to a straight line through the origin on a graph of slide distance against v², gradient 0.20 s² m⁻¹ (so a = 1 ÷ (2 × 0.20) = 2.5 m s⁻², screen only). Repeats per setting are not shown (fa1-v15 owns repeats and scatter bars); one grey tag "each point: mean of three runs".
- **New evidence (`ci706ca3dc`; prediction 2, 3 s).** Day 2, after the bench has been wiped down: the same speeds give longer slides; still straight through the origin, but steeper (gradient 0.25 s² m⁻¹, a = 2.0 m s⁻²). Chips "the model is wrong", "the surface changed", "the light gate is faulty". Answered by reasoning in scene 8: the gradient is 1 ÷ 2a, so steeper means a smaller deceleration, less friction; the line is still straight, so s ∝ v² survives. The alternative cause is checked, not dismissed: a gate reading speeds too low would also steepen the line, so the gate is checked with a known speed (Revision 2: the card is dropped through the gate from a measured height and its blocking time matches the free-fall prediction; re-timing the same card at the same launcher setting had no independent reference, because a changed launch speed could cancel a timing error). The conclusion is that the evidence supports a surface change, not that it proves one. Plain meaning: "evidence: data or observations that support or challenge an idea" (as fa0-v01).
- **Modify the investigation (`ci706ca3dc`).** The evidence exposes an uncontrolled variable (the surface condition), so: use one marked strip; clean and dry it the same way every session; start each session with a check run at one fixed launcher setting (a control that shows whether the surface has changed). Further investigation suggested by the surprise: make the surface the independent variable (`phys-y11-surface-smooth-v1.png` and `phys-y11-surface-rough-v1.png` strips), which is the bench version of the wet-road question in scene 5.
- **Testable and contestable (`cid1f36329`).** Plain meanings: "testable: it makes predictions an experiment could show to be wrong" (as fa0-v01's "could be shown wrong"); "contestable: anyone with better evidence can challenge it, and it changes if the evidence holds up". Our model could have failed (curved points); it passed over the range tested. If careful tests at higher speeds showed the deceleration changing with speed, the model would have to change. Real example, one clause: Newton's laws, tested for centuries, needed changes for speeds close to the speed of light (Year 12, named only). Knowledge is accepted because it keeps passing tests, not because someone important said so.
- **Routine and memory aid.** Four steps, on a coded strip that ticks through the lesson's own example: model (choose the principles and split the problem), assume (state what the answer depends on), solve (with units in every line), test (check the answer makes sense, then test the model with evidence). Memory aid: "Model, assume, solve, test." (see "Memory aid check"), said in scene 15.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A car travels at 20 m s⁻¹ on a level road. The driver's reaction time is 1.5 s, and the car then brakes with a constant deceleration of 8.0 m s⁻². (Stopping distance = reaction distance + braking distance.) (a) Calculate the stopping distance. (b) The driver is distracted and the reaction time doubles. Calculate the new stopping distance."
Model answer: (a) reaction distance = 20 × 1.5 = 30 m; braking distance from v² = u² + 2as with v = 0: s = 20² ÷ (2 × 8.0) = 25 m; stopping distance = 55 m. (b) reaction distance = 20 × 3.0 = 60 m; braking distance unchanged, 25 m; stopping distance = 85 m. Classic mistake (crossed): "doubling the reaction time doubles the stopping distance: 110 m".

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested).** "A student tests whether the distance a sliding block travels before stopping is proportional to its launch speed squared. A graph of stopping distance against speed squared is a straight line through the origin, except that the two fastest runs lie well above the line. (a) Does this evidence show that the model is wrong? Explain your answer. (b) Propose one modification to the investigation that would help explain this evidence."
Model answer: (a) Not on its own. The model assumes a constant deceleration; points above the line mean the block slid further than predicted, so its deceleration was smaller at high speeds. This could be a genuine limit of the model (friction changing with speed) or a fault in the method (for example the block lifting or twisting as it leaves the launcher). The evidence challenges the model only in the high-speed range and needs checking. (b) Any one, justified: repeat the fastest runs to see whether they are consistent; add more launch speeds above the current range; add a second light gate to measure the deceleration directly at each speed; guide the block so it leaves the launcher flat. Classic mistake (crossed): "delete the two points because they don't fit".
