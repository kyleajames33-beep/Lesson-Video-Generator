## Decisions

- **Learner question (placeholder rewritten; open decision 2).** Route placeholder: "How can I problem-solving in a defensible physics investigation?" Rewritten as one plain causal question: "How can a physics model solve a real problem, and what should change when new evidence doesn't fit?" The answer, built in order: a real problem becomes solvable when we split it into parts the physics can describe and state what we assume (2); the model's equations give numbers, and the numbers show a cause the eye doesn't expect: ten more km h⁻¹ at the start means reaching the ball at about 40 km h⁻¹ instead of stopping (3, 4); the same model says what causes what, so it predicts the effect of speed, reaction time and grip (5); a model is trusted only after it is tested, so a bench investigation tests its braking part (6); new evidence that doesn't fit is evaluated: it may expose a flaw in the investigation rather than the model, and the investigation is modified (7, 8); that is why scientific knowledge is testable (it predicts things that could fail) and contestable (better evidence can change it) (9); a routine pulls it together (10).

- **The model (`cibc9c30ac`).** Plain meaning (as fa0-v04): "model: a simplified description, often an equation, that predicts what happens". Two parts, each from physics already taught: reaction distance = speed × reaction time (constant velocity, v05); braking distance from v² = u² + 2as with v = 0, so s = u² ÷ 2a (v09; a here is the size of the deceleration), recalled as "the braking distance goes with speed squared, as we saw with kinetic energy" (v26). Assumptions stated on screen and said: reaction time 1.5 s; steady deceleration 7.0 m s⁻² on a dry road; level, straight road. "Assumption: something the model takes to be true, so the answer depends on it" (plain meaning).

Two predictions from the model: a distracted driver at 40 km h⁻¹ with a 2.5 s reaction time travels 11 m further before braking (total 36.6 m, past the ball); on a wet road (deceleration 4.0 m s⁻², tagged "assumed"), the braking distance at 40 km h⁻¹ is 15.4 m, nearly double. Test: v23's spring launcher (`phys-y11-spring-launcher-v1.png`) pushes v19's wooden block (`phys-y11-wooden-block-v1.png`, with a coded 2.00 cm card on top) through a light gate (`phys-y11-light-gate-v1.png`) to the data logger (`phys-y11-data-logger-v1.png`); the block slides to rest; the slide distance from the gate is read on a metre rule (`phys-y11-metre-rule-v1.png`). Day 1 points lie close to a straight line through the origin on a graph of slide distance against v², gradient 0.20 s² m⁻¹ (so a = 1 ÷ (2 × 0.20) = 2.5 m s⁻², screen only). Repeats per setting are not shown (fa1-v15 owns repeats and scatter bars); one grey tag "each point: mean of three runs".
- **New evidence (`ci706ca3dc`; prediction 2, 3 s).** Day 2, after the bench has been wiped down: the same speeds give longer slides; still straight through the origin, but steeper (gradient 0.25 s² m⁻¹, a = 2.0 m s⁻²). Chips "the model is wrong", "the surface changed", "the light gate is faulty". Answered by reasoning in scene 8: the gradient is 1 ÷ 2a, so steeper means a smaller deceleration, less friction; the line is still straight, so s ∝ v² survives. The alternative cause is checked, not dismissed: a gate reading speeds too low would also steepen the line, so the gate is checked with a known speed (Revision 2: the card is dropped through the gate from a measured height and its blocking time matches the free-fall prediction; re-timing the same card at the same launcher setting had no independent reference, because a changed launch speed could cancel a timing error). - **Modify the investigation (`ci706ca3dc`).** The evidence exposes an uncontrolled variable (the surface condition), so: use one marked strip; clean and dry it the same way every session; start each session with a check run at one fixed launcher setting (a control that shows whether the surface has changed). Further investigation suggested by the surprise: make the surface the independent variable (`phys-y11-surface-smooth-v1.png` and `phys-y11-surface-rough-v1.png` strips), which is the bench version of the wet-road question in scene 5.
- **Testable and contestable (`cid1f36329`).** Plain meanings: "testable: it makes predictions an experiment could show to be wrong" (as fa0-v01's "could be shown wrong"); "contestable: anyone with better evidence can challenge it, and it changes if the evidence holds up". Our model could have failed (curved points); it passed over the range tested. If careful tests at higher speeds showed the deceleration changing with speed, the model would have to change. Real example, one clause: Newton's laws, tested for centuries, needed changes for speeds close to the speed of light (Year 12, named only). Knowledge is accepted because it keeps passing tests, not because someone important said so.

### 1. hook (how fast at the ball?)
> Last time, theory said the total momentum survives a collision. Before we test that, let's see how physics solves a real problem, and what to do when the evidence surprises us. ### 5. cause and effect

### 9. testable and contestable
> This is how scientific knowledge works. A good model is testable: it makes predictions an experiment could show to be wrong. Ours could have failed, if the points had curved away from the line.
