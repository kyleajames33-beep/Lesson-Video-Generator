- **Author notes (not learner copy):**
  (a) Drude picture: v_d = eEτ/m, with τ the mean time between collisions, set by the thermal and lattice scattering, not by the drift (random speeds of about 10⁶ m s⁻¹ dwarf v_d). So τ is unchanged when E doubles and v_d ∝ E; I = nAev_d ∝ V for a fixed wire at fixed temperature. "Loses the drift it had gained" is the Drude model's average statement; real scattering is quantum (electrons scatter from lattice vibrations and defects, not from ions as obstacles). The ion-collision picture is the Year 11 model, used by the site and the syllabus wording.
  (b) Energy to the lattice: each collision transfers some of the energy the field gave the electron; this is why the drift does not grow. The rate (P = I²R) is v17's.

### 1. hook (twice the voltage)
> Last time, a voltage set up a field in a wire, and the free electrons drifted along it. Now turn the voltage up to twice as much, on the very same wire. What happens to the current? Does it stay the same, double, or just keep on **growing**?

Visual: opens on v12's last frame (see "Opening frame"). The "Next" chip becomes the heading: "Voltage, current and resistance"; the memory line fades. On "twice as much" the dial turns from "6.0 V" to "12 V" (the chip "more voltage, more current?" pulses) and the teal field arrows double in length. On "growing" chips "the same?", "double?", "keeps growing?" brighten and a 3-second countdown ring runs. The gate's readout stays "coulombs per second?".

### 2. collisions hold them back
> To answer that, we need to know what holds the electrons back. The wire isn't empty. It's packed with metal ions, vibrating in a lattice. Between collisions, the field speeds each electron up a little along the wire. Then it crashes into an ion, gets knocked off in a random direction, and loses the drift it had gained. Each crash also gives the ion energy, so it vibrates harder, and the wire warms up. So the electrons can't keep speeding up. The collisions keep their drift at a steady speed. This opposition to the flow is called **resistance**.

Visual: camera continue; the dial returns to "6.0 V" for the explanation. The lattice zooms in on one tracked electron (`DriftSawtooth`): on "speeds each electron up" a small amber "qE" push arrow and a blue drift tick growing; on "crashes into an ion" a grey collision flash, the electron leaves at a random angle and the tick resets; on "vibrates harder" that ion's jiggle grows and a faint warm tint spreads along the wire. The small sawtooth graph (right) draws "drift gained" against time: teeth rising, dropping to zero at each flash, with a dashed "average drift" line. On "can't keep speeding up" a dashed ghost of an unbroken rising line (no collisions) fades in and takes a grey scribble cross; the chip "keeps growing?" dims. On "resistance" chip "resistance: how strongly a conductor opposes the current". The chip "what holds them back?" ticks.

### 3. twice the push, twice the flow
> Now double the voltage. Across the same wire, that makes the field twice as strong, so each electron gets twice the push. Between collisions, it picks up twice as much drift. The collisions still come just as often, so on average the electrons drift twice as fast, and twice as many coulombs pass each second. The current **doubles**. So as long as the wire's resistance stays the same, the current is proportional to the voltage. Halve the voltage, and the current **halves**.

Visual: camera continue. On "double the voltage" the dial turns to "12 V"; the field arrows double; the amber push arrow on the tracked electron doubles. On "twice as much drift" the sawtooth's next teeth rise twice as high, with the flashes at the same spacing (a row of grey tick marks under the graph shows the equal spacing); the dashed average line rises to twice its height (labels "6.0 V: average" ghosted, "12 V: twice the average"). On "twice as many coulombs" the gate's readout ticks twice as fast. On "doubles" chip "double?" glows; "the same?" and "keeps growing?" take grey scribble crosses. Right column, on "proportional": boxed "constant resistance: I ∝ V" with grey tag "constant resistance: same wire, same temperature". > Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Resistance (R, in ohms, Ω) is how strongly a conductor opposes the current. Drifting electrons collide with the vibrating ions of the lattice, which keeps their drift steady and warms the wire.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A metal wire, kept at a constant temperature, carries a current of 0.30 A when a voltage of 4.0 V is applied across it. (a) Calculate the current in the wire when the voltage is increased to 10 V. (b) Explain, in terms of the electrons in the wire, why the current increases."
Model answer: (a) At constant temperature the resistance is constant, so I ∝ V: I₂ = I₁ × V₂/V₁ = 0.30 × 10 ÷ 4.0 = 0.75 A. (b) A larger voltage across the same wire produces a stronger electric field in it, so the force on each free electron is larger. Between collisions with the lattice ions each electron gains more drift velocity, while the collisions happen just as often, so the average drift speed increases and more charge passes a point each second: the current increases (in proportion). Classic mistake (crossed): "0.12 A" (the ratio inverted).

**Attempt 2 (scene 15; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks suggested).** "Wires P and Q are made of the same metal and are at the same temperature. Wire Q is three times as long as wire P and has twice its diameter. (a) Explain, in terms of the movement of electrons, how each of these two differences affects the resistance of Q compared with P. (b) Determine the resistance of Q in terms of the resistance of P, R_P."
Model answer: (a) Length: for the same voltage across it, the voltage is spread over three times the length, so the electric field in Q is a third as strong; the electrons drift a third as fast and the current is a third, so the length alone triples the resistance (more lattice to cross). Diameter: twice the diameter gives four times the cross-sectional area (area ∝ diameter²), so four times as many free electrons drift side by side at the same speed; the current is four times as large, so the area alone makes the resistance a quarter. (b) R_Q = 3 × ¼ × R_P = ¾ R_P (0.75 R_P). Classic mistake (crossed): "R_Q = 3/2 R_P" (treating twice the diameter as twice the area).

Then find out why a longer wire resists more, why a thicker wire resists less (and why doubling the diameter quarters it), why nichrome resists far more than copper, and why a lamp's current surges at switch-on. Two exam-style questions at the end.
