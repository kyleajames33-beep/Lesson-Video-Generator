**Boundary:** this video prepares students to carry out and analyse the strength investigation; it does not claim that watching it is conducting it. It teaches two pH tests of strength: (1) the matched comparison: a strong and a weak acid (and a strong and a weak base) at the same concentration, 0.100 mol/L, so the pH gap comes from how completely each ionises; (2) the tenfold dilution test: dilute each solution exactly tenfold with volumetric glassware and watch how far the pH moves (strong acid about one unit, weak acid about half a unit; the bases mirror this), which does not depend on trusting the label's concentration. It also covers the variables, validity, reliability and accuracy, and the risk assessment. **Placement limits (playlist order 8):** this video comes before conjugate pairs (chem-m6-c06, order 9), pH calculation (chem-m6-c07, order 10), ICE tables (chem-m6-c08, order 12) and Ka (chem-m6-c10, order 14). So no pH is calculated from a concentration, no Ka or Kb appears, and the half-unit result for the weak acid is explained with C05's particle idea only (diluting a weak acid lets a bigger fraction of its molecules ionise), not derived. The pH meter routine (calibrate, rinse, blot, wait for steady, three readings in fresh portions) is P03's and is recalled in one scene, not re-taught. The particle model of strong against weak, the arrows and the "concentrated weak can read lower than dilute strong" surprise are C05's and are recalled, not re-taught. Conductivity is not used (open decision).

- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Matched concentration means equal formal (analytical) concentration: 0.100 mol/L of each substance dissolved, whatever fraction ionises. Teacher-prepared solutions from standardised stocks; NaOH solutions absorb CO₂ and slowly lose hydroxide, so they should be fresh. (b) Ideal values at 25 °C (concentration model, not shown): 0.100 mol/L HCl pH 1.00; 0.100 mol/L CH₃COOH (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) pH 2.88; 0.100 mol/L NH₃ (Kb 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) pH 11.12; 0.100 mol/L NaOH pH 13.00. A glass electrode responds to activity: at an ionic strength of 0.100 mol/L the Davies activity coefficient is about 0.78, so a strong acid's measured pH would sit about 0.11 above ideal (about 1.11) and a strong base's about 0.11 below (about 12.89). Measured values in school labs commonly fall between ideal and activity values because the meter is calibrated only from 4 to 10 and extrapolated, and liquid-junction potentials at extreme pH shift readings. The class means (HCl 1.04, NaOH 12.95) were chosen inside that band and close to the C05 hook's "pH 1.0" and "pH 13", so viewers who remember C05 are not confused; the science check should confirm they are plausible. Ethanoic acid and ammonia have low ionic strength (about 0.0013 mol/L), so their activity shift is about 0.02 (2.90 and 11.10 against ideal 2.88 and 11.12). (c) Dilution test at 0.0100 mol/L (ideal): HCl 2.00, CH₃COOH 3.38, NH₃ 10.62, NaOH 12.00; the class means (2.04, 3.39, 10.60, 11.95) sit close to P03's 0.010 mol/L means (2.03, 3.40, 10.59, 11.96), within 0.01, as a separate session would. Changes: HCl +1.00, CH₃COOH +0.49, NH₃ −0.50, NaOH −1.00. Ideal changes: strong exactly 1.00 (activity would make it about 0.94); weak 0.51 (from 2.875 to 3.382). (d) Why about half a unit: when the ionised fraction is small, [H₃O⁺] ≈ √(Ka × c), so a tenfold dilution divides [H₃O⁺] by √10 ≈ 3.2, a pH rise of 0.50. The approximation holds while the weak acid is mostly un-ionised (ethanoic acid at 0.100 mol/L: 1.3% ionised; at 0.0100 mol/L: 4.2%). As a weak acid becomes very dilute the rise grows toward 1, and any acid diluted far enough approaches pH 7 without passing it (bank aid "Dilution can't cross pH 7"). The narration says "much less than ten times" and "about half a unit for ethanoic acid", not a general law; the √ argument is C08/C10 material. The memory aid's condition tag carries "weak acid mostly un-ionised". For bases, [OH⁻] behaves the same way and pH = 14 − pOH at 25 °C (C07), so the base changes are the mirror; the narration says the bases "mirror" the acids and gives no hydroxide factor. (e) Ratio from the readings: 2.90 − 1.04 = 1.86 units; 10^1.86 = 72, said "about seventy times". C05 said "about eighty" from one-decimal data (1.0 and 2.9, 10^1.9 = 79). Both are honest for their data; scene 5 says "from these readings". Bases: 12.95 − 11.10 = 1.85 units; learner copy says only "far more hydroxide" (the hydroxide factor needs Kw, C07). (f) Significant figures: concentrations 0.100 and 0.0100 mol/L (3 s.f., volumetric glassware); the pipette volume 10.00 mL and flask 100.0 mL; c₂ = 0.100 × 10.00 ÷ 100.0 = 0.0100 mol/L (3 s.f.). Readings to 0.01 as displayed. Ratio from a difference with two decimal places: 72, said "about seventy". In A1 the readings have two decimal places; in A2, 2.90 − 2.40 = 0.50. (g) A1 data: methanoic acid Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ (pKa 3.75); at 0.50 mol/L the quadratic gives [H₃O⁺] 9.40 × 10⁻³ mol/L, pH 2.03. 0.0020 mol/L HNO₃: pH 2.70. At equal 0.100 mol/L: HNO₃ pH 1.00, HCOOH pH 2.38. (h) A2 data: an acid of Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ at about 0.88 mol/L reads pH 2.40; diluted tenfold to about 0.088 mol/L it reads 2.90 (quadratic). A strong monoprotic acid at pH 2.40 diluted tenfold would read 3.40. "Monoprotic" is in the question so that sulfuric acid's weak second step cannot confuse the test. (i) Calibration range: readings near 1 and 13 lie outside the 4.00 to 10.00 calibration, so their absolute values are less certain (a few hundredths to about 0.1). The strength conclusions rest on gaps of about 1.9 units and on changes of 1.00 against 0.50, which are far larger. Schools with pH 1.68 or 12.45 standard buffers can extend the calibration (screen tag only: "a buffer near each end, if available"). (j) Pronunciation: "pH" as letters; "zero point one zero zero" digit by digit; "zero point zero one zero zero"; "ethanoic" as "eth-an-OH-ik"; "methanoic" as "meth-an-OH-ik"; hydronium (never "H three O plus"); "c one V one" is not spoken.

Main question: *Two bottles, both labelled 0.100 mol/L: one hydrochloric acid, one ethanoic acid. How can a pH meter show which acid is strong, what makes the comparison fair, and how could you still tell them apart if you couldn't trust the label?*
Entry knowledge (C05, P03, Module 5 practicals, Year 11): strong and weak as complete and partial ionisation; compare at the same concentration; diluting a weak acid lets a bigger fraction ionise; lower pH means more hydronium, one unit is ten times (chem-m6-c05); the pH meter routine and the 0.010 mol/L readings (chem-m6-p03); validity, reliability, accuracy, variables, risk (chem-m5-p01); dilution with volumetric glassware (Year 11 Module 2).
Stops after: explaining why strength must be compared at matched concentration; carrying out the matched comparison and explaining the pH gap by extent of ionisation; designing and carrying out a tenfold dilution with volumetric glassware; explaining why a strong acid's pH rises by about one unit and a weak acid's by much less (about half for ethanoic acid), and that the bases mirror this; naming the variables; judging validity, reliability and accuracy; writing the risk rows. No pH calculation (chem-m6-c07), no Ka or Kb (chem-m6-c10), no conjugate pairs (chem-m6-c06), no conductivity.
Handoff: conjugate pairs and amphiprotic species: why chloride never takes its proton back but ethanoate does (route chem-m6-c06).

### 5. what the gap means
> At the same concentration, hydrochloric acid reads almost **two units** lower. Each unit is ten times, so from these readings it has about **seventy times** as much hydronium. That's because every hydrochloric acid molecule gives its proton to water, but only a **small fraction** of ethanoic acid molecules do. Single arrow, double arrow. The bases mirror it: sodium hydroxide gives far more **hydroxide** than ammonia does.

### 8. why the strong acid moves one unit
> Why a whole unit? Hydrochloric acid is already completely **ionised**. Diluting it tenfold just spreads the same hydronium ions through ten times the volume, so there's a **tenth** as much hydronium in each litre. A tenth is one pH unit.

Two bottles, both labelled 0.100 mol/L: one hydrochloric acid, one ethanoic acid. Use a pH meter to show which is strong, with a fair comparison at matched concentration. Then dilute each solution exactly tenfold and watch how far the pH moves: strong acids move about one unit, weak acids much less. Includes variables, validity, reliability and accuracy, a risk assessment and two exam-style questions.
