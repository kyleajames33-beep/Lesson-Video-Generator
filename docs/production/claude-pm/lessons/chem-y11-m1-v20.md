### 2. first decision: ionic or covalent?
> These rules come from IUPAC, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, so chemists everywhere use the same names. The first step is always the same. Look at the elements. A metal with a non-metal, or the ammonium ion, means an ionic compound, made of **ions**. Only non-metals means a covalent compound, where the atoms share electrons instead. The two kinds are named in different **ways**.

### 3. two-element ionic names
> Start with the simplest ionic compounds, made of two elements. Name the metal first, unchanged. Then change the ending of the non-metal's name, so chlorine becomes chloride, oxygen becomes oxide, and sulfur becomes **sulfide**. So N a C l is sodium chloride, and M g O is magnesium **oxide**. Notice there are no numbers in these names. Sodium and magnesium each form only one ion, so the charges already fix the **ratio**.

### 5. a numeral for the charge
> It's potassium oxide, not dipotassium oxide. Ionic names don't count ions, because the charges already do that **job**. But copper can form two different ions, so copper oxide could mean either powder. The fix is a Roman numeral, in brackets after the metal's name, giving the charge on the metal ion. This is called a Stock **numeral**. It's the metal's charge, also called its oxidation state, and not the number of **atoms**.

### 6. which copper oxide is which?
> To find the numeral, work backwards from the anion, as we did last time. In C u O, one oxide ion is minus two, so the copper is plus two. That's copper two oxide, the black **powder**. In C u two O, the same minus two is shared between two coppers, so each one is plus one. That's copper one oxide, the red **powder**. The two in the formula counts the coppers. The one in the name is their **charge**.

### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Decide first: metal (or NH₄⁺) present means ionic; non-metals only means covalent.
2. Ionic: metal name, then the anion: chloride, oxide, sulfide (NaCl sodium chloride). No counts. Polyatomic ions keep their names (Na₂SO₄ sodium sulfate; (NH₄)₂CO₃ ammonium carbonate).
3. A metal with more than one charge (Fe, Cu, Pb, Sn) gets a Roman numeral for its charge: CuO copper(II) oxide; Cu₂O copper(I) oxide; FeCl₃ iron(III) chloride.
4. Covalent: prefixes count atoms: mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, hexa 6 (no mono on the first element): CO₂ carbon dioxide, CO carbon monoxide, N₂O₄ dinitrogen tetroxide.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Ionic: charge in brackets. Covalent: count in the prefix.*

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style).** "Give the IUPAC name of each compound. (a) PbO₂ (b) SF₆ (c) Fe₂(SO₄)₃ (3 marks)"
Model answer: (a) Two O²⁻ total −4, so Pb⁴⁺: lead(IV) oxide (1). (b) Non-metals only, 1 S and 6 F: sulfur hexafluoride (1). (c) Three SO₄²⁻ total −6, shared by two Fe, so Fe³⁺: iron(III) sulfate (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "iron(II) sulfate" (subscript read as the charge).

**Attempt 2 (scene 15; original wording, HSC style).** "A student names CuCl₂ as 'copper dichloride' and SO₃ as 'sulfur oxide'. Explain the error in each name and give the correct IUPAC name for each compound. (3 marks)"
Model answer: CuCl₂ is ionic (a metal with a non-metal), so the number of chloride ions is not given by a prefix; copper can form more than one ion, so its charge is shown by a Stock numeral: two Cl⁻ total −2, so Cu²⁺, copper(II) chloride (1, 1 for the reason). SO₃ is covalent (two non-metals), so prefixes must give the number of atoms, since sulfur and oxygen form more than one compound (SO₂ and SO₃): sulfur trioxide (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "prefixes on an ionic compound".
