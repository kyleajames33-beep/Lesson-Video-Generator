Author notes (not learner copy):
(a) Copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O, is a red to red-brown powder; copper(II) oxide, CuO, is a black powder (both standard laboratory reagents; CRC Handbook descriptions). Both are made of copper and oxygen only.
(b) Oxidation state: for a simple ion it equals the ion's charge. Oxidation numbers in molecules and polyatomic ions come in Module 3 (redox). Learner copy uses "the charge on the metal ion, also called its oxidation state".
(c) IUPAC Nomenclature of Inorganic Chemistry (Red Book, 2005), IR-5.2: multiplying prefixes are not elided except "monoxide"; "tetraoxide" is the strict form. NESA and most Australian texts use "tetroxide"; both are accepted. Screen uses the site's "tetroxide" with a small grey "(also tetraoxide)".
(d) Metals named as variable in learner copy: iron, copper, lead, tin. Single-charge metals named: sodium, magnesium, aluminium (and potassium, calcium in examples). Zinc and silver have one common charge; not raised.
(e) Common names (water, ammonia, methane) are IUPAC-accepted and not built from prefixes; one grey tag in scene 9 says "some molecules keep common names: water, ammonia". Hydrogen compounds such as HCl are named "hydrogen chloride" without prefixes by convention; not raised.
(g) Compositional names (Revision 2; IUPAC Red Book 2005, IR-5; https://iupac.qmul.ac.uk/BriefGuide/inorganic.html): names with multiplicative prefixes on ionic compounds (copper dichloride, iron trichloride, dipotassium oxide) are valid IUPAC names alongside Stock names (copper(II) chloride). Stock naming is the route taught here (root decision 37); the alternatives are named once in scene 11 as valid and are never crossed. Older oxidation-state names for molecular compounds (carbon(IV) oxide) are not shown.
(f) Pronunciations: "IUPAC" (EYE-you-pack); "Stock" (as the word); "copper two oxide", "copper one oxide", "iron three chloride", "iron two sulfate", "iron three sulfate", "lead four oxide" (the Roman numerals are spoken as numbers, the usual spoken form); "tetroxide" (teh-TROX-ide); "hexafluoride" (hex-a-FLOOR-ide); "pentachloride" (pen-ta-CLOR-ide).

> Last time, we built formulas from ion charges. Now let's go the other way, from a formula to a name. Here's why it matters. These two powders are both made of nothing but copper and oxygen. One is red, and one is **black**. If you asked for copper oxide, which one would you get? A good name has to point to exactly one **compound**. That's what the naming rules **do**.

### 2. first decision: ionic or covalent?
> These rules come from IUPAC, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, so chemists everywhere use the same names. The first step is always the same. Look at the elements. A metal with a non-metal, or the ammonium ion, means an ionic compound, made of **ions**. Only non-metals means a covalent compound, where the atoms share electrons instead. The two kinds are named in different **ways**.

### 3. two-element ionic names
> Start with the simplest ionic compounds, made of two elements. Name the metal first, unchanged. Then change the ending of the non-metal's name, so chlorine becomes chloride, oxygen becomes oxide, and sulfur becomes **sulfide**. So N a C l is sodium chloride, and M g O is magnesium **oxide**. Notice there are no numbers in these names. Sodium and magnesium each form only one ion, so the charges already fix the **ratio**.

### 5. a numeral for the charge
> It's potassium oxide. In the naming route we'll use, ionic names don't count ions, because the charges already do that **job**. But copper can form two different ions, so copper oxide could mean either powder. The fix is a Roman numeral, in brackets after the metal's name, giving the charge on the metal ion. This is called a Stock **numeral**. It's the metal's charge, also called its oxidation state, and not the number of **atoms**.

### 6. which copper oxide is which?
> To find the numeral, work backwards from the anion, as we did last time. In C u O, one oxide ion is minus two, so the copper is plus two. That's copper two oxide, the black **powder**. In C u two O, the same minus two is shared between two coppers, so each one is plus one. That's copper one oxide, the red **powder**. The two in the formula counts the coppers. The one in the name is their **charge**.

> Next, polyatomic ions. Each one keeps its own name, so you name it as a whole, and never split it into **elements**. N a two S O four is sodium sulfate. Calcium nitrate is the compound from last time. And when ammonium is the positive ion, it goes first, like a metal, as in ammonium **carbonate**. Names like sulfate, nitrate and carbonate tell you the ion contains oxygen. So calling sodium sulfate sodium sulfide is a classic slip, because sulfide means sulfur on its **own**.

### 9. covalent compounds: count the atoms
> Now the covalent compounds, made of non-metals only. Here there are no ions, so there are no charges to fix the ratio. Carbon and oxygen, for example, can join as C O, or as C O **two**. So the name itself must count the atoms, using prefixes. Mono means one, di means two, tri three, tetra four, penta five, and hexa **six**. C O two is carbon dioxide. C O is carbon monoxide. We drop mono from the first element, so it's not monocarbon **monoxide**.

### 11. the big contrast
> So here's the big contrast. In an ionic name, a Roman numeral gives the metal's charge. In a covalent name, a prefix gives the number of atoms. You'll also see names like copper dichloride in other books. They count the ions instead, and they're valid IUPAC names too. Here, we use the numeral **route**. Ionic: name the charge if it can change. Covalent: count the **atoms**.

### 12. putting it together
> Let's run three formulas through the whole method. F e S O four. Iron is a metal, so it's ionic. Sulfate is minus two, so the iron is plus two, which makes it iron two **sulfate**. P C l five. Phosphorus and chlorine are both non-metals, so it's covalent, and we count the atoms. It's phosphorus **pentachloride**. Last, A l, bracket O H, close bracket, three.
