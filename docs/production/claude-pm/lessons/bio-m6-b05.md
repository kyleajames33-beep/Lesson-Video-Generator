### 3. a somatic mutation: a patch, and sometimes a cancer

If the change hits a gene that controls cell division, and more mutations follow, the patch can keep dividing, and become a cancer. That's how UV causes most skin cancers.

### 4. a germ-line mutation: every cell, and onward
> A **germ-line mutation** is different. Suppose it's in a cell that makes sperm. A sperm carrying it fertilises an egg, so the zygote has it, and so does every cell copied from the zygote. That includes the new animal's own germ cells, so it can be passed on again, and enter the gene pool. The parent it arose in isn't affected, and the offspring may show nothing, if the new allele is recessive.

### 5. the distinction
> So that's Angelina Jolie's case. Her inherited copy was in every cell, including those that make eggs, so each child had a one in two chance of getting it. A somatic mutation is copied only into one cell's descendants, so it affects part of one organism, and isn't inherited. A germ-line mutation can reach every cell of the next generation. That's why, in animals, only germ-line mutations add to the gene pool.

### 6. the second question: coding DNA
> Now the second question, where in the DNA? Only about one to two per cent of human DNA is **coding DNA**, the stretches whose codons are translated into protein. A change there alters a codon, so it can be silent, swap an amino acid, add an early stop, or shift the frame. The rest is **non-coding DNA**, and much of it was once written off as junk.

### 7. non-coding DNA that controls a gene
> But some non-coding DNA controls genes. Remember the promoter, where RNA polymerase binds to copy a gene. Change a base there, and the polymerase may bind less well, so less mRNA is made, or none. Every codon is still correct, there's just less protein. Other control stretches, called **regulatory sequences**, can sit far away, and decide when a gene is switched on, and in which cells.

### 15. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Somatic mutation: in a body cell. Copied by mitosis into that cell's descendants, so it can affect part of one organism (for example, a skin cancer). Not inherited (in animals).
- Germ-line mutation: in a cell that makes gametes. An offspring that inherits it has it in every cell, including its germ cells, so it can be passed on and enter the gene pool.
- Coding DNA (about 1 to 2% of human DNA) is translated into protein; a mutation there changes a codon (silent, missense, nonsense, frameshift).
- Non-coding DNA includes promoters and regulatory sequences, which control when, where and how much a gene is expressed (lactase persistence), and introns, cut out of mRNA. Some has no known job.
- Significance depends on what the changed stretch does, and what the change does to it, not on whether it is translated.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (19 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell; Strachan and Read, Human Molecular Genetics) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Jolie, 2013.** Angelina Jolie, "My Medical Choice", The New York Times, 14 May 2013: "I carry a 'faulty' gene, BRCA1"; "my doctors estimated that I had an 87 percent risk of breast cancer and a 50 percent risk of ovarian cancer". She did not name which parent it came from; "She'd inherited it" is right for a germ-line BRCA1 variant found by testing. "Her doctors estimated" matches her wording. The video gives no general carrier risk (site correction 1). Naming a real person: she is named only as the person who disclosed it, with no image, as on the site.
- **"Each of her children had a one in two chance".** A heterozygous carrier passes a given allele to each child with probability 1/2 (B9). Correct.
- **"A raised risk, not a certainty" (grey tag).** BRCA1 penetrance for breast cancer is high but incomplete (Kuchenbaecker et al. 2017: cumulative risk about 72 per cent to age 80 across carriers). Correct.
- **Somatic patch by mitosis.** Mitosis copies the genome into both daughter cells, so a somatic mutation is carried by the descendants of the mutated cell only (a clone). Sun-exposed skin carries many such clones (Martincorena et al. 2015). Correct.
- **Timing.** A mutation in an early embryo cell is carried by all that cell's descendants, a large part of the body (somatic mosaicism); a later one gives a smaller clone. "The earlier it happens, the bigger the patch": correct as a tendency for dividing tissues. Author note: an early mutation can also reach the germ line (gonadal mosaicism); not spoken.
- **Cancer needs more than one mutation, in division-control genes.** Most cancers arise from several somatic mutations accumulating in oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes in one lineage. Correct.
- **UV and skin cancer.** Cancer Council Australia: about 95 per cent of melanomas and 99 per cent of non-melanoma skin cancers in Australia are caused by UV exposure. "Most skin cancers": correct.
- **Germ-line route.** A mutation in a spermatogonium can be carried by many sperm; a zygote from such a sperm carries it in every cell, including its own germ line. "The parent it arose in isn't affected": a mutation confined to the germ line does not change the parent's body. "May show nothing, if the new allele is recessive": correct (heterozygote).
- **Coding fraction.** Protein-coding sequence is about 1 to 1.5 per cent of the human genome (about 20,000 protein-coding genes; International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium 2004). "About one to two per cent": correct, matching the site.
- **"Once written off as junk".** Ohno's term "junk DNA" (1972) and its later debate. A fair historical statement.
- **Promoter mutations.** Promoter mutations reduce transcription with a normal coding sequence, for example *HBB* promoter mutations causing beta-plus thalassaemia (such as -28 A>G). "The polymerase may bind less well, so less mRNA is made, or none": correct at this level (author note: in eukaryotes, transcription factors bind the promoter and recruit RNA polymerase; B6b's simpler "polymerase binds the promoter" is kept).
- **Regulatory sequences far from the gene.** Enhancers act from thousands to about a million bases away and control timing and tissue. Correct.
- **Lactase persistence.** *LCT* expression normally falls after weaning in most mammals and in about two thirds of people. In people of northern European ancestry, persistence is mainly associated with the -13910 C>T variant (rs4988235), about 14,000 bases upstream of *LCT*, inside an intron of the neighbouring *MCM6* gene, in an enhancer that keeps *LCT* expressed (Enattah et al. 2002; Olds and Sibley 2003). Other variants in the same region cause persistence in some African and Middle Eastern populations (Tishkoff et al. 2007), hence "In many of them". The lactase protein is unchanged. Inherited as a dominant trait, with strong recent positive selection in dairying populations; "spread through those populations" states the outcome. The screen shows no base letters (orientation is avoided). Correct.
- **Introns and splice sites.** Most human genes contain introns, removed from pre-mRNA by splicing; changes deep in an intron are usually neutral; changes at the splice donor or acceptor sites cause exon skipping or intron retention (several beta-thalassaemia alleles). "Can make the cell cut in the wrong place, and the protein changes": correct.
- **"No known job".** Much non-coding DNA has no demonstrated function; estimates of the constrained fraction are roughly 8 to 15 per cent (Rands et al. 2014), against ENCODE's broader "biochemical activity". "Has no known job, so changes there are often neutral": suitably hedged.
- **TERT promoter in melanoma (screen only, scene 11).** Recurrent UV-signature mutations in the *TERT* promoter create new transcription factor binding sites and raise telomerase expression, found in about 70 per cent of cutaneous melanomas in the first studies (Huang et al. 2013; Horn et al. 2013). The cell text "melanoma: a switch stuck on" is a plain summary; the science check should confirm it.
- **Fly attempt.** X-ray induced somatic mutations in *Drosophila* eye cells give white-facet clones (the classic eye-spot somatic mutation assays expose larvae, whose eye disc cells still divide). Author note: a white patch shows only where the eye cells lose every working *white* allele (one X in a male, or a heterozygous female); the card names no gene, sex or allele, and no demand depends on it. Only offspring from sperm descended from the mutated cell carry it, hence "some offspring". Correct.
