## Decisions

- **Main teaching move: every step has a reason, and every reason is about light or air.** A light microscope shines light up through the slide (scene 2), so the specimen must be thin (light passes through one layer), wet (no air pockets, cells stay plump), stained (contrast, because most of the cell is nearly colourless) and covered without trapped air (bubbles look like cells). Students who keep the cause can rebuild the method. The site's describe answer says the same: "a reason attached to each technique that has one".
- **The surprise beat: a bubble can pass for a cell (scenes 1, 7).** The hook shows a careless slide with dark-ringed circles; scene 7 sets a bubble beside a real cell and names the visible differences (perfect circle, thick black rim, bright centre, no nucleus, crosses cell edges). The site's own reason for the angled coverslip: "a trapped air bubble is a dark-edged circle that beginners mistake for a cell."
- **The prediction beat (scene 6).** Before the angled coverslip, students predict what a flat-dropped coverslip traps. A 2 s thinking hold with a "Predict" chip (not a countdown; a quick check, not a practice question), as p01's scene 5.
- **Identify by appearance, not function (scene 9; the stop's "identify").** The site's describe answer: "Describing each structure by how it looks, not by what it does, is what the word 'identify' is asking for in a microscopy question." Cell wall: the firm, straight outline around each cell, shared with its neighbours. Nucleus: a small, darker, oval body, often near one side. Cytoplasm: the pale, slightly grainy layer just inside the wall and around the nucleus. **Science correction carried (site finding 1):** the site calls the cytoplasm "the pale, evenly stained region filling the space inside the wall", but the same answer says "a large central vacuole occupies most of the interior". In an onion epidermis cell most of the clear interior is the vacuole, and the cytoplasm is a thin layer lining the wall. The video says so in one sentence (v03 taught the vacuole; grey tag), so students do not learn to label the vacuole as cytoplasm.
- **Cell wall versus membrane (scene 10, attempt 2).** Cheek cells, stained with methylene blue, show a nucleus and cytoplasm but no cell wall: their edge is the cell membrane, and the cells are rounded and irregular. This contrast is why the onion is the specimen for "identify the cell wall", and it sets up attempt 2's crossed mistake (calling the onion cell's outline "the cell membrane": in a plant cell the membrane is pressed against the wall and too thin to see as a separate line at this power). The cheek-cell scene is brief (about 30 s): the site method names it as the alternative, and it uses v03's plant and animal contrast without re-teaching it.
- **Focusing (scene 8).** Stage 5 assumed, recalled with its two reasons only (the site's describe answer credits both): low power first because it shows a wider area, so cells are easy to find; focus by widening the gap between lens and slide while looking through the eyepiece, after closing the gap while watching from the side, so the lens can't crack the slide; on high power, fine focus only. "Objective" glossed as "the lens just above the slide".
- **The method (site method, kept in order, with its reasons).** (1) One drop of water in the middle of a clean slide (keeps cells hydrated; fills the space under the coverslip so there are no air pockets). (2) Snap an onion scale backwards, peel a small piece of the thin skin (epidermis) from its inner, curved surface with forceps, and lay it flat in the drop without folding (light must pass through; folded or thick pieces stack cells that can't all be focused). (3) One drop of iodine solution (adds contrast; the nucleus takes up more stain and looks darker). (4) Coverslip lowered from one edge at about 45 degrees with a mounting needle (liquid spreads along the edge and pushes air out ahead of it); blot extra liquid with filter paper. (5) Low power first, then high power. The site's "or scrape inner cheek cells" alternative becomes scene 10 as a commercially prepared slide (NSW government schools prohibit cheek-cell smears; site correction logged).
- **Stain order.** The site adds the iodine before the coverslip. Many school methods instead place the coverslip first and draw iodine under it with filter paper (irrigation). The video uses the site's order (simpler, one fewer technique) and adds a grey tag in scene 5 "some methods add the stain after the coverslip". Codex science check to confirm both are standard.
- **Magnifications named.** ×40 to find and centre (×4 objective, ×10 eyepiece) and ×400 to identify (×40 objective). Screen only (grey chips); the narration says "low power" and "high power". No calculation (route stop: cell size belongs to v06).
- **Risk table (scene 11).** Three spoken rows, each hazard, harm and a control for this practical: glass slides and coverslips (cuts: hold by the edges; report breakages; don't pick up broken glass); iodine solution (stains skin and clothes, irritates eyes: one drop at a time, safety glasses, wash splashes off with water); the microscope lamp and its cord (electrical; dry hands, cord clear, switch off before unplugging). Teacher-note rows, not shown: the knife used to cut the onion (teacher cuts the onion into scales beforehand, so students only peel); methylene blue (stains; small dropper bottles). Shown table: only the row being spoken (p01's rule); earlier rows collapse to one-word grey chips. Codex to confirm against NSW Department of Education science safety guidance, resolved at Revision 2: cheek-cell sampling is not permitted (brief 138), so a prepared slide is used.
- **Memory aid (author-written).** "**Thin, wet, stained, slanted.**" One word per preparation step, each carrying its reason: thin so light passes through one layer; wet so there are no air pockets and the cells stay plump; stained so the parts show up; slanted so the coverslip pushes air out. Shown after scene 6 has explained all four, then large on the notes screen with the second line "Then low power first." Assumptions checked under "Memory aid check".
- **Site alignment (L05, kept in substance).** The site's definitions of wet mount and stain (scenes 2, 5); the method (scenes 3 to 8); "Lowering the coverslip at an angle reduces bubbles that could be mistaken for cells" (scenes 6, 7); "iodine stains skin and clothes" (scene 11); the describe answer's reasons for thin and flat, the angled coverslip and focusing away from the slide, and its appearance-based identification (scenes 4, 6, 8, 9). Not used here: the mini-grid card, the magnification equation, the activity and practice calculations, the specialised-cells card and the pathology section (all v06's); the site's hook question about real size (v06's hook).

> Step three. Add one drop of iodine **solution**. A stain is a dye that sticks to some parts of a cell more than others. ### 7. a bubble or a cell?
> Why does that matter so much? Under the microscope, an air bubble is a perfect **circle** with a thick black rim and a bright centre. It has no nucleus, and it can sit right across several cells. ### 9. identify the three structures
