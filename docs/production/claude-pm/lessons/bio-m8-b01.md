### 2. a range, not a fixed number

### 4. five parts, built in the cold
> Your body runs the same kind of loop, with five parts. Say you step into a cold wind. First, the **stimulus**: a change in the variable, here body temperature starting to fall. Second, a **receptor** detects it: thermoreceptors, temperature sensors in your skin and in the hypothalamus, a small region at the base of the brain. Third, the **control centre**, the hypothalamus, compares that signal with the set point. Fourth, it signals **effectors**, the muscles and glands that act. Skeletal muscles start shivering, and small arteries in the skin, called arterioles, narrow. Fifth, the **response**: more heat is made, less is lost, and temperature climbs back.

### 5. why it's called negative
> Here's the key idea. The response pushes the variable the opposite way to the original change. Temperature fell, so the response raises it. That's why it's called **negative feedback**: negative means opposing, not bad. And watch what happens next. As temperature returns, the receptors detect less change, so their signals weaken and the extra shivering fades. The response settles to what's needed, not to zero: in a cold room, some extra heat production carries on, because the cold hasn't gone away. And small delays can let temperature drift a little either side of the set point.

### 6. positive feedback, the contrast
> Compare that with **positive feedback**, where the response strengthens the original change. When a blood vessel tears, a few platelets stick to the damaged wall. It can't hold a variable steady, so homeostasis relies on negative feedback.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment relatively stable, within a tolerance range around a set point (core temperature about 36.5 to 37.5 °C), so enzymes keep working.
- The loop, in order: stimulus (a change in the variable), receptor (detects it), control centre (compares with the set point), effector (acts), response (opposes the change).
- Negative feedback: the response opposes the deviation; as the variable returns, the signals and the extra response ease, and small fluctuations can occur around the set point. Positive feedback amplifies a change until an end point (blood clotting).

1. (10a, construct and explain, 5 marks.) Exercise raises blood CO₂; chemoreceptors in the brainstem and major arteries; medulla oblongata; diaphragm and intercostal muscles; faster, deeper breathing. (a) Construct a negative feedback loop naming the five components. (b) Explain why it is negative feedback.
   Model: (a) Stimulus: blood carbon dioxide concentration rises above its usual level (1 mark for stimulus and receptor); receptor: chemoreceptors in the brainstem and major arteries; control centre: medulla oblongata (1 mark for control centre and effector); effectors: diaphragm and intercostal muscles; response: increased rate and depth of breathing removes more CO₂ through the lungs, so blood CO₂ falls back towards its usual level (1 mark). (b) The response opposes the original change: a rise in CO₂ produces a response that lowers CO₂ (1 mark); as CO₂ returns, the receptors detect less change, so breathing rate and depth move back towards resting levels as the extra stimulus falls (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed out: "Stimulus: exercise". Other wrong chains named in the review: naming the lungs as the control centre; "the receptors detect the faster breathing".
2. (10c, identify and explain, 4 marks.) Adult moves from 22 °C to 5 °C air; two-line graph. (a) Identify the regulated variable. (b) Explain the core temperature pattern with reference to negative feedback.
   Model: (a) Core body temperature (1 mark). (b) After the move, heat loss to the colder air increased and core temperature fell (from 37.0 to 36.6 °C), the stimulus, which thermoreceptors in the skin and hypothalamus detected (1 mark). The hypothalamus, comparing this with the set point, signalled effectors: skeletal muscles shivered (more heat produced) and skin arterioles narrowed (less heat lost), so core temperature rose back towards 37 °C (1 mark). This is negative feedback because the response opposed the fall; as temperature returned the signals weakened and the extra responses eased towards what was needed, so core temperature then varied only slightly (36.8 to 37.0 °C) while air temperature stayed at 5 °C (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed out: "regulated variable: air temperature" (the 2019 Q29 marker comment). Other wrong chains named in the review: "temperature stayed constant" (the graph shows a dip and oscillation); naming sweating as the effector.
