## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route), answered as a cause.** "How can two graphical methods measure gravitational acceleration reliably?" The answer, built in order: a fall is too quick to time by hand, and one result can't show whether a method works (1); v09's equations predict two straight-line graphs for a ball released from rest, s against t² with gradient ½g and v against t with gradient g (2); a gradient from a best-fit line through repeated, electronically timed data uses every point, not one pair (3 to 7); repeats then show two separate qualities, accuracy (closeness to the accepted value) and reliability (agreement between repeats) (8); a method can be reliable but not accurate when a systematic error shifts every run the same way, and more repeats cannot remove that (9); each kind of error has its own fix (9).
- **Hook surprise (site orient, made visible).** A ball dropped 1.000 m lands in about 0.45 s; a stopwatch user's reaction time, about 0.2 s, is shown as a shaded band nearly half the fall time. "And even with a perfect timer, one result can't tell you if your method is any good." No people are drawn: the stopwatch plate and a coded time bar carry it.
- **Why t² (owner physics bar: cause before rule).** Scene 2 plots the method 1 points against t first: a curve. The horizontal axis then re-labels to t² and the points slide sideways into a straight line (each point's x moves from t to t², computed). The student sees why the method squares the time before being told the gradient is ½g.
- **The two methods (site methods 1 and 2), with real school apparatus.** Method 1, bench top: an electromagnet on a stand holds a steel ball; switching it off releases the ball and starts a millisecond timer; the ball lands on a trapdoor switch that stops it; five heights 0.200 m to 1.000 m. Method 2, a longer fall: a ball dropped about 2.5 m from a landing beside a vertical tape, filmed at a high frame rate; video analysis software (the site activity's "analyses video frames") gives the velocity at each instant. The site's `we-1` numbers (2.0 m/s at 0.20 s, 6.9 m/s at 0.70 s) are kept exactly, as two points read off method 2's best-fit line; their 0.70 s of fall implies a drop of about 2.4 m, which is why method 2 needs the landing (author note b).
- **Gradient from a best-fit line, not two raw readings (site correction, logged).** Both methods read the gradient from two points far apart on the line of best fit ("the straight line that passes as close as possible to all the points"). This corrects the site worked example's implication that one two-point slope matching 9.8 is "a good result".
- **Accuracy and reliability, defined by what is compared (site card; logged correction applied).** Accuracy: how close a result is to the accepted value, 9.80 m/s² for Sydney (an appropriate local reference, as the logged correction asks; author note c). Reliability: how closely repeated results agree. Shown with two rows of three repeat values on one number line: method 1 tight and low (9.61, 9.62, 9.63; mean 9.62, range 0.02; percentage error 1.8%), method 2 centred and spread (9.6, 9.8, 10.0; mean 9.8, range 0.4). The number line makes "tight but off" and "centred but spread" visible before the words "systematic" and "random" are used for them.
- **Prediction (3 s countdown, scene 9; site misconception 2).** "Would ten more runs bring method 1's mean up to 9.80?" Answer by reasoning: all three runs are low by about the same amount, so something shifts every run the same way (a systematic error); averaging removes random scatter, not a shift. The cause shown: the magnet takes a few milliseconds to let go after the timer starts, so every time is too long and g comes out low (author note d). On the number line, ten ghost repeats pile up around 9.62, not 9.80.
- **Random error made visible (scene 6).** Video frames blur a fast ball, so each tracked position is slightly off, sometimes high, sometimes low; the v-t points scatter about the line. This is the cause of method 2's wider range. Fix: a shorter exposure and reliable tracking to cut the blur, and more repeats to reduce the effect of random error on the mean (a higher frame rate alone does not do this).
- **Outlier (scene 4; P25-FA0-G5-05 in part).** In run 1, the first timing at 0.600 m reads 0.372 s, which plots well right of the line (t² = 0.138 s² against about 0.126 s² expected). It is circled, the drop is repeated (0.355 s), and the repeat fits. The grey tag gives a plausible cause ("possible cause: ball clipped the trapdoor?"), not a certainty; the narration only repeats the drop (Revision 2 reserve cut).
- **Variables (scene 3; P25-FA0-G2-01 in part).** Method 1 only: independent variable the drop height (chosen), dependent variable the fall time (measured), controlled variables the same ball, release from rest, the same timer and trapdoor, height measured the same way (bottom of the ball to the trapdoor). Method 2 has no independent variable in the same sense (one drop, many instants), so variables are not forced onto it.
- **Significant figures (P25-FA0-G5-07 in part).** Method 1: heights to the millimetre and times to the millisecond give three-figure data, so g to three figures (9.61); the gradient keeps a fourth figure as an intermediate (4.806). Method 2: velocities read to 0.1 m/s (two figures at 2.0), so g to two figures (9.8). Said in one sentence each.

- **Site errors found (this read, 2 Nov; for `../site-corrections-phys-y11.md`; the "a good result" line is already logged and is not repeated here):**
  1. `assessing-accuracy-and-reliability`: "The velocity–time method is often more accurate because plotting many points averages out random timing errors." Both methods plot many points and fit a line, so fitting many points does not by itself show that the velocity–time method is more accurate. Fitting reduces the effect of random error in either method; neither fit removes a systematic error such as a timer delay, and a result can contain both kinds. Proposed: "Both methods use a line of best fit through many points, which reduces the effect of random errors. Neither removes a systematic error, such as a timer delay or air resistance."
  2. Short-answer Q2 model answer: "a constant timing offset shifts the s against t² line without changing its gradient as much." A constant offset in t shifts a v-t line sideways and leaves its gradient unchanged, but it does not simply shift an s-t² graph: t² becomes t² + 2tδ + δ², so the gradient changes. The same answer's claim that air resistance "distorts the v against t gradient more" is unsupported. Proposed: "A constant timing offset leaves the v-t gradient unchanged but changes the s-t² gradient; air resistance lowers both."
  3. Method 1 card: "a straight line through the origin with gradient ½g". True only when distance and time are both measured from the moment of release from rest. Proposed: add "if the object is released from rest and both s and t are measured from the release".

### 1. hook (too fast to time)
> Drop a ball from one metre, and it lands in about nought point four five seconds. With a stopwatch, your reaction time is about a fifth of a second, nearly half of what you're **measuring**. And even with a perfect timer, one result can't tell you if your method is any good. So how can two different graphs measure g, the acceleration of a falling object, and how do we decide which result to **trust**?

The gradient of the line of best fit is 4.85 m s⁻². (a) Calculate the value of g obtained. (b) Calculate the percentage error, taking the accepted value as 9.80 m s⁻². Model answer: (a) s = ½gt², so the gradient is ½g; g = 2 × 4.85 = 9.70 m s⁻² (1 mark). (b) % error = |9.70 − 9.80| ÷ 9.80 × 100 = 1.0% (1 mark). (c) No: reliability is how closely repeated results agree; one result has nothing to agree with, so repeat the whole experiment and compare the values (2 marks: definition, and the need for repeats). Classic mistake (crossed): "reliable, because it is close to 9.80" (closeness is accuracy).

**Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording; 4 marks suggested).** "Three repeat runs of a velocity–time method gave values of g of 9.91, 9.93 and 9.95 m s⁻². The accepted value is 9.80 m s⁻². Model answer: mean = 9.93 m s⁻², range = 0.04 m s⁻²: the repeats agree closely, so the results are reliable (1 mark). All three are shifted the same way by a similar amount, which suggests a systematic error is most likely (1 mark); the readings agree closely, so random error appears small (1 mark).
