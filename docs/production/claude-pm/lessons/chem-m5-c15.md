### 1. hook (insoluble, yet no stone)
> This is a kidney stone. Most are mainly **calcium oxalate**, which the solubility rules call insoluble. And urine always contains some **calcium ions** and some **oxalate ions**. Yet most of the time, **no stone** forms. So "insoluble" can't be the whole story. A solid only forms when there are **enough** ions. How many is enough?

### 2. Qsp (the same expression, right now)
> Ksp describes a saturated solution, at equilibrium. For the mixture in front of you right now, use the same expression with the concentrations you have **at this moment**. That's the **ion product**, **Q s p**. Just as Q is compared with K, it tells you which way things have to go. It uses the same **powers** too: for lead iodide, Qsp is lead times iodide squared.

Visual: two cards side by side. Left "Ksp = [Ca2+][SO4 2-] at equilibrium (saturated)" with the C14 saturated beaker thumbnail. ### 3. the decision (over, equal, under)
> If **Qsp is bigger than Ksp**, there are more ions than a saturated solution can hold. Ions lock together, and a **precipitate** forms, until Qsp falls back to Ksp. If Qsp **equals** Ksp, the solution is just saturated. If Qsp is **smaller**, nothing precipitates: the solution could even dissolve more.

Visual: the C8 log number line, fixed teal Ksp marker in the middle (tag "Ksp: saturated"). On "bigger than Ksp" the coral Qsp marker lands to the right; a lens shows crowded ions locking into a small white cluster that sinks (ported Qsp motion, 1 : 1); the Qsp marker slides back to the Ksp mark as the solid forms (tag "until Qsp = Ksp"). On "equals" the marker sits on the mark (tag "saturated"). On "smaller" it lands to the left; the lens shows sparse ions, tag "no precipitate; could dissolve more". A rule card writes: "Qsp > Ksp: precipitate. Qsp = Ksp: saturated. Qsp < Ksp: no precipitate." A chip "Qsp < Ksp: precipitate" fades in and is crossed. Hold about 2 seconds.

### 4. mixing spreads the ions (why dilute first)
> Here's the step most people miss. When two solutions are mixed, each ion spreads into the **combined volume**. Mix 50 millilitres with 50 millilitres, and every ion now has **100 millilitres** to move in, so each concentration **halves**. Work out the concentrations **after mixing** first, then Qsp.

### 7. unequal volumes: work through moles

### 10. the common ion effect (Ksp stays put)
> One more case. Here's a saturated silver chloride solution, with solid on the bottom. Now add some **sodium chloride**. Chloride is already part of this equilibrium, so it's a **common ion**. The chloride concentration jumps, so Qsp is suddenly **bigger than Ksp**. Silver ions and chloride ions lock onto the solid until Qsp is back down to Ksp: the equilibrium shifts **left**, as Le Chatelier's principle predicts. So **less** silver chloride stays dissolved. But Ksp itself **hasn't changed**: only temperature changes it.

### 11b. solve, check and compare
> Ksp is s times 0.10. **Divide both sides by 0.10**: s is **1.77 times ten to the minus nine**. That's far under 5 per cent of 0.10, so dropping it was safe. ### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Qsp (ion product) uses the Ksp expression with the current ion concentrations, including the powers. Example: Qsp(PbI2) = [Pb2+][I-]^2.
2. Qsp > Ksp: a precipitate forms until Qsp = Ksp. Qsp = Ksp: saturated. Qsp < Ksp: no precipitate.
3. Mixing: find concentrations after mixing first. n = c x V for each ion, then divide by the total volume (equal volumes halve each concentration, and skipping this makes a 1 : 1 Qsp four times too big).
4. Common ion: adding an ion already in the equilibrium raises Qsp above Ksp, so solid forms and solubility falls. Ksp is unchanged (only temperature changes it).
5. Solubility with a common ion: initial row is not zero, e.g. AgCl in 0.10 mol/L Cl-: s x 0.10 ≈ Ksp, s = 1.77 x 10^-9 mol/L (check s is under 5% of 0.10).

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *Dilute first. Then Qsp. Over Ksp, it falls out.* Condition tag: "same powers as Ksp; Ksp at the same temperature".

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why "insoluble" ions can coexist without a solid: a solid needs enough ions (1). Why Qsp above Ksp means a precipitate: more ions than a saturated solution holds, so they lock together until Qsp equals Ksp (3). Why concentrations fall on mixing: the same ions spread through more volume (4). Why the undiluted Qsp is four times too big: each of two concentrations is doubled (6). Why moles first for unequal volumes: moles do not change on mixing, volume does (7). Why a common ion lowers solubility: Qsp jumps above Ksp, so solid forms (10). Why Ksp does not change: it depends only on temperature (10, 15). Why s can be dropped next to 0.10: s is tiny, checked against 5 per cent (11).
