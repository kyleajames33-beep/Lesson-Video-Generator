### 1. hook: two thousand dollars, due today
> Two hundred and thirty-three dollars left over every week. That's a healthy budget. Then the hatchback needs a two thousand dollar repair, and the mechanic wants paying today. The surplus would cover it, eventually. Two thousand divided by two thirty-three is nearly nine weeks of saving. But the bill is due **now**. Without savings, it goes on a credit card or a payment plan, and it costs more. So how do saving, an emergency fund and tracking your spending protect you from money **trouble**?

### 2. living expenses
> The answer is an emergency fund, savings kept for surprises, like a repair, or a gap in pay. But how big? It's measured in living expenses, what you'd still have to pay if your pay stopped. Look at last time's budget. If your pay stopped tomorrow, you'd still pay rent, your phone, food, the car and the bills. That's the fixed costs and the variable essentials, two sixty-eight plus two thirty-four, five hundred and two dollars a **week**. But you'd stop eating out and cut the extras, so the discretionary spending stays **out**. Building the fund on everything you spend makes the target bigger than it needs to **be**.

### 3. how big, and how long to build?
> The usual guide is three to six months of living expenses. A year has fifty-two weeks, so three months is thirteen weeks, and six months is twenty-six. Three months is five oh two times thirteen, six thousand five hundred and twenty-six **dollars**. Six months is thirteen thousand and fifty-**two**. Now, how long to build the smaller one, saving the whole surplus? Sixty-five twenty-six divided by two thirty-three is twenty-eight point oh one. Watch the jar at twenty-eight weeks. It's two dollars short. You can't half-save a week, so round up. It takes twenty-nine **weeks**. Rounding down leaves you short when the bill **arrives**.

### 4. save first
> Twenty-nine weeks only works if the money is actually saved. Here's the trick most people use. Save first. Move the money to savings on payday, before you spend anything. An automatic transfer does it for you. What's left in your everyday account is what's left to spend, so the saving can't get eaten by small purchases during the **week**. Keep the fund in a separate account, so it's there for surprises, and not for **shopping**.

### 5. monitoring: actual against budget
> A budget is a plan. Monitoring is checking whether you stuck to it, regularly. For each category, the variance is the actual spending minus the budget. Say one week you spent ninety-two eating out, against sixty. That's plus thirty-two, overspent. Entertainment was thirty-one, against forty, minus nine. Clothes were on budget, and groceries were plus sixteen. Keep the signs and add them, plus thirty-nine **dollars**. If every week went like that, the surplus would drop to a hundred and ninety-four, and the fund would take thirty-four weeks, not twenty-**nine**. Small overspends quietly add five weeks. Adding the numbers without their signs gives fifty-seven, which isn't what **happened**.

### 6. three months or six?
> So should the fund be three months, or six? That's a judgement about risk, so back it with numbers. With a steady wage, pay is unlikely to stop suddenly, so three months, six and a half thousand dollars, is a sensible **target**. With casual or changing hours, pay can drop without warning, and it can take longer to recover. Then six months, about thirteen thousand dollars, is safer, even though it takes fifty-seven weeks to build instead of twenty-**nine**. A good answer names the situation, and quotes the **numbers**.

### 7. four strategies, and the memory aid
> Put it together, and each strategy answers a different question. A budget asks, can my pay cover my life? Saving first asks, does the saving actually happen? An emergency fund asks, what if a big bill comes, or my pay stops? And monitoring asks, am I sticking to the plan? No single one is enough on its own. So, budget, save first, keep a fund, and check your spending regularly, every week or **month**.
