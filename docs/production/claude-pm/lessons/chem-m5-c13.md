Main question: *When you mix two clear solutions of ionic compounds, how can you tell whether a solid will form, what it is, and what's left dissolved?*
Entry knowledge (C11 and Year 11): dissolved ionic compounds are separate hydrated ions (C11); ionic formulas from charges, polyatomic ions, balancing, state symbols, precipitation reactions met in Module 3 (Year 11).
Stops after: listing the ions in two solutions; swapping partners and checking both products against supplied or learned rules; naming the precipitate (and its colour for the three syllabus pairs); writing the molecular, full ionic and net ionic equations with spectators identified and charge checked; recognising "no precipitate" with no net ionic equation; stating which ions remain dissolved after filtering, including an excess reagent; choosing a test solution to tell two ions apart. No Ksp, no Qsp, no water-treatment selectivity, no conduct of the investigation.
Handoff: investigating the solubility rules yourself (route chem-m5-p03, practical support); then Ksp, putting a number on "insoluble" (chem-m5-c14).

### 1. hook (two clear solutions, one yellow solid)
> Here are two clear, colourless solutions: **potassium iodide** and **lead nitrate**. Add a few drops of one to the other, and a **bright yellow solid** appears and slowly settles. Neither bottle had anything yellow in it. So where did the solid come from? And what's left in the liquid around it?

### 2. what's really in each bottle (four kinds of ions)
> As you saw in the dissolving video, potassium iodide solution isn't full of K I units. It's **potassium ions and iodide ions**, each surrounded by water, moving separately. Lead nitrate solution is **lead ions and nitrate ions**. So when you mix them, you have **four kinds of ions** in one tube. The only new thing that can happen is that ions meet **new partners**: lead with iodide, or potassium with nitrate.

Visual: a particle lens opens above each bottle: amber K+ and violet I- with water shells; amber Pb2+ and violet NO3- with water shells. On "four kinds of ions" the two lenses slide together and merge into one lens over the tube, all four kinds mingling (tag "4 kinds of ion"). On "new partners" two pen arcs link Pb2+ to I- and K+ to NO3-, labelled "new pair?". Hold about 1 second.

### 3. the precipitate and the spectators
> Lead ions and iodide ions attract each other more strongly than water can keep them apart, so they lock into a solid lattice of **lead iodide** that comes out of the solution. A solid that forms like this is a **precipitate**. Potassium and nitrate ions stay dissolved, exactly as they were, so they take no part. Ions like that are called **spectator ions**. So after mixing, the tube holds **solid lead iodide**, with potassium ions and nitrate ions still dissolved in the water.

Visual: in the merged lens (ported motion idea), Pb2+ and I- ions shed their water shells, lock into a small yellow lattice cluster (1 Pb2+ to 2 I-) and sink; on "precipitate" the term and plain meaning write under the lens, and the painted precipitate tube pulses. On "spectator ions" the K+ and NO3- ions keep swimming and get small grey "spectator" tags. On "solid lead iodide" a composition card writes beside the tube: "solid: PbI2 (yellow)" and "dissolved: K+, NO3-". As the composition card writes, a chip "every ionic compound dissolves in water" fades in and is crossed (screen only). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 4. where the rules come from
> So how could you know in advance which pair locks together? Chemists mixed pairs of solutions again and again and recorded which ones made a solid, and the patterns they found are the **solubility rules**, a first prediction from experiments. In the **practical video**, you'll find them yourself.

Visual: a coded 5 x 5 grid (cations down the side: Na+, Ca2+, Ba2+, Pb2+, Ag+; anions across: Cl-, I-, SO4 2-, CO3 2-, OH-) fills cell by cell with small dots: clear for no solid, a dot in the solid's real colour (most white; AgI pale yellow; PbI2 bright yellow; Ag2CO3 pale yellow; silver with hydroxide brown, author note i), a light cloud for "slight" (CaSO4, Ag2SO4, PbCl2, Ca(OH)2). On "solubility rules" a pen bracket groups the all-clear Na+ row with the tag "pattern: sodium salts all dissolve". On "practical video" a chip "Practical video: find the rules by experiment" writes with a pen "later" tag. Hold about 1 second.

### 5. the rules (NAGSAG)
> Here's the version to learn, in order: **NAGSAG**. **Nitrates, Ammonium, and Group 1** ions like sodium and potassium: always soluble, no exceptions. Then **Sulfates**: soluble, except with barium and lead, which are insoluble, and calcium and silver, which are only slightly soluble. **Acetates**: soluble. And **Group 17**, the halides chloride, bromide and iodide: soluble, except with **silver and lead**. Everything else, like **carbonates and hydroxides**, is mostly insoluble, unless it's paired with a Group 1 ion or ammonium. Barium hydroxide is one more exception: it dissolves.

Visual: the rules card replaces the grid. Six letters "N A G S A G" stack down the left, each writing its row on its cue: "N: nitrates (NO3-): soluble"; "A: ammonium (NH4+): soluble"; "G: Group 1 (Na+, K+): soluble" with a teal bracket over the first three rows, "always soluble, no exceptions". "S: sulfates (SO4 2-): soluble, except Ba2+, Pb2+ (insoluble); Ca2+, Ag+ (slightly)". "A: acetates (CH3COO-): soluble". "G: Group 17 halides (Cl-, Br-, I-): soluble, except Ag+, Pb2+" with a footnote "PbCl2: slightly soluble". On "carbonates and hydroxides" a separate box below: "Mostly insoluble: carbonates (CO3 2-), phosphates, hydroxides (OH-), sulfides, unless with Group 1 or NH4+". On "Barium hydroxide" the box adds "Ba(OH)2 soluble; Ca(OH)2 slightly". Hold about 3 seconds (reading hold; the card returns in 21 and the key notes).

### 6. the method (swap partners, check both)
> So here's the method. List the four ions, then **swap partners**: each positive ion pairs with the other solution's negative ion. That gives two possible products. Then **check both** against the rules. If one is insoluble, that's your precipitate. If both are soluble, nothing forms.

Visual: a swap cross: two compound tiles "A+ B-" and "C+ D-" on the left; on "swap partners" the cations cross over (pen arrows forming an X) to give "A+ D-" and "C+ B-". On "check both" a tick box appears under each new pair, with the card shrunk in the corner. A chip "checked one product only" fades in and is crossed (caption "check both"). Hold about 1 second.

### 7. worked example 1: potassium chloride and silver nitrate
> Try the first syllabus pair: potassium chloride and silver nitrate. Swap partners: **silver chloride** and **potassium nitrate**. Potassium nitrate is a nitrate, and potassium is Group 1, so it's **soluble**. Silver chloride is a halide with silver, one of the exceptions, so it's **insoluble**. A **white** precipitate forms. The full equation is potassium chloride plus silver nitrate gives **silver chloride solid, plus potassium nitrate, aqueous**.

Visual: the swap cross fills with "K+ Cl-" and "Ag+ NO3-", crossing to "Ag+ Cl-" and "K+ NO3-". On "soluble" the KNO3 box ticks green with "nitrate, Group 1: soluble"; on "insoluble" the AgCl box gets an amber flag "halide with Ag+: insoluble" and the rules card's Group 17 row flashes in the corner. On "white" the painted `m5x-ppt-agcl-white-v1.png` stands in the left column. On "silver chloride solid" the equation stack's first line writes: "KCl(aq) + AgNO3(aq) → AgCl(s) + KNO3(aq)" with "molecular equation" in the margin. Hold about 1 second.

### 8. the full ionic equation (split only the aqueous)
> That equation hides what's really in the tube. So write every **aqueous** compound as its separate ions. On the left: potassium ions, chloride ions, silver ions and nitrate ions. On the right, potassium nitrate splits too, but silver chloride is a **solid**, so it stays **whole**. This is the **full ionic equation**.

Visual: the ported token flight: each (aq) compound in line 1 splits and its ions fly down from their own token to line 2: "K+(aq) + Cl-(aq) + Ag+(aq) + NO3-(aq) → AgCl(s) + K+(aq) + NO3-(aq)". On "solid" the AgCl(s) token drops straight down unsplit, with a teal lock icon and tag "(s): never split". Margin label "full ionic equation". Hold about 1 second.

### 9. cancel the spectators (net ionic equation)
> Now look for ions that appear **unchanged on both sides**. Potassium and nitrate. They're the spectators, so **cross them out**. What's left is the **net ionic equation**: silver ion plus chloride ion gives silver chloride solid. Check the charges: **plus one and minus one** make zero on the left, and the solid is neutral. And this net equation is the same **wherever** the silver and chloride ions came from.

Visual: on "unchanged on both sides" matching pairs in line 2 get pen links (K+ to K+, NO3- to NO3-). On "cross them out" each pair is struck in grey on both sides. On "net ionic equation" line 3 writes: "Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) → AgCl(s)" with margin label "net ionic equation". On "plus one and minus one" a small charge tally writes under it: "(+1) + (-1) = 0 → 0". On "wherever" two ghost chips "from NaCl" and "from CaCl2" point at the same line 3, tagged "same net equation". Hold about 2 seconds.

### 10. worked example 2: the lead iodide formula
> Swap partners for the hook's pair: lead with iodide, potassium with nitrate. Potassium nitrate is soluble. Lead with a halide is an exception, so **lead iodide** is the bright yellow precipitate. But its formula isn't P b I. A lead ion is **two plus**, and an iodide ion is only **one minus**, so it takes **two iodides** to balance one lead: P b I two. That's why the full equation needs two potassium iodides, and why two potassium ions and two nitrate ions are the spectators. The net ionic equation is **lead ion plus two iodide ions gives lead iodide solid**. Charges: plus two, and two minus ones, make zero.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a solid can appear from two clear solutions: the bottles hold separate ions and mixing offers new partners, one of which holds together in a solid (2, 3). Why some ions are spectators: they stay hydrated and unchanged (3). Why the rules are trusted: they summarise experiments (4). Why both products are checked: either new pair could be the insoluble one (6). Why the solid is not split: it is not dissolved ions (8). Why spectators cancel: identical on both sides, so they describe no change (9). Why PbI2 has a 2: charges must balance (10). Why BaSO4 is safe to swallow and Ba2+ is not: dissolved ions are absorbed, the solid barely dissolves (11). Why excess ions stay in the filtrate: no partner is left (13).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** all ionic compounds dissolve because water is polar (3); checking one product (6); splitting the precipitate (8); PbI (10); "no reaction" (12); forgetting excess ions (13, 18); CuOH (16).
- **Worked-example rule:** one equation line per scene; the swap cross and the rules row it depends on are shown together; the earlier lines stay on screen only while the next line needs them; the stack clears between pairs. Atom and charge balance checked on screen for every net equation.
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen tasks (a new pair; a filtrate with an excess reagent), the supplied rules card visible as an exam stimulus would provide, every condition stated (Cu2+ is 2+; "more than enough"), 10 seconds each with the invitation to pause, nothing answer-bearing. One crossed mistake each; feedback about 20 to 28 seconds each at the measured rate.
- **Avoided double teaching:** hydration (C11) is recalled in one clause; ionic formulas and balancing (Year 11) are used, with the charge-balance reason given at the one place it is needed (10).
- **Out of scope by design:** conducting the investigation (P03); Ksp, Qsp and precipitation at given concentrations (C14, C15); water-treatment precipitant selection (pack correction 1); the Fox Talbot hook (pack correction 3); sequential ion tests (Module 8).
