### 2. the hinge: Pythagoras with a correction
> The angle between two known sides is called the included angle. It's the angle those two sides make where they meet. Picture the two fences on a hinge at the corner. Close the hinge to exactly ninety degrees, and Pythagoras gives the third side. Two hundred and forty squared plus a hundred and eighty squared is ninety thousand, so the third side would be three hundred **metres**. Now open the hinge wider. The far ends move apart, so the third side gets longer. Close it tighter, and it gets **shorter**. So the cosine rule is Pythagoras with a correction for the angle. In symbols, c squared equals a squared plus b squared, minus two a b cosine C. Here c is the side opposite the angle C, and a and b are the two sides that form it. At ninety degrees, cosine C is zero, so the correction **vanishes**.

### 3. the paddock's wire
> For the paddock, a is two hundred and forty, b is a hundred and eighty, and C is a hundred and ten degrees. Remember the half circle from last time, with its one-metre arm. The arm's height is the sine. How far it reaches sideways, adjacent over a hypotenuse of one, is the **cosine**. Past ninety degrees, the arm reaches back the other way, so the cosine is negative. At a hundred and ten degrees, it's about minus zero point three four two, the same size as cosine seventy degrees, but **backwards**. So minus two a b cosine C becomes a plus, and the correction **adds**. Two times two hundred and forty times a hundred and eighty is eighty-six thousand four hundred. Times zero point three four two, that's about twenty-nine thousand five hundred and fifty. Ninety thousand plus that is about a hundred and nineteen thousand five hundred and fifty. That's still c squared. Take the square root, and the third side is about three hundred and forty-six **metres**. Longer than three hundred, just as the open hinge **showed**.

### 4. worked example: a side

### 5. an angle from three sides
> The same rule can find an angle when all three sides are known. Rearranged, cosine C equals a squared plus b squared minus c squared, all over two a b. The side you subtract, c, is the one opposite the angle you want. Say a triangle has sides of seven, nine and thirteen, and we want its largest angle. The largest angle faces the longest side, so thirteen goes **last**. Forty-nine plus eighty-one minus a hundred and sixty-nine is minus thirty-nine, over a hundred and twenty-six. That's about minus zero point three one. A negative cosine means the arm reaches backwards, so the angle is obtuse. Every sideways reach belongs to just one angle, so inverse cosine has no choice to make. It gives a hundred and eight degrees, two **minutes**. There's nothing to subtract from a hundred and eighty here. That step was only for inverse **sine**.

### 6. which rule?
> So which rule? Look at what you're given. If you can see a side and the angle opposite it, both known, use the sine rule. If two angles are known, find the **third**, and then you have a pair. Otherwise, you'll have two sides and the angle between them, or all three sides. That's the cosine **rule**.
