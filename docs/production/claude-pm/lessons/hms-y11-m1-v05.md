### 2. five groups, from society down to the body
> The things that shape health are called **determinants**, and the syllabus sorts them into five groups. They act in order, from society down to the body. Then socioeconomic characteristics, like income, education and occupation. Then health behaviours, like diet, exercise and smoking. And last, biomedical factors, like blood pressure and weight. A clinic treats the bottom of the stack. But the top is what moves a whole population.

### 3. interaction one: chains
> These groups don't act one at a time. They interact, in three ways. The first is a chain, where one determinant produces the next. Take a rural community. Specialist services and jobs are concentrated in the cities. That puts care a long way away. Distance makes seeing a specialist cost a day's pay and petrol, so people put it off. And when they finally go, the condition is further along. Every link is ordinary. The chain is what does the damage.

### 4. clustering, feedback and the gradient
> The second way is clustering. Disadvantages tend to arrive together, low income with insecure housing, risky work and poor transport. Because they cluster, their effects compound, so together they do more harm than each would alone. The third way is feedback. Illness makes it harder to work, which cuts income, which worsens the conditions that caused the illness. Put all this together across Australia, and you get the social gradient. Health improves at every step up the income ladder, not just at the bottom. So this isn't only about poverty. It's about how resources are spread across everyone.

### 5. why people take health risks
> Now, the risky behaviours in the middle of the stack. Why do some groups smoke more, or eat worse? The syllabus asks for sociological causes, which means people's situations, not their character. There are five. Norms: where most people around you smoke, smoking takes no decision, and not smoking does. Stress: when money or housing is insecure, short-term relief is worth a lot, and smoking or comfort eating does give relief. Availability and marketing: what's cheap and close is what people eat. Control: prevention is an investment in a future you have to believe in. And belonging: some behaviours are part of fitting in.

### 6. patterned, not blameless
> That doesn't mean people have no responsibility. People in the same situation do make different choices. The point is that behaviour is patterned. It follows groups, and patterns have causes at the level of the group. So telling people to choose better misses the real constraint, which is often stress, price, environment or belonging, not a lack of knowledge.

### 8. why behaviour-only campaigns widen gaps
> Here's the most surprising idea in this topic. Picture a campaign that just gives information: eat more vegetables, walk more. To act on it, people need money, time, transport and security, the very things the determinants spread unevenly. So the better-off group improves faster. The average goes up, so the campaign looks like a success. But the gap gets wider. The fix isn't to drop information. It's to pair it with changes to conditions, like the price and availability of healthy food, safe streets and nearby services, and to target help by need.
