## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "How do we communicate a chemistry conclusion that evidence supports?" The answer, in order: a conclusion answers the question so a reader can check it (2); build it from claim, evidence, reasoning and limits (2 to 4); use cautious, precise language (5); use correct notation (6); pick the form the reader needs (7); judge others' conclusions by the same parts and give useful feedback (8, 9).

- **Hook (3 s prediction, scene 1).** Three drafts as text cards: A "The experiment worked and mass was conserved." B "This proves mass is always conserved." C "The mass lost, a mean of 0.82 g over three runs, was close to the 0.88 g of carbon dioxide predicted, which supports conservation of mass." Which would a scientist trust? C. See "Memory aid check".
- **The model conclusion (built in scenes 3 and 4; shown whole in scene 14 and the notes).** "Claim: the mass lost closely matched the predicted mass of carbon dioxide. Evidence: mean loss 0.82 g (three runs, 0.81 to 0.83 g) against 0.88 g predicted from 2.00 g CaCO₃. Reasoning: CO₂(g) crossed the system boundary, so the balance lost its mass; atoms are not destroyed, so if all the gas formed escapes and nothing else leaves, the loss should equal the gas formed. Limits: the mean was about 7% below the prediction in every run, which suggests a systematic error: probably some CO₂ stayed in the flask (dissolved or in the air space), so it was weighed with the flask rather than counted as lost; swirl gently at the end. ### 2. what a conclusion needs
> Draft C. A conclusion answers the investigation's question, and a reader should be able to check every part of it. Draft A gives no numbers, so there's nothing to check. The reasoning, the chemistry that links the evidence to the claim. And the limits, what makes the result less **certain**. Claim, evidence, reasoning, then the **limits**.

### 5. supports, not proves
> Now the words. Why does "proves" fail? Three runs of one reaction can't prove a law for every reaction, and our results weren't even exact. Scientists say evidence supports a claim, or is consistent with it, because new evidence could always change the picture. And use the course terms precisely. Our runs agree closely, so they're precise. The whole experiment repeated consistently, so it's reliable. But the results sit below the prediction, so they're not fully accurate, and calling them accurate would claim more than we **showed**.

But for a talk, or a digital slide for your class, cut it to the claim, one graph and the main limit, because listeners can't go back and reread. Pick the form by what your audience needs to **see**.

Saying "good job" doesn't help anyone. "Add your mean mass lost, with units" **does**.

## Memory aid check
