### 2. efficiency and cardiac output
> **Stroke volume** is the blood the left ventricle pushes out per beat. **Cardiac output** is the blood moved per minute: heart rate times stroke volume. At rest, about seventy beats of seventy millilitres, roughly five litres. Endurance training enlarges the left ventricle's chamber, so each beat pushes out more. The runner's hundred and twenty millilitres a beat, times forty-two, makes about five litres. The untrained heart's sixty-six needs seventy-six beats. Same output, fewer beats. That's **efficiency**: the oxygen the body needs, at the lowest cost, with room to rise. So the low heart rate is a sign of fitness, not the cause.

### 3. three links, and the cost of keeping up
> Delivery runs through three links. Link one, **loading**: oxygen crosses into the blood at the alveoli. Link two, **carrying**: haemoglobin holds about two hundred millilitres of oxygen in every litre of blood. Link three, **delivering**: the heart and vessels move that blood to the muscle. Five litres a minute times two hundred millilitres a litre gives the litre a minute on our readout. When a link weakens, the heart usually beats faster to keep delivery up. Delivery is saved, but each litre of oxygen costs more heartbeats. That's lost efficiency.

### 5. acclimatisation
> Over two to four weeks, the body adapts. That's **acclimatisation**. The kidneys sense low oxygen and release a hormone, erythropoietin, or E P O, which makes more red blood cells. Haemoglobin rises, so each litre carries more again. The body repairs link one by strengthening link two, and muscles grow more capillaries. That's why athletes live high and train low: they sleep at altitude for the adaptation, and come down to train at full intensity.

### 6. haemoglobin levels

That's **anaemia**: too little haemoglobin. The commonest cause is low iron, the atom that holds the oxygen. The risk is highest in teenage girls who menstruate, endurance athletes, and low-iron diets.

### 7. vascular disease: when the pipes fail
> Link three can fail even with a good pump and good blood. In **atherosclerosis**, fatty plaque builds up in artery walls over decades. It narrows the channel, so less blood gets through, and stiffens the wall, so the artery can't widen to send more blood to working muscle. Where it happens decides the damage. In the coronary arteries, which supply the heart muscle, it causes chest pain on exertion, called **angina**, and a full blockage causes a heart attack. In the legs, walking hurts. In the brain, it can cause a stroke.

### 8. two big hearts
> Stiff, narrow arteries also raise blood pressure, which sets a trap. The left ventricle must push harder, so its wall thickens. The runner's heart is bigger too. So ask what grew. In the runner, the chamber grew, so stroke volume rises. With high blood pressure, the wall grew, the chamber has less room to fill, and stroke volume falls. ## Memory aid check

- **"Load, carry, deliver: name the broken link; the heart pays in beats."** Assumptions: the three links are in series, so delivery is limited by any one; compensation is mainly by raising cardiac output, usually through heart rate. Exceptions worth knowing (author note, not learner copy): at maximal effort heart rate cannot rise further, so a broken link lowers maximum delivery (VO₂ max) instead of raising cost; altitude also raises ventilation (a respiratory cost). The card still holds for "at a given workload", which is how the video uses it.
- **"Altitude: short of pressure, not of oxygen."** Assumption: "oxygen" means its fraction in air (about 21%). Correct; the partial pressure (the amount per breath) is what falls.
