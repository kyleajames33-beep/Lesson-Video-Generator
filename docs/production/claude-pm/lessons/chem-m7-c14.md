Syllabus dotpoint (as printed, with its examples):

"model and compare the structure, properties and uses of addition polymers of ethylene and related monomers, for example:
- polyethylene (PE)
- polyvinyl chloride (PVC)
- polystyrene (PS)
- polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) (ACSCH136)"

This video covers how addition polymerisation opens the C=C bond, drawing a repeat unit from a monomer and a monomer from a repeat unit, why the chains form a solid that softens on heating (thermoplastic behaviour), and how branching (LDPE against HDPE) and side groups (PVC, PS, PTFE) set properties and uses. Condensation polymers are chem-m7-c15; the polymer practice video is chem-m7-g04 (route review change 4).

**Boundary:** this video starts by opening the alkene double bond, and stops at linking repeat-unit structure to properties and uses. It teaches: a monomer is a small molecule that joins into a long chain, the polymer (many parts); in addition polymerisation the weaker second bond of each C=C breaks (c04a), leaving each carbon one spare bond, which joins it to the next monomer; an initiator opens the first double bond and each new chain end opens the next (one sentence, no mechanism); nothing else forms, so every monomer atom ends up in the chain; the repeat unit: the monomer redrawn with a single bond, a continuation bond out of each backbone carbon, square brackets crossed by those bonds, and n outside; the two carbons from the double bond form the backbone and everything else hangs off as a side group; the equation n CH₂=CHCl → [–CH₂–CHCl–]ₙ; going backwards: take the two backbone carbons, remove the continuation bonds and restore the C=C, adding no hydrogens; the chains are very long and lie alongside each other, so dispersion forces acting along the whole contact hold them together and the polymer is a solid; heating lets the chains move and slide past each other, so the plastic softens and can be remoulded, and cooling restores the forces; the covalent bonds in the chains do not break; this is a thermoplastic, which is what makes melt recycling possible; no bonds join one chain to another, only these forces (Revision 2); LDPE and HDPE share the monomer and repeat unit but differ in branching (made under different conditions): branches stop chains packing closely, so less contact, weaker dispersion forces, lower density, softer, lower melting (about 110 °C; squeeze bottles, films); straight chains pack closely with neatly lined-up regions, so more contact, stronger forces, higher density, stiffer, higher melting (about 130 °C; crates, milk bottles); PVC: the polar C–Cl bond adds dipole–dipole attraction between chains, so plain PVC is rigid (pipes); plasticisers between the chains push them apart and make it flexible (cable coating); polystyrene: the bulky, stiff benzene ring on every second carbon catches on the rings of neighbouring chains, so at room temperature the chains can hardly slide, and it is rigid and brittle (Revision 2: the cause is restricted sliding, not poor packing, which scene 6 links to softness); blown with gas it becomes a light insulating foam (cups); PTFE: every H replaced by F; C–F bonds are among the strongest single bonds carbon forms and the F atoms wrap the backbone, so very few chemicals attack it (chemical resistance comes from these bonds, not from forces between chains); C–F bonds are polar, but the even fluorine coating makes their pulls largely cancel, and fluorine holds its electrons so tightly that they hardly shift, so other substances make only very weak dispersion forces with the surface (c03), so almost nothing sticks (non-stick pans, plumber's tape). **Not here:** condensation polymers, PET and nylon (chem-m7-c15; the PET bottle in the hook plate gets a grey forward tag); polymerisation conditions and the radical mechanism (initiation, propagation, termination by name; author note a); tacticity and crystallinity numbers (author note b); thermosets beyond one grey chip (site L23 extension; author note c); environmental persistence, microplastics, recycling codes and bioplastics (site L21 card 4 and L23, beyond the 2017 dotpoints; route review change 4; ### 2. opening the double bond, again and again
> A small molecule that joins up into a long chain is a **monomer**, and the chain is a polymer, meaning many parts. Remember, an alkene's double bond has a weaker second bond. A starter molecule called an initiator opens the first double bond, and each new chain end opens the next one, thousands of times. Nothing else is made. Every atom of every monomer ends up in the **chain**.

### 5. why a solid, and why it softens
> So why is polyethylene a solid, when ethene is a gas? Each chain is huge, and it lies alongside its neighbours for thousands of carbons. Dispersion forces act all along that **contact**, so together they hold the chains firmly. No bonds join one chain to another, only these forces. Warm the plastic, and the chains move faster, until they can **slide** past each other, so it softens and can be moulded. Cool it, and the forces hold the chains again. The covalent bonds inside each chain don't break. A plastic that softens like this, again and again, is a **thermoplastic**, and that's what lets it be melted down and recycled.

### 6. LDPE and HDPE: branches decide

### 9. PTFE: a fluorine sheath

- **Does it explain causes?** Why the monomer must be an alkene: only the double bond's weaker second bond can open and leave a spare bond on each carbon (2). Why no hydrogens are added when recovering the monomer: restoring the C=C completes each carbon's four bonds (4). Why it softens and can be recycled: no bonds join one chain to another, so heat lets chains slide without breaking chain bonds (5). Why LDPE is softer and less dense: branches stop packing, so less contact and weaker forces (6). Why polystyrene is rigid and brittle, and foam insulates: bulky, stiff rings catch on neighbouring chains, so the chains can hardly slide; trapped air (8). Why PTFE resists chemicals and is non-stick: strong C–F bonds under a fluorine sheath (resistance, not forces between chains); the even sheath cancels the C–F pulls and tightly held electrons hardly shift, so only weak surface forces form (9).
- **Misconceptions addressed:** the C=C stays in the polymer (3, crossed chip); hydrogens are added when recovering the monomer (4; feedback 1, crossed); melting breaks the chains (5, said positively; feedback 2, crossed); LDPE and HDPE have different monomers (6, said positively); LDPE is stiffer than HDPE (6, said positively); PTFE's C–F bonds are non-polar (9, said positively); poor packing always makes a plastic soft (8, the "You might expect" prediction beat, Revision 2).
- **One idea at a time:** one model per scene (a monomer row and its chain; one repeat unit; one reverse; one chain box with one change at a time: heat, branches, Cl, rings, F sheath); the comparison builds one row at a time.
- **Response opportunity:** two unseen HSC-style tasks with every condition visible (the monomer's formula and the drawn repeat unit in A1; both polymers, both uses, the shared monomer and both demands in A2), 10 seconds each with the invitation to pause, nothing answer-bearing. One crossed mistake each; feedback lengths under "Narration length estimate".
- **Avoided double teaching:** the weaker second bond and addition are c04a callbacks (bond opener reused); dispersion forces, contact and branching are c03 callbacks (spaghetti chip); dipole–dipole from a polar bond is a c08b callback.

- **Out of scope by design:** mechanisms and conditions; thermoset chemistry; environmental lifecycle; natural polymers; polypropylene as a taught example.
