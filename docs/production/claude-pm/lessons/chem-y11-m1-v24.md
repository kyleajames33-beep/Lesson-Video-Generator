### 2. the electron sea model
> In a piece of metal, each atom gives up its outer electrons, which are called valence electrons. They don't go to another atom. They spread out through the whole piece of metal, so they're delocalised, meaning they aren't tied to any one atom. What's left behind are positive ions, called cations, packed in a regular **lattice**. So a metal is a lattice of cations in a sea of moving **electrons**. The metallic bond is the attraction between the positive cations and the negative electron **sea**.

Visual: inside the lens, neutral copper atoms in a grid; as the first sentences run, each atom's outer electron (blue) lifts off and wanders between the atoms, tag "delocalised: not tied to any one atom". ### 3. no fixed direction
> Here's the key feature. In a covalent bond, the shared electrons sit between two particular atoms, so the bond points in one direction. In a metal, every cation is attracted to the electrons all around it, in every direction. The metallic bond has no fixed **direction**. Chemists call it **non-directional**, and that's what lets a metal bend.

Visual: at the left a small v23 ball-and-stick bond with its shared pair between two atoms, a single arrow along it, tag "covalent: one direction". At the right one cation in the lattice is ringed; faint blue attraction lines draw to electrons all around it. On "direction" the lines pulse together. On "non-directional" the tag writes "metallic bond: non-directional" (44 px); hand tags ink "one way" with a pen arrow on the covalent bond, blue "every way" with pen rays round the ringed cation.

### 4. electrons carry the current
> First, conductivity. A current is a flow of charge, so a conductor needs charged particles that can move. Connect a battery, and the delocalised electrons drift toward the positive **terminal**, while new electrons flow in from the negative end. The cations stay where they are. They only **vibrate**. So metals conduct as solids, because their electrons are already free to move. In the salt crystal, the ions are locked in place until it melts or **dissolves**.

### 5. layers slide, attraction stays
> Now hit the metal. The force pushes one layer of cations to slide over the next **layer**. After the slide, each cation still has the electron sea all around it, so it's still attracted on every **side**. Nothing has to pull apart. In a salt crystal, the same slide would put positive ions next to positive ions. They'd repel, and the crystal would **split**. In the metal, the electron sea keeps the attraction going, so the metal changes shape instead of **breaking**.

Bending moves the cations, but it doesn't trap the electrons. The electron sea is still continuous through the bent wire, so it still **conducts**.

### 7. charge carriers: three kinds of solid
> Here's a pattern exam questions love. To conduct, a substance needs charged particles that are free to move. In a metal, those are the delocalised electrons, so it conducts as a solid and as a **liquid**. In an ionic compound, they're the ions, so it conducts only when molten or **dissolved**. And in a molecular substance, like sugar or pure water, the outer electrons are held in bonds and lone pairs, so there's almost nothing free to carry the **charge**.

### 10. alloys: bumps in the layers
> Now mix in other atoms. An alloy is a metal mixed with other elements. The gold-coloured Australian coins from the first video in this series are mostly copper, with some aluminium and **nickel**. The alloy is harder than pure copper, which helps the coins resist **wear**. But the electron sea is still there, so it still **conducts**.

### 16. handoff: carbon, two ways
> So in metals, delocalised electrons explain both bending and conducting. But metals aren't the only solids with delocalised electrons. ## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 12; original wording, HSC style).** "Aluminium can be rolled into thin foil, and the foil still conducts electricity. Explain both of these properties in terms of the structure and bonding of aluminium. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Aluminium consists of a lattice of Al³⁺ cations in a sea of delocalised valence electrons, held by non-directional metallic bonding (1). When rolled, layers of cations slide over one another while the electron sea keeps every cation attracted, so the metal changes shape without breaking (malleable) (1). The delocalised electrons remain free to move through the deformed metal, so the foil still conducts electricity (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "the metallic bonds break when it's rolled".

**Attempt 2 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style).** "Sterling silver is an alloy of about 92.5% silver and 7.5% copper. Explain why sterling silver is harder than pure silver, with reference to its structure. (3 marks)"
Model answer: Pure silver has a regular lattice of identical cations, so layers slide over one another easily (1). In sterling silver, copper atoms of a different (smaller) size occupy some lattice positions and disrupt the regular layers (1). Layers cannot slide past these irregularities as easily, so more force is needed to deform the alloy, making it harder (1). Classic mistake (crossed): "harder because it contains more atoms".
