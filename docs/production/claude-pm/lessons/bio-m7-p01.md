- **Main teaching move:** a puzzle. Eight cups, one secret starter, three rounds of mixing, and every cup turns pink. Who started it? The final colours cannot tell you. The general move students keep: a final test shows who is infected now, not who was first or when; timing plus contacts finds a source.
- **The simulation (fluid exchange with an indicator).** Each student has a numbered cup with 10 mL of liquid. Seven cups hold water; one, chosen secretly by the teacher, holds dilute sodium carbonate solution (about 0.1 mol/L), a base. In each round, each student pairs with a new partner; one pours all of their liquid into the partner's cup, the pair swirls it, then pours half back, so both cups again hold about 10 mL of the same mixture. Before round one and after every round, each student puts three drops from their cup into their own column of a well plate (a sample saved for each time point). Everyone records their partner on the class contact sheet. After round three, the teacher adds one drop of phenolphthalein indicator to every cup, then to every well. Pink means the cup or sample contains base ("infected"). **Controls:** a negative control cup (10 mL of the same water, never mixed) and a positive control cup (10 mL of the sodium carbonate solution) each receive indicator. **Repeats:** the class runs the whole simulation again with a different secret starter (and, if time allows, a different pairing list). Eight students and three rounds are used on screen so every cup, well and contact fits; a class of 20 to 30 runs the same method.
- **Worked dataset (invented for the video, checked below).** Students A to H; secret starter D. Round 1 pairs: A with B, C with D, E with F, G with H. Round 2: A with C, B with E, D with G, F with H. Round 3: A with H, B with D, C with F, E with G. Infected after each round: start D; after round 1 C, D; after round 2 A, C, D, G; after round 3 all eight. New cases per round: 1 (start), 1, 2, 4. Every pairing in round 3 joins one infected and one uninfected cup, so the count doubles each round, which makes the person-to-person shape plain. The tracing logic: after round 1, C and D are pink and were partners, so one of them started it; only the saved start sample can tell which (D).
- **Why phenolphthalein and sodium carbonate.** Phenolphthalein is colourless below about pH 8.2 and pink above about pH 10 (Year 11 Chemistry students may know it; Biology-only students get a plain meaning: "a chemical that changes colour"). Each mixing halves the base concentration in the receiving pair at most, so after three rounds the most dilute infected cup holds roughly 1/8 to 1/16 of the starting concentration; 0.1 mol/L sodium carbonate diluted 16-fold (about 0.006 mol/L) is still about pH 11, comfortably pink. This also gives scene 9's limit: after many more rounds the base would dilute until the pink fades (a false negative), unlike a pathogen that multiplies. Sodium carbonate is chosen over sodium hydroxide because it is far less corrosive at this strength (author note; schools also use dilute sodium hydroxide, which needs stricter eye protection).

### 8. the epidemic curve
> Now count the new cases each round: one at the start, one after round one, two after round two, four after round three. ## Review notes for Claude (author, not learner copy)

- **Pronunciation.** "phenolphthalein" as "fee-nol-THAY-leen"; "sodium carbonate" as written; "fomite" as "FOH-mite"; letters A to H and P to U as letters; "ten millilitres" in full.
- **Doubts for Claude.** (a) Scene 2 calls mixing "like direct contact"; the fluid exchange best models exchange of body fluids or close contact, so "like" is deliberate. (b) Scene 7 names contact tracing in one line, with b10 owning it as a control measure. (c) Scene 8's doubling depends on the chosen pairs (every round-3 pair joins one pink and one clear cup); in real classes, some rounds pair two infected cups and the curve grows more slowly, which the grey tag and scene 10 cover. (d) Scene 10's "the pink could fade" is true only after many rounds (about ten in a single chain at 0.1 mol/L); "after many rounds" is spoken. (e) The H350 statement is accurate for typical 1% phenolphthalein solutions in Australia; the science check should confirm before voicing, since students may find "possible cancer hazard" alarming; the wording follows the SDS rather than softening it.
