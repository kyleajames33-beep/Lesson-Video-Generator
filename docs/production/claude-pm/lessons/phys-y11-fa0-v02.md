## Decisions

- **Learner question (route placeholder rewritten; root to enter).** The route's "How can I planning scientific investigations in a defensible physics investigation?" becomes **"Why does an experiment's plan decide whether its results can be trusted?"**, the question v01's handoff chip already names. One causal chain: a test that can't separate the effect from its errors can't decide anything (1, 2); controlling every other variable lets one change be the cause (3); repeats, a spread of values and one routine make readings agree (4); the timer must resolve the difference being looked for (5, 6); a control run checks the method itself (7); a plan that leans on someone else's number needs a source worth trusting (8); risks are rated and brought down before the work starts (9); an honest plan records everything and claims only what the evidence shows (10); so the plan, not the luck of the day, decides what the results are worth (11).

- **Main example (paper cones; v01's modified question).** The trolley is not planned here, because fa1-v15 plans and runs it in full (its scenes 3 and 4 teach the trolley's variables and its no-pull control). The paper-cone fall is chosen instead: it is v01's own modified question (continuity; no new physics), every planning decision in it is visible (cone width, equal mass, a timer choice that really matters), and its hazards are mild but real, so the risk assessment can teach likelihood and consequence without alarm. - **The rushed test (hook, scenes 1, 2).** One drop of a 12 cm cone and a 14 cm cone, hand-timed: 0.91 s and 0.84 s (illustrative, tagged), so the wider cone seems faster. The model times are about 0.83 s and 0.92 s (author note b): the stopwatch's reaction-time scatter has swapped them. 3 s prediction: "Is the hypothesis wrong, or the test?" (chips "hypothesis wrong", "the test can't tell", "both"). Answered by reasoning in scene 2: hand-timing error (about 0.1 s or more) is as big as the difference sought (about 0.1 s), and one drop each can't rule out a draught. The hook never says the hypothesis is right; it says this test can't decide (route stop: predictions are not reported as observations).
- **Four plan questions (organiser, scene 2).** "Does it test the right thing? Will the readings agree? Is it safe? Is it honest?" These map to validity (3, 7), reliability (4 to 6, 8), safety (9) and ethics (10), and become the plan board's four sections. The memory aid compresses them (Memory aid check).
- **Variables justified (scene 3; `ci9ce164ec`).** IV: the cone's rim width (it changes the facing area and the steepness; changed by overlapping the slit more or less; Revision 2: learner copy says width "changes its facing area, and its steepness too", so the test is of width for this paper-cone design, not area alone); DV: the fall time from 1.500 m; controlled, each with the same stated reason ("if it changed too, we couldn't tell which change caused the difference", v01's two-differences lesson in one clause): mass (same 20.0 cm paper circle and the same 2 cm strip of tape for every cone), drop height and release (from rest, point down), still air (windows and doors shut, fans off). "Valid" glossed as "tests what it claims to test" (as v15 and v24). v10's meanings of independent, dependent and controlled are recalled in a clause. Author note (c) records that changing the overlap also changes the cone's steepness; this is a real limitation, kept in author notes and the fa0-v03 and fa0-v05 discussion, not learner copy, because omitting it here does not teach a wrong idea about planning.
- **Reliable primary data (scene 4; `ci6c11c587`).** Three repeats per cone (one odd drop can't decide the result); five widths spread evenly from 10 to 18 cm (a pattern, not two points); one routine for every drop (each reading taken the same way). "Reliable: repeat the whole set, and check that its pattern agrees" (Revision 2: the same meaning as fa0-v05, repeating the whole experiment; v10 used whole repeat runs). The three repeats within a set check the scatter (precision, named in fa0-v05). The timer and the method check, the other two parts of how reliable data are obtained, follow in 5 to 7.
- **Materials and technologies (scenes 5, 6; `ci1ba1d4e1`).** A 3 s prediction: "Which timer could measure that?" (chips "stopwatch", "light gate", "phone video"). Stopwatch: hand-timing error about 0.1 s or more, as big as the difference; light gate: times to 0.001 s, but most school gates have a gap only a few centimetres wide, too narrow for an 18 cm cone (author note d; Codex to confirm the typical gap); phone video at 240 frames per second against a metre-rule scale: a frame about every 0.004 s, and every drop can be watched again. Also chosen: a balance reading to 0.01 g to check every cone's mass; the same paper and tape (materials). The rule taught: choose equipment by what the measurement needs, not by what's quickest.
- **Experimental control (scene 7; `ci9ce164ec`).** Plain meaning: "a run where the thing being tested is missing, so we have something to compare with". Here: a steel ball dropped from the same 1.500 m, filmed the same way; the air barely pushes on it, so it should take the free-fall time from s = ½gt², about 0.55 s (v09, v10 recall). If the method gives that, the timing works, and any extra time a cone takes comes from the air. This matches v15's control (with no pull, no acceleration) in kind. Root and the physics check to confirm the framing (open decision 3).
- **Secondary data when planning (scene 8; `cid3f0dfde`).** The plan relies on the reaction-time value used in scenes 2 and 6. Three invented source cards, tagged "example sources, invented for this video": (A) an advertisement for a reaction-training app, "most people react in 0.05 s", no method; (B) a university physics lab guide that measured students' reaction times with a ruler-drop test, shows its method and data, about 0.2 s; (C) a science education agency page, about 0.2 to 0.25 s. Four checks, each a question: who made it, and why? does it show how it was measured? does it agree with other sources? is it current? A fails the first three. Plain meaning: "reliable source: one whose information you have good reason to trust". The real value (typical simple visual reaction time about 0.2 to 0.25 s) is in author note (e) for Codex to confirm.
- **Risk assessment (scene 9; `cif90164e3`).** Plain meanings: hazard (something that could cause harm); risk (how likely the harm is, combined with how bad it would be); control (a step that lowers the risk). An example 3 by 3 matrix (likelihood: unlikely, possible, likely; consequence: minor, moderate, major; levels low, medium, high), tagged "example matrix: use your school's". Two spoken rows: (1) a higher drop that needs climbing on a chair: possible and major, so high; the best control removes the hazard, so the plan sets the drop at 1.500 m, reachable from the floor (status "removed"); (2) the tripod's legs in a walkway: possible and moderate, so medium; set up out of the walkway, a trip becomes unlikely, so low. A third row is on screen only: the steel ball in the control run (possible, minor, low; sand tray, feet clear), matching v10. Footer: "your teacher approves the plan; follow your school's risk assessment". No new safety rule is invented; the controls are those already used in v10 and v15 or general classroom practice (Safety facts check).
- **Ethics (scene 10; `ci5ca4a539`).** Development of knowledge: record every drop, including odd ones, with a note, and never quietly delete a result that doesn't fit; if the plan had used classmates' reaction times as primary data, that is data about people, so they would need to agree, be free to say no, and have their names kept off the results. Use of knowledge: the advertisement used a science-sounding number to sell a product; using science honestly means claiming only what the evidence shows. No law or policy is cited; the framing is general research ethics as schools present it (open decision 4).

A2: a hanging mass of about 0.1 to 0.5 kg falling about 0.8 m from bench height can bruise toes; "possible, moderate, medium" is a reasonable rating, and other well-argued ratings with a matching control earn the marks (marking note).

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 12; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A student needs to measure the time a trolley takes to roll 0.50 m along a track. The time is about 0.6 s. The options are a hand-held stopwatch that displays time to 0.01 s, or two light gates connected to a data logger that times to 0.001 s. A typical human reaction time is about 0.2 s. Choose the more appropriate equipment and justify your choice."
Model answer: The light gates (1 mark). Hand timing depends on reaction time at the start and the stop, which varies from try to try, so the measured time can be out by about 0.1 s or more, which is a large fraction (about 17 per cent or more) of 0.6 s (1 mark). Light gates start and stop automatically when the trolley breaks the beams, so there is no reaction time and the time is resolved to 0.001 s (1 mark). Classic mistake (crossed): "the stopwatch, because it reads to 0.01 s" (the display's resolution is not the limit; the reaction is).

**Attempt 2 (scene 14; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested).** "In a trolley investigation, a string runs over a pulley at the edge of the bench to a hanging mass of 0.20 kg. When the trolley reaches the end of the track, the hanging mass can fall about 0.8 m to the floor. (a) Use the risk matrix to rate the risk of this hazard. (b) Describe one control, and explain how it lowers the risk." (The example matrix is printed on the card.)
Model answer: (a) A falling mass landing on a foot is possible, and a bruised or injured foot is a moderate consequence, so the risk is medium (1 mark; a different reasoned rating that uses the matrix correctly is accepted). (b) Place a sand tray under the hanging mass and keep feet clear of the drop (1 mark): an injury becomes unlikely, so the likelihood falls and the risk becomes low (1 mark). Also accepted: shorten the drop so the mass lands gently (lowers the consequence); fit an end stop so the trolley stops before the mass reaches the floor. Classic mistake (crossed): "be careful" (not a control: it changes neither likelihood nor consequence).
