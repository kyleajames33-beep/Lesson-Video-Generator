## Decisions

- **Learner question (route placeholder rewritten; root to enter).** The route's "How can I planning scientific investigations in a defensible physics investigation?" becomes **"Why does an experiment's plan decide whether its results can be trusted?"**, the question v01's handoff chip already names. One causal chain: a test that can't separate the effect from its errors can't decide anything (1, 2); controlling every other variable lets one change be the cause (3); repeats, a spread of values and one routine make readings agree (4); the timer must resolve the difference being looked for (5, 6); a control run checks the method itself (7); a plan that leans on someone else's number needs a source worth trusting (8); risks are rated and brought down before the work starts (9); an honest plan records everything and claims only what the evidence shows (10); so the plan, not the luck of the day, decides what the results are worth (11).

The three repeats within a set check the scatter (precision, named in fa0-v05). The timer and the method check, the other two parts of how reliable data are obtained, follow in 5 to 7.
- **Materials and technologies (scenes 5, 6; `ci1ba1d4e1`).** A 3 s prediction: "Which timer could measure that?" (chips "stopwatch", "light gate", "phone video"). Stopwatch: hand-timing error about 0.1 s or more, as big as the difference; light gate: times to 0.001 s, but most school gates have a gap only a few centimetres wide, too narrow for an 18 cm cone (author note d; Codex to confirm the typical gap); phone video at 240 frames per second against a metre-rule scale: a frame about every 0.004 s, and every drop can be watched again. Also chosen: a balance reading to 0.01 g to check every cone's mass; the same paper and tape (materials). The rule taught: choose equipment by what the measurement needs, not by what's quickest.
- **Experimental control (scene 7; `ci9ce164ec`).** Plain meaning: "a run where the thing being tested is missing, so we have something to compare with". Here: a steel ball dropped from the same 1.500 m, filmed the same way; the air barely pushes on it, so it should take the free-fall time from s = ½gt², about 0.55 s (v09, v10 recall). If the method gives that, the timing works, and any extra time a cone takes comes from the air. This matches v15's control (with no pull, no acceleration) in kind. Root and the physics check to confirm the framing (open decision 3).

### 1. hook (is the hypothesis wrong, or the test?)
> The trolley hypothesis is ready, and the trolley investigation will test it. But a hypothesis isn't a plan, and the plan decides whether anyone can trust the results. So let's plan last video's paper question. At a fixed mass, how does the area facing the air change the fall time? Here's a rushed test. Two paper cones, same mass, one wider, each timed once with a stopwatch. The wide one takes nought point eight four seconds, and the narrow one nought point nine one. Is the hypothesis wrong, or is the **test**?

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Valid: the test measures what it claims. Change one independent variable, measure one dependent variable, and control every other variable that could change the result, because otherwise you can't tell which change caused it.
