### 1. hook (the more acidic solution, the weaker acid?)
> Two acids, two pH readings. An unknown acid, H X, at zero point zero five zero zero moles per litre, reads **two point seven eight**. Ethanoic acid at zero point five zero zero moles per litre reads **two point five two**, so it's more acidic. Does that make ethanoic acid the **stronger** acid? Not necessarily. Its solution is **ten times more concentrated**. To compare strength fairly, you need a number that doesn't depend on concentration.

### 2. Ka is that number
> You've met that number in Module 5: **K a**, the equilibrium constant for the acid ionising, hydronium times the anion, over the whole acid. K a doesn't change with concentration, and the bigger it is, the further the acid ionises at the **same concentration**. For ethanoic acid, K a is **one point eight times ten to the minus five**. But we don't know K a for H X. We can find it **from the pH**, by running the ICE table backwards.

### 3. worked example: the hydronium, and strong or weak

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Ka measures an acid's tendency to ionise; its value does not depend on concentration (at a given temperature). pH depends on Ka and c, so compare acid strength with Ka or pKa.
2. Ka from pH: [H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ = [A⁻]; [HA] = c − [H₃O⁺]; Ka = [H₃O⁺]² / (c − [H₃O⁺]). Example: 0.0500 mol/L HX, pH 2.78: Ka = 5.7 × 10⁻⁵.
3. pKa = −log Ka and Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ. Lower pKa = bigger Ka = stronger acid; one pKa unit is a factor of 10 in Ka.
4. Strong acid: ionises completely, [H₃O⁺] = c, no measurable Ka in water. Weak acid: [H₃O⁺] ≪ c, Ka much less than 1, pKa positive.
5. Conjugate pair: Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (25 °C). The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
