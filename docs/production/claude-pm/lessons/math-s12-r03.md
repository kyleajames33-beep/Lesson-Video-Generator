### 2. mark and release
> The method is called capture-recapture. Catch some fish, mark them, let them go, then catch again later. Say the club nets forty fish, puts a small tag on each one, and lets them all go. Then it waits a week, so the tagged fish can swim off and mix through the whole **lake**. Now the lake holds forty tagged fish, and an unknown number that aren't **tagged**.

### 3. the second catch
> A week later, the club nets fifty fish, and eight of them have tags. Here's the key idea. If the tagged fish have mixed in evenly, the fraction tagged in the net should match the fraction tagged in the whole lake. Mixed evenly means every part of the lake has the same share of tagged fish. So a net full of fish is like a small **copy** of the lake. In the net, eight out of fifty are tagged. That's eight fiftieths, or sixteen per **cent**. So about sixteen per cent of the whole lake should be tagged **too**.

### 4. finding the population
> And we know how many tagged fish are in the lake, forty. So forty is about sixteen per cent of all the fish. Think of it as equal fractions. Eight out of fifty equals forty out of how many? Eight times five is forty, so fifty times five is two hundred and **fifty**. That's about two hundred and fifty fish in the lake. As a rule, multiply the number tagged by the number in the second catch, then divide by the number tagged in that catch. Forty times fifty, divided by eight, is two hundred and **fifty**. Some books write it in **letters**. N is about n one times n two, over m two, where N is the population, n one the number tagged, n two the second catch, and m two the tagged ones in it. Check, forty out of two hundred and fifty is sixteen per cent. It's an estimate, so we say about two hundred and fifty **fish**.

### 6. when the estimate goes wrong
> But there's a catch. The method only works if some things are true. The tagged fish must mix back in evenly. Being tagged mustn't make a fish easier or harder to catch. No fish should be born, die, arrive or leave between the two catches. And the tags must stay **on**. Watch what happens if one of these fails. Say the tagged fish hang around the jetty, right where the club sets its net. Now the net catches sixteen tagged fish instead of **eight**. Forty times fifty, divided by sixteen, is only a hundred and twenty-five. Too many tagged fish in the net makes the number we divide by too big, so the estimate comes out too **small**.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Capture-recapture: tag some, release, catch again later.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 9; original, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A fishing club catches 75 bass, tags them and releases them. Two weeks later it catches 120 bass, and 18 of them are tagged. (a) Estimate the number of bass in the lake. (b) Tagged bass may stay near the boat ramp, where most fishing happens. If so, would the estimate be too high or too low? Explain."
Model answer: (a) 75 × 120 ÷ 18 = 500, so about 500 bass. (b) Too low: tagged bass are caught more than their true share, so 18 is too big; it is the number we divide by, so the estimate is too small. Classic mistake (crossed): "too high".

**Attempt 2 (scene 11; site SA 1, 2 marks suggested).** "A ranger tags 45 koalas in a reserve. A month later, 60 koalas are surveyed and 9 of them carry a tag. (a) Estimate the number of koalas in the reserve. (b) State one assumption the estimate relies on."
Model answer: (a) 45 × 60 ÷ 9 = 300, so about 300 koalas. (b) Any one, applied: no koalas are born, die, arrive or leave during the month; tagged koalas are as easy to find as untagged ones; tags stay on; tagged koalas mix through the reserve. Classic mistake (crossed): "the sample is random" (not applied to this survey).
