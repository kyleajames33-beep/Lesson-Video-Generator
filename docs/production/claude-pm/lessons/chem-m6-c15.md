**Boundary:** this video starts from the route's insoluble sample and unsuitable direct endpoint and stops at excess-reagent subtraction and the conductivity intersection. It teaches: why a direct titration of calcium carbonate fails (it barely dissolves, reacts only at the solid's surface, so the indicator changes before the solid has reacted, and dissolved carbon dioxide blurs the endpoint); the back titration (a measured excess of standard acid, complete reaction, boiling off carbon dioxide, titrating the leftover acid with standard base); why the carbon dioxide must go (it would use sodium hydroxide, so the leftover acid looks bigger and the carbonate too small); the four-step calculation (acid added, acid left over, acid used by subtraction of moles, the mole ratio), then mass and percentage by mass; the conductometric titration as a method for samples whose colour or cloudiness hides an indicator; why the readings form two nearly straight stretches (each equal portion of titrant makes the same ion change before equivalence, and adds the same excess after it); reading equivalence where the two best-fit lines cross, rather than at one measured point; why dilution by the titrant bends the lines and how it is kept small (a large sample volume made up with water, a concentrated titrant, or readings corrected for the volume added); a concentration from the crossing volume. **Not here:** why the strong acid conductivity falls to a V and the weak acid rises with a bend (chem-m6-c14 scenes 8 and 9; ### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Back titration: used for insoluble or slow-reacting samples, such as CaCO₃ in antacid tablets, eggshell or limestone. Add a measured excess of standard acid, let it react completely, boil off the CO₂, then titrate the leftover acid with standard base.
2. n(acid used) = n(acid added) − n(acid left over). Subtract moles, not volumes. Then use the mole ratio from the balanced equation (CaCO₃ : HCl = 1 : 2).
3. Conductometric titration: measure the conductivity after each equal portion of titrant; useful when colour or cloudiness hides an indicator. Equivalence is where the two straight best-fit lines cross (the bottom of the V for a strong acid, the bend for a weak acid).
4. Each portion of titrant dilutes the flask and bends the lines: use a large sample volume and a concentrated titrant, or correct each reading for the volume added.
