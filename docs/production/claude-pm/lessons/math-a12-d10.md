## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "Which valid candidate gives the global maximum or minimum?" The answer, built in order: a local maximum is only the highest nearby, so it need not be the largest value on the interval (hook); a global maximum is a point where f(x) ≤ f(a) for every x in the domain, so no part of the graph is higher (2); inside the interval, where the curve is smooth, the highest point must have a flat tangent, but at an end or a corner it need not (3); so the candidates are the flats inside, the ends and every interior point where f′ doesn't exist (such as a corner), and comparing the values of f at all of them gives the answer, with no classification needed (4).

### 2. global maximum and minimum
> The largest value over the whole domain is called the global maximum. A point a, f of a, is a global maximum if f of x is less than or equal to f of a, for every x in the domain. Picture a horizontal line at that height. For a global maximum, no part of the graph pokes above **it**. Put the line at two, the local maximum, and the curve pokes through near the right-hand **end**. Put it at eighteen, and the whole graph sits on or below it. A global minimum is the same idea with a floor. No part of the graph dips **below** it.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a local maximum need not be global: it is compared only with nearby points, and the curve climbs higher near the end (1, 2). Why interior extremes on a smooth curve are stationary: a sloped tangent means a step uphill finds a higher point (3). Why endpoints and corners are candidates: the uphill step leaves the domain, or there is no single tangent (3). Why comparing values is enough: the extremes exist on a closed interval with an unbroken curve and must be among the candidates, so the largest candidate value is the largest value (3, 4).
