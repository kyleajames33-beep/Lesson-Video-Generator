## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "How does a split-ring motor keep turning?" is causal. The answer, built in order: two opposite forces on the coil's sides make a torque (3, 4); that torque falls to zero face on and would reverse after it (5, 7); the split ring swaps the coil's connections at that moment, reversing the current, so the forces swap and the torque keeps turning the same way (8); the coil's inertia carries it through the zero (8).
- **Hook (site lesson 09 Analyse step 5 and lesson 10 "hidden problem"; original staging).** v15's motor, then the same coil wired straight to the supply (no split ring; coded leads straight to the coil's ends, which can only swing so far). 3 s prediction: "keeps spinning", "rocks then stops", "doesn't move". The coil swings, overshoots, rocks back and settles face on to the poles. Back on the split ring, it spins. This makes the split ring's job the question.
- **Parts and functions (syllabus "the functions of its components"; site lesson 10 "Field and coil", "Contacts and switching").** Named on the new painted demonstration motor plate with pen arrows: field magnets (provide B), coil or armature (carries the current that feels the forces), axle (lets it turn), split-ring commutator (two half rings that turn with the coil), brushes (fixed sliding contacts that carry current to the turning ring). "Armature" given as the other name once.
- **Forces on the sides (v07 recalled).** End-on coded view: the two long sides cross the field at right angles; their currents run opposite ways (one out of the screen, one in), so their forces are opposite (amber, up and down). The short end sides: their forces act along the axle and give no turning effect (site Analyse step 2). Direction from v07's coded axes, "thumb I, fingers B, palm F" named in words only (root decision 3).
- **Torque derivation (route "nIAB sin theta with the area-normal convention"; Module 5 v09 and v09 recalled).** Each long side is r = w/2 from the axle and feels F = nIlB (n turns, v07's F = lIB with n wires). The angle between r and F equals θ, the angle between the area normal and B (shown by rotating both arrows together; author note a). τ = 2 × (w/2) × nIlB × sin θ = nI(lw)B sin θ = nIAB sin θ. On screen also "= nIA⊥B, A⊥ = A sin θ" so the data-sheet form is recognised (author note b).
- **Edge on and face on (new contrast with v09's aid).** θ = 90° (normal across the field, the coil's plane along the field, "edge on"): torque maximum, nIAB. θ = 0° (normal along the field, "face on"): the forces pull along the coil's plane through the axle, torque zero: the dead point. Spoken contrast: in video nine, face on gave full flux; here face on gives no torque. Site misconception: "Maximum flux corresponds to zero torque".
- **Angle convention (site lesson 09 uses the plane angle and cos; open decision 1).** The video uses the syllabus and data-sheet form, θ from the area normal. One sentence in scene 6 warns that some books measure the angle from the coil's plane and use cos, so "always check which angle". The site's worked example (θ = 30° between the plane and B) is presented as "the plane is at thirty degrees to the field, so the normal is at sixty", giving the site's 3.1 × 10⁻² N m.
- **Worked example (site lesson 09, unchanged values).** 50 turns, 4.0 cm × 3.0 cm, 3.0 A, 0.20 T: A = 1.2 × 10⁻³ m², τ_max = 3.6 × 10⁻² N m; plane at 30° to B: θ = 60°, τ = 3.6 × 10⁻² × sin 60° = 3.1 × 10⁻² N m. Two stages, area conversion in the givens.
- **The problem and the fix (route "explain commutator reversal and dead points"; site lesson 10 DC structure; lesson 09 radial field card).** Without a commutator, past the dead point the same current gives the same force directions, but the sides have swapped over, so the torque turns the coil back: it rocks and settles (author note c, friction). With the split ring, each half ring is joined to one end of the coil and turns with it; the brushes stay put; at the dead point the brushes cross the gaps and swap halves, so the current in the coil reverses, the forces reverse, and the torque keeps its sense. A τ against angle graph changes from a sine wave (rock) to a train of positive humps (spin). The coil's inertia carries it across the dead point, while the brushes briefly touch the gaps (no current) (author note d).
- **Smoothing (site HSC tip: radial field and commutator; lesson 10 "multiple coils").** One scene: a coil stopped exactly at the dead point will not start by itself, and the torque pulses; real motors use several coils at different angles (with more commutator segments), and curved pole pieces with an iron core make a radial field that keeps the sides edge on to the field, so the torque stays near maximum. Named and shown, not derived (site lesson 09 `analyse-radial-field` is outside the route sections; open decision 2).
- **Memory aid (scene 10): "Every half turn, flip the current."** See "Memory aid check". The edge on and face on contrast is the scene 5 tag, "Face on, no torque. Edge on, full torque.", and also appears in the key notes as a second line, not as a second aid.

- **Author notes (not learner copy):**

### 2. the parts
> Let's name the parts. The magnets provide the magnetic field. The coil, also called the armature, carries the current, and it's free to turn on an **axle**. At one end of the axle is the split-ring commutator. It's a ring cut into two halves, and each half is joined to one end of the coil, so the halves turn with the **coil**. Pressing on the ring are two brushes. They stay still, and they carry current from the supply into whichever half they're **touching**.

Visual: the painted plate `y12p-dc-motor-model-v1.png` fills the stage. On "The magnets provide the magnetic field" a pen arrow and label "field magnets: provide B" with a few slate field lines between the poles. On "axle" pen arrows and labels "coil (armature): carries the current" and "axle". On "touching" two grey brushes press on opposite sides of the ring, label "brushes: fixed contacts", orange current arrows from the pack's red lead through one brush into one half ring.

### 3. two forces on the coil
> Now look at the coil end on, along the axle. Its two long sides cross the magnetic field at right angles. Current comes out of the screen in one side, and goes into the screen in the other. From video seven, each side feels a force at right angles to both the current and the **field**. And because the currents run opposite ways, the forces point opposite ways, one up, one **down**. Two equal, opposite forces, a distance apart. That pair turns the **coil**. The short end sides don't help, because their forces just push along the **axle**.

Visual: camera turns to the end-on coil (coded): the axle a small circle at the centre, the coil a line through it at θ = 90° (edge on), its left side a dot (orange "current out of the screen"), its right side a cross ("into the screen"), slate field lines left to right ("B"). ### 4. how big is the torque?
> How strong is that turning effect? From Module five, torque is r F sine theta. Each long side is half the coil's width from the axle, and with n turns, its force is n I l B. Here's the neat part. The angle that matters turns out to be the angle between the field, and the coil's area normal, the arrow at right angles to its face, from video **nine**. Add the two sides, and the width times the length is the area. So the torque is n I A B sine **theta**. That's the formula on your data **sheet**.

Visual: on "torque is r F sine theta" a chip "τ = rF sin θ (Module 5)". On "half the coil's width" ink dashed lever arms "r = w/2" from the axle to each side. On "n I l B" the force labels pulse. On "nine" the coil rotates to about 50° and the navy area normal appears at the axle, at right angles to the coil; an angle arc "θ" between the normal and B; as the coil turns, the angle between r and F (shown by a faint second arc) stays equal to θ (author note a). On "theta" `AlignedAlgebra`: "τ = 2 × (w/2) × nIlB × sin θ = nI(lw)B sin θ = nIAB sin θ" with "lw = A" boxed. On "sheet" the strip shows the data-sheet form "τ = nIA⊥B = nIAB sin θ" with a small tag "A⊥ = A sin θ; θ: area normal to B".

### 5. edge on and face on
> So where is the torque biggest? When the coil is edge on to the field, its normal is at ninety degrees to the field, sine theta is one, and the torque is the maximum, n I A **B**. As it turns, the torque gets smaller. When the coil is face on to the field, the normal points along the field, theta is zero, and the two forces pull straight through the axle. The torque is **zero**. This is called the dead **point**. Notice the contrast with video nine. Face on gave full flux, but here face on gives no **torque**.

Visual: the end-on coil turns slowly from edge on (θ = 90°) to face on (θ = 0°) under a cursor; to the right a `DataGraph` "τ against θ" draws live. On "B" at θ = 90° the forces' lever arms are longest; graph point "τ_max = nIAB". On "As it turns" the lever arms shrink as the forces stay vertical. On "zero" at θ = 0° both force arrows lie along the coil line through the axle; graph point "τ = 0". On "point" label "dead point (face on)". On "torque" a two-row chip: "face on: full flux (video 9), zero torque"; "edge on: zero flux, full torque".

### 6. worked example
> Let's do one. A coil has fifty turns, each four point zero centimetres by three point zero centimetres. It carries three point zero amps in a field of zero point two zero tesla. The area is one point two times ten to the minus three square metres. Multiply n, I, A and B, and the maximum torque is three point six times ten to the minus two newton **metres**. Now the coil's plane is at thirty degrees to the field. Careful, that's the plane, so the normal is at sixty **degrees**. The torque is three point six times ten to the minus two, times sine sixty, three point one times ten to the minus two newton **metres**. Some books measure the angle from the plane and use cos. Always check which angle you're **given**.

### 7. why it rocks without a split ring
> Now back to the hook. Without a split ring, the current in each side never changes direction. The coil turns towards face on, and its inertia carries it past the dead point. But now the sides have swapped places. The side on the left is now on the right, still with the same current and the same force. So the torque now turns the coil **back**. It swings back and forth, and friction stops it face on. That's the **rocking**.

### 8. what the split ring does
> The split ring fixes this. Watch the brushes. As the coil reaches the dead point, the gaps in the ring pass the brushes, and each brush moves onto the other half of the **ring**. That swaps the connections, so the current in the coil **reverses**. Now the side on the left has the force upwards again, and the torque keeps turning the coil the same **way**. For a moment, right at the dead point, no current flows. But the coil is already turning, so it keeps going through that point. Every half turn, the same thing happens, so the coil keeps **turning**.

### 9. smoother motors
> A simple motor like this still has a weak spot. Its torque pulses, and if it stops exactly at the dead point, it won't start by itself. Real motors use several coils at different angles, so one coil is always near its best position. And curved magnets around an iron core keep the coil's sides edge on to the field almost all the way round. That's called a radial field, and it keeps the torque close to its **maximum**.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Parts: magnets (field B); coil or armature (carries I, feels the forces); axle; split-ring commutator (two half rings joined to the coil's ends, turn with it); brushes (fixed contacts to the supply).
2. The two long sides carry opposite currents, so they feel opposite forces (F = nIlB): a pair that makes a torque. End sides: no turning effect.
3. τ = nIA⊥B = nIAB sin θ, θ: angle between the area normal and B. Edge on (θ = 90°): maximum. Face on (θ = 0°): zero, the dead point. Face on: full flux, no torque.
4. Example: 50 turns, 1.2 × 10⁻³ m², 3.0 A, 0.20 T: τ_max = 3.6 × 10⁻² N m; plane at 30° (θ = 60°): 3.1 × 10⁻² N m. Check which angle you are given.

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 11; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "A rectangular coil of 120 turns measures 4.0 cm × 2.5 cm. **Attempt 2 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 3 marks).** "Explain the function of the split-ring commutator in a simple DC motor."
Model answer: The forces on the two long sides of the coil produce a torque. Without a commutator, once the coil turns past the face-on (dead point) position the torque reverses and turns the coil back, so it would rock and stop. The split ring is joined to the ends of the coil and turns with it; every half turn, at the dead point, the halves swap brushes, reversing the current in the coil. The forces on the sides reverse, so the torque keeps acting in the same rotational direction and the coil rotates continuously (its inertia carries it through the dead point). Classic mistake (crossed): "it changes AC into DC".
