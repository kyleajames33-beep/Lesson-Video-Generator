### 2. the chance line
> Probabilities live on a line from zero to one. Zero means impossible, it can't happen. Pull a green marble from a bag of red, blue and yellow ones, and the chance is **zero**. One means certain, it must happen. Roll a normal die, and it's certain to show a number from one to six, so that's **one**. Right in the middle is a half, even chance, like a coin landing heads. And the rain forecast, zero point two, sits closer to zero than to a half, so rain is unlikely, but it could still **happen**. A probability can never be below zero, or above one.

### 3. one probability, three ways
> A probability can be written three ways. Twenty per cent means twenty out of a hundred, which simplifies to one fifth. And one fifth as a decimal is one divided by five, zero point **two**. So one fifth, zero point two and twenty per cent are the same probability. Here's another. A quarter is one divided by four, zero point two five. Times a hundred, that's twenty-five per **cent**. To go from a fraction to a decimal, divide. From a decimal to a percentage, multiply by a **hundred**.

### 4. converting: an exam-style example
> Here's an exam-style question. Write three eighths as a decimal and as a percentage, then write seventy-five per cent as a fraction. Three eighths means three divided by eight, which is zero point three seven **five**. Times a hundred moves the decimal point two places, so that's thirty-seven point five per **cent**. Seventy-five per cent is seventy-five over a hundred, and both divide by twenty-five, so it's three **quarters**. Moving the point only one place, and writing three point seven five per cent, is the slip. A percentage is times a **hundred**.

### 5. experiments, trials and the sample space
> To find a probability, we first need every possible result. Rolling a die is a chance experiment, anything with an uncertain result. One roll is a trial, one go of the experiment. Each possible result is an outcome. And the set of every possible outcome is called the sample space, written S. For a die, it's one, two, three, four, five and six, written inside curly **brackets**. The number of outcomes in it is written n of S, so here, n of S is **six**.

### 6. listing in order: two coins
> With two coins, it's easy to miss an outcome. So list them in order. Call them coin one and coin two. Coin one heads, coin two heads. Heads, tails. Tails, heads. Tails, tails. That's **four**. Notice heads then tails, and tails then heads, are different outcomes, because the coins are different. A tidy order means nothing is missed, and nothing is counted **twice**.

### 8. too many to list: multiply
> Sometimes there are too many outcomes to list. Roll a die and toss a coin. Each of the six faces can go with heads or with tails, so that's six lots of two, **twelve**. When one step has six choices and the next has two, multiply. Now a phone's four-digit PIN. Each digit can be any of ten, zero to nine. So it's ten times ten times ten times ten, ten **thousand**. Nobody wants to list ten thousand codes. Multiplying the choices at each step counts them **all**.
