## Decisions

- **Anchor: the record sheet (d04's frame).** Five columns, five classifications. The sheet's columns peel off as cards and fall through the type tree, so every term lands on a variable students have already met in the investigation.
- **The arithmetic test first (site key tip).** "Does arithmetic on it make sense?" separates numerical from categorical before any other word is used. "Average of the postcodes" is the visual surprise.
- **Plain meanings at first use.** Variable ("something that can change from one person or thing to the next"); numerical ("the answers are numbers you count or measure, and arithmetic on them makes sense"); categorical ("the answers are groups or labels"); discrete ("counted: separate values with gaps between, often whole numbers"); continuous ("measured: any value in a range, as precise as your measuring allows"); nominal ("categories with no natural order"); ordinal ("categories with a natural order").
- **Continuous even when rounded.** Sleep is written to the nearest half hour, which looks like separate steps, but the time itself can be any value. A zoom ruler shows 7.5 h opening up into 7 h 32 min 10 s. Rounding changes how it's recorded, not what kind of variable it is.
- **"Depending on responses": two switchboard examples.** The sleep question asked with d01's buttons (ordinal categorical) or with a number box (continuous numerical); screens counted (0, 1, 2, 3: discrete numerical) or ticked as "none, one, two or more" (ordinal categorical). How the answers are collected decides the type of the data collected.
- **The type tree (recurring model).** Two questions: "Does arithmetic make sense?" Yes: numerical, then "counted or measured?" (discrete, continuous). No: categorical, then "Is there a natural order?" (ordinal, nominal). Reused in d07 when choosing graphs.

### 1. hook: which columns can we average?
> Here's our record sheet. Every column is a variable, something that changes from one student to the next. Hours of sleep, screens in the bedroom, where the phone charges, how rested they feel, and how they get to school. Try averaging each column. Sleep, fine. Screens, fine. But what's the average of bedroom and kitchen? It makes no sense. Before we can display or analyse any of this, we need to know what type of data each column holds. So is this data numerical or categorical, discrete or continuous, nominal or **ordinal**?

### 2. numerical or categorical: the arithmetic test
> The first question sorts everything. Does arithmetic on the answers make sense? If the answers are numbers you count or measure, and adding or averaging them means something, the variable is numerical. Hours of sleep and screens in the bedroom are numerical. If the answers are groups or labels, the variable is categorical. Where the phone charges, how rested you feel, and how you get to school are all categorical. You can count how many students said bedroom, but you can't add bedroom to **kitchen**. Those counts are numbers, but the answers themselves aren't. That's the arithmetic test.

### 3. numerical: discrete or continuous
> Numerical variables split again. Is the number counted, or measured? Screens in the bedroom is counted. You can have two screens or three, but not two and a half, so the values are separate, with gaps between them. That's discrete. Hours of sleep is measured. Someone might sleep seven hours thirty-two minutes and ten seconds. Any value in a range is possible, limited only by how precisely you measure it. That's continuous. Time, distance, mass and temperature are all measured, so they're all continuous. Here's the trap. We wrote sleep to the nearest half hour, so the answers look like separate steps. But the sleep itself didn't come in steps. Rounding changes how a value is written, not what kind of variable it is. Sleep is still **continuous**.

Visual: the tree's blue branch splits at a node "counted or measured?" into "counted: discrete" and "measured: continuous". On "Hours of sleep is measured" the sleep card drops into "continuous". ### 4. categorical: nominal or ordinal
> Categorical variables split too. Is there a natural order? How rested do you feel has answers not at all, a little, somewhat, quite, and very. They go in order, so the variable is ordinal. Where the phone charges, bedroom, kitchen, lounge or other, has no natural order. You could list them any way. That's nominal, which just means names. Getting to school, by bus or nearby, is nominal as well. The order matters for what you can say. With ordinal data, you can say one student is more rested than another. With nominal data, you can only say they're different, or count how many fall in each **category**.

Visual: the tree's violet branch splits at "Is there a natural order?" into "yes: ordinal" and "no: nominal". On "not at all, a little, somewhat, quite, and very" the five labels line up as an ordered strip with an arrow "more rested →"; the rested card drops into "ordinal" (hand tag "order?"). On "bedroom, kitchen, lounge or other" four label chips shuffle into different orders twice, unchanged in meaning (hand tag "no order"); the phone card drops into "nominal". On "by bus or nearby" the travel card drops into "nominal". On "more rested" the ordered strip glows; on "category" (bold) olive tallies "bedroom 12 | kitchen 5 | lounge 2 | other 1" sit under the nominal bin.

### 5. numbers that are labels, and questions that decide
> Two traps live at the edges. First, some numbers are really labels. A postcode like twenty-seven fifty is written in digits, but the average of two postcodes is nonsense. Jersey numbers are the same. They're categorical, nominal. Second, the same question can give either type, depending on how the answers are collected. Remember the sleep question's buttons? Under seven, seven to under eight, and so on. Collected that way, the answers are ordered groups, so the data are ordinal categorical. Ask people to type a number of hours instead, and the data are numerical and continuous. Screens work the same way. Count them, and you get discrete numerical data. Offer none, one, or two or more, and you get ordinal categories. How you ask decides the type of data you **get**.
