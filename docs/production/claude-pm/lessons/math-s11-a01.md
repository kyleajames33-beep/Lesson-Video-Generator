### 2. the length ladder
> Every metric unit is a base unit with a prefix in front. The prefix tells you its size. Milli means one thousandth, so a millimetre is a thousandth of a metre. Centi means one hundredth, so a centimetre is a hundredth of a metre. And kilo means a thousand, not a hundred, so a kilometre is a thousand **metres**. Put them in a ladder, with the biggest at the top. Ten millimetres make a centimetre, a hundred centimetres make a metre, and a thousand metres make a **kilometre**. It helps to picture them. A bank card is just under a millimetre thick. A triple-A battery is about a centimetre wide. A metre is a bit taller than a kitchen bench, and a kilometre is two and a half laps of a running **track**. Watch one metre change its name. It's a hundred centimetres, a thousand millimetres, or nought point nought nought one of a **kilometre**.

### 3. which way: multiply or divide?
> Back to the ribbon, one point eight five metres. To centimetres, you're going down the ladder to a smaller unit. Smaller units fit into the ribbon more times, so the number gets bigger, and you multiply. Times a hundred moves the decimal point two places to the right, so one point eight five times a hundred is a hundred and eighty-five **centimetres**. Times a thousand gives eighteen hundred and fifty **millimetres**. Now go back the other way. Eighteen fifty millimetres to metres is going up the ladder to a bigger unit. Bigger units fit fewer times, so you divide by a thousand, and get one point eight five **metres**. If you multiply by mistake, you'd say the ribbon is one million eight hundred and fifty thousand metres, which is about twice the drive from Sydney to **Brisbane**. Always ask, does my answer make **sense**?

### 4. units must match
> Converting matters most when you calculate. A fun run is five kilometres, on a four hundred metre track. How many laps is that? Five divided by four hundred gives nought point nought one two five laps, and that's clearly **wrong**. The five is in kilometres and the four hundred is in metres, so you're dividing apples by oranges. Match the units first. Five kilometres is five thousand metres. Five thousand divided by four hundred is twelve and a half **laps**. Before any calculation, check that every measurement is in the same **unit**.

### 5. mass: grams, kilograms and tonnes
> Mass, how heavy something is, uses grams, with the same prefixes. A milligram is a thousandth of a gram, a kilogram is a thousand grams, and a tonne is a thousand kilograms. So every step in the mass ladder is a **thousand**. A bag of mandarins on the scale reads one point three five kilograms. In grams, multiply by a thousand, thirteen hundred and fifty **grams**. A muesli bar has ninety-five milligrams of sodium. In grams, divide by a thousand, nought point nought nine five **grams**. And a ute carries forty-five bags of sand at twenty kilograms each. That's nine hundred kilograms, or nought point nine of a **tonne**. Mixed units need matching before you add them, too. Three parcels weigh four hundred and fifty grams, one point two kilograms and eight hundred grams. In kilograms, that's nought point four five, plus one point two, plus nought point eight, which is two point four five **kilograms**.

### 6. capacity: millilitres to megalitres

A thousand millilitres make a litre, a thousand litres make a kilolitre, and a thousand kilolitres make a megalitre. Mega means a million, so a megalitre is a million **litres**. This jug holds one point five litres, which is fifteen hundred **millilitres**. A rainwater tank holds five thousand litres, which is five **kilolitres**. Now watch the tank fill, counted in millilitres. Five million millilitres, the same water as five **kilolitres**. And farm dams are measured in megalitres. Twelve megalitres is twelve thousand kilolitres, or twelve million **litres**. That's as much water as twenty-four hundred of those **tanks**.
