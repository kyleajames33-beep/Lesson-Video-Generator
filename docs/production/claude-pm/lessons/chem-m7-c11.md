**Boundary:** this video starts by identifying the amine and amide functional groups, and stops at comparing amines, amides and carboxylic acids for basicity, intermolecular forces and reactions. It teaches: an amine is ammonia with one or more hydrogens replaced by carbon groups, and its nitrogen keeps a lone pair (a pair of electrons not used in bonding); classification by the number of carbon groups bonded to the nitrogen (primary 1, secondary 2, tertiary 3), not the alcohol rule (propan-2-amine is a primary amine); naming primary amines ("alkan-x-amine": ethanamine, propan-1-amine, propan-2-amine) and secondary and tertiary amines with N- prefixes (N-methylethanamine, N,N-dimethylethanamine); an amide is a C=O bonded straight to a nitrogen (–CONH₂), like an ester with the single-bonded oxygen replaced by nitrogen, formed by condensation of a carboxylic acid with an amine (or ammonia) when heated strongly, losing water; naming amides ("alkanamide", the C=O carbon is C1 and counted in the chain: ethanamide, propanamide, N-methylethanamide); intramolecular bonds are polar covalent (N–H, C–N, C=O); hydrogen bonding in primary and secondary amines is weaker than in alcohols because N is less electronegative than O, so the N–H bond is less polar (propan-1-amine 48 °C against propan-1-ol 97 °C, same electron count); among isomers, fewer N–H hydrogens means fewer hydrogen bonds and a lower boiling point (propan-1-amine, two N–H, 48 °C; N-methylethanamine, one, about 37 °C); tertiary amines have no N–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond to each other (trimethylamine 3 °C); small amines mix with water because water hydrogen bonds to the lone pair; primary amides have two N–H hydrogens and a C=O that takes hydrogen bonds, so each molecule makes more hydrogen bonds, building networks, and they boil much higher than acids of the same electron count, which mostly pair off as dimers (propanamide 213 °C against propanoic acid 141 °C); within each series, longer chains add dispersion, so boiling points rise; an amine's lone pair takes a proton from water (RNH₂ + H₂O ⇌ RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻), so its solution turns red litmus blue, but the equilibrium lies to the left (a weak base; Module 6 recall: ammonia); with hydrochloric acid it forms an ionic alkylammonium salt (ethylammonium chloride), which barely evaporates, so the fishy smell fades (the lemon trick); in an amide the C=O beside the nitrogen draws the lone pair in and shares it across N, C and O (the same C=O effect as the carboxylate in c09: sharing loosens the grip on a proton, which made the acid stronger and makes the amide barely a base), so it takes almost no protons from water and its solution is close to neutral; it is not acidic either (no O–H; its N–H hydrogens barely let go); the comparison: a carboxylic acid gives a proton from its O–H, an amine takes one with its lone pair; both are weak; the acid raises the hydronium ion concentration and turns blue litmus red (and fizzes with sodium hydrogen carbonate, a c09 recall on screen), the amine raises the hydroxide ion concentration and turns red litmus blue; mixed at room temperature they form a salt by proton transfer (methylammonium ethanoate), with no water formed; strong heating is needed to drive off water and make an amide. **Not here:** Kb and pKb values or calculations (not in the 2017 dotpoints; author note d); aniline and aryl amines (aromatic, beyond the course; the site marks phenol as extension, and aniline is the same case); amide resonance structures with formal charges (author note e); restricted rotation and peptide planarity (Biology and chem-m7-c15 territory); amide hydrolysis (author note f; root decision in Revision 2: no dotpoint needs it); polyamides and nylon (chem-m7-c15); quaternary ammonium detergents (chem-m7-c12 mentions cationic detergents); diamines putrescine and cadaverine (site hook; author note g); reaction of amines with sodium metal.

### 8. an amine is a weak base
> Back to the litmus. In water, the amine's lone pair **takes** a proton from a water molecule, like ammonia in Module six. That forms an ethylammonium ion, and leaves a hydroxide **ion** behind. ### 18. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Amine: ammonia with H replaced by alkyl groups (carbon chains, no C=O on N); N has a lone pair. Classify by carbon groups on N: 1° (RNH₂), 2° (R₂NH), 3° (R₃N). Propan-2-amine is 1°. Names: ethanamine, propan-1-amine, N-methylethanamine, N,N-dimethylethanamine.
2. Amide: C=O bonded to N, –CONH₂ (acid + amine, heated strongly → amide + water, condensation). Names: "-amide", the C=O carbon is C1: ethanamide, propanamide, N-methylethanamide.
3. Amine forces: N less electronegative than O, so N–H is less polar and amine hydrogen bonds are weaker (propan-1-amine 48 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C). Isomers: fewer N–H, lower boiling point, 1° > 2° > 3° (N-methylethanamine 37 °C; trimethylamine 3 °C, no N–H).
4. Amide forces: primary amides have two N–H + C=O, so more hydrogen bonds per molecule and a network (propanamide 213 °C > propanoic acid 141 °C, which pairs off as dimers). Similar size: primary amide > acid > alcohol > amine > alkane. Longer chain, higher boiling point.
5. Amine = weak base: RNH₂ + H₂O ⇌ RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻ (more OH⁻: red litmus → blue). With HCl: RNH₂ + HCl → RNH₃⁺Cl⁻ (an ionic salt). Amide: lone pair delocalised (shared) with the C=O, so it barely takes H⁺ and is close to neutral; no O–H, so not acidic.
6. Acid against base: carboxylic acid gives H⁺ (O–H), turns blue litmus red, fizzes with NaHCO₃; amine takes H⁺ (lone pair), turns red litmus blue, no fizz. Together at room temperature: a salt (CH₃COOH + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃NH₃⁺CH₃COO⁻), not an amide.
