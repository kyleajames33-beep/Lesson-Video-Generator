- **"kilo = 1000" first, word for word (Year 12 r14).** Scene 2 says "kilo means a thousand" and shows the chip "kilo = 1000" before writing 10³.
- **Powers as counts.** "Ten to the power three means three tens multiplied." A negative power means dividing by ten that many times, so 10⁻³ = 1 ÷ 1000 = 0.001 (a small positive number, not a negative one).
- **Steps of three, with centi the odd one (recurring model).** The prefix ladder from a01 grows, drawn as in a01 with the biggest at the top (Revision 2): T 12, G 9, M 6, k 3, | c −2, m −3, µ −6, n −9 from top to bottom. Each step between the big ones is ×1000; going down to a smaller unit, multiply.
- **Case matters.** Capital M is mega, lower case m is milli: a factor of a thousand million apart. This becomes the memory aid.
- **Storage uses the metric meaning.** "Giga means a billion", as storage makers label it. The binary difference (a computer reporting 256 GB as about 238 "GB") is an author note only, because the syllabus defines the prefixes as powers of ten.

### 2. kilo, mega, giga, tera: powers of ten
> Start with one you know. Kilo means a thousand, so kilo equals a thousand. A thousand is ten times ten times ten, three tens multiplied. We write that as ten to the power three. A power of ten just counts how many tens are multiplied **together**. Each big prefix is a thousand times the one before. Mega means a million, which is ten to the six. Giga means a billion, ten to the nine. And tera means a trillion, ten to the **twelve**. So the powers go up in threes, because every step multiplies by a thousand, and a thousand is three more **tens**. A two terabyte hard drive holds two times ten to the twelve bytes, that's two thousand **gigabytes**.

### 3. centi, milli, micro, nano: negative powers
> Small prefixes go the other way. Milli means a thousandth, so you divide by ten, three times. That's written as ten to the power minus three. A negative power doesn't make a negative number. It's a small positive number, nought point nought nought **one**. Centi, a hundredth, is ten to the minus two. Micro, a millionth, is ten to the minus six, and its symbol is the Greek letter mu, which looks like a u with a tail. Nano, a billionth, is ten to the minus **nine**. A sheet of paper is about a tenth of a millimetre thick. That's a hundred micrometres. The processor's parts are thousands of times smaller again, just a few **nanometres**.

### 4. scientific notation
> Now we can write big and small numbers neatly. Scientific notation means a number from one up to, but not including, ten, times a power of ten. Take two hundred and fifty-six gigabytes. Giga is ten to the nine, so it's two hundred and fifty-six times ten to the nine bytes. But two hundred and fifty-six isn't between one and ten. Move the decimal point two places left, to two point five six. Moving it left divides by a hundred, so the power goes up by two to make up for it. That's two point five six times ten to the eleven **bytes**. Small numbers work the same way. A chip feature of about three nanometres is three times ten to the minus nine **metres**. A common slip is to treat giga like kilo, and write only three zeros. That's two hundred and fifty-six thousand bytes, about one blurry **photo**.

### 5. changing prefixes
> To change from one prefix to another, count the steps on the ladder. Terabytes to gigabytes is one step down, to a smaller unit, so multiply by a thousand. Two terabytes is two thousand gigabytes. Millimetres to micrometres is one step down as well, so nought point five millimetres is five hundred **micrometres**. Prefixes also make sizes easy to compare. A movie download of four gigabytes on a two hundred and fifty-six gigabyte phone means two hundred and fifty-six divided by four, sixty-four movies, if nothing else was on **it**. And that two terabyte drive holds about seven point eight phones' **worth**.

### 6. significant figures
> Questions often ask for an answer to a number of significant figures. These are the digits that carry the measurement, counted from the first digit that isn't zero. Zeros before the first digit just hold the place, so they don't count. A zero between digits does. So nought point nought nought nought three nought four six has four significant figures, not **seven**. A photo from this phone is forty-thirty-two pixels wide and thirty twenty-four tall. That's twelve million, one hundred and ninety-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-eight pixels. To three significant figures, keep the first three digits, one, two, one. The next digit is a nine, so the last one rounds up to **two**. That's one point two two times ten to the seven, twelve point two **megapixels**. To two significant figures, it's one point two times ten to the **seven**. Small numbers too. The processor ticks three point two billion times a second, so each tick takes one divided by three point two times ten to the nine seconds. A calculator shows three point one two five E minus ten. The E means times ten to the power. To two significant figures, that's three point one times ten to the minus ten **seconds**.
