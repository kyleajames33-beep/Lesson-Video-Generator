Main question: *Why do comparable abiotic measurements differ among aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems?*
Entry knowledge: see above. Students have watched FA3 v23, v15, v13 and v10.
Stops after: aquatic means water-based and terrestrial means land-based; temperature, light and pH apply in both and can be compared directly, while dissolved oxygen and turbidity apply only in water, salinity is measured mainly in water (salty soils use different methods), and soil moisture, humidity and wind matter on land, so they are reported separately; water takes a lot of energy to warm and cools slowly, so a reef's water changes by about 1 °C in a day while desert air changes by 15 °C or more; a range means something only with its time frame (over a year the reef runs about 24 to 29 °C and the desert about 0 to 45 °C); air is about 21 per cent oxygen everywhere, but water holds only a few mg/L of dissolved oxygen, and warm water holds less (about 11 mg/L at 10 °C, about 8 at 25 °C); a cold, fast stream (11 mg/L) holds more oxygen than a warm, still pool (6 mg/L), and warm water raises animals' oxygen demand as supply falls, so oxygen-hungry mayfly nymphs live in the stream; salinity is measured in ppt (fresh water below about 0.5, seawater about 35, an estuary swinging from about 5 to 25 with tide and river flow); comparing by factor shows the reef stable on both temperature and salinity, the desert extreme in temperature, and the estuary changeable on both; stable conditions allow specialists such as corals, which bleach after one or two degrees of extra warmth, the desert demands heat and drought adaptations, and the estuary suits only species that control water and salt; a fair comparison uses the same instrument and method, comparable times and seasons, repeated readings and a named medium, and a factor that doesn't apply is "not applicable", never zero.

(b) Dissolved oxygen is oxygen dissolved in water, so it is a water-only (aquatic-specific) factor with no equivalent in air or dry soil (1 mark). On land, organisms are surrounded by air at about 21% oxygen, which barely varies, so oxygen is not limiting and is not measured; it is reported for the lake as a feature of that ecosystem, not compared (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed out: "the grassland has 0 mg/L oxygen". Other wrong chains: comparing averages only ("the lake is warmer"); "the lake is colder because water is cold".
2. (11c, evaluate, 3 marks.) Mangrove creek water: one reading, 6 am, July, glass thermometer. Coastal dune air: one reading, 2 pm, January, digital probe. Conclusion: "The dune is hotter, so it is the harsher ecosystem." Evaluate.
   Model: Temperature is a shared factor, so creek water and dune air can be compared, as long as the medium is stated (1 mark). The two readings were taken at different times of day, in different seasons and with different instruments, and only once each, so the difference could be caused by the method and timing rather than by the ecosystems, and no range or repeat is available (1 mark). Judgement: the conclusion is not supported; measure both at the same times over a full day in the same season with the same instrument, repeat and compare ranges, and judge "harsher" on more than one factor (for example the creek's changing salinity) (1 mark). Classic mistake crossed out: "water and land can never be compared".
