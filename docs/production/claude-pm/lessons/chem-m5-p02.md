### 1. hook (a colour that gives a number)
> In the last video, K changed with temperature, and every K we used was simply given to us. So where do those numbers come from? Nobody can count ions in a test tube. But here is the iron thiocyanate tube again. Iron three plus and thiocyanate join to make F E S C N two plus, an ion that is deep **red**, and the more of it there is, the deeper the colour. If we could turn that colour into a **number**, we could measure a concentration at equilibrium, and from that, a **real K**. So how does a colour reading give you K?

### 2. the colourimeter (what it measures)
> The instrument is a **colourimeter**. A lamp shines light through a small clear container of the solution, called a **cuvette**, and onto a detector. The red ion looks red because it soaks up **blue light** and lets the red through, so we set the colourimeter to blue light, about four hundred and forty-seven nanometres. The more red ions in the light's path, the more blue light they absorb, and the less reaches the detector. The meter reports this as **absorbance**, where zero means no light is absorbed. First we put in a cuvette of plain water, the **blank**, and set it to zero, so the water and the glass don't count.

Visual: the painted colourimeter (`m5x-colourimeter-v1.png`) centre-left; on "colourimeter" a pen label points at it. On "lamp" a coded cutaway opens beside it as a side view: lamp, blue filter, a cuvette slot, detector, readout "A = ?". On "cuvette" a painted cuvette (`m5x-cuvette-3-v1.png`) slides into the cutaway slot with a pen label "cuvette: holds the sample in the beam". ### 3. absorbance and concentration (A = εlc)
> Here is the useful part. If the light and the cuvette stay the same, **doubling** the number of red ions in the light's path **doubles** the absorbance. Chemists write this as A equals epsilon l c. A is the absorbance, c is the concentration, **l** is the width of the cuvette the light travels through, and **epsilon** is a number that says how strongly this ion absorbs this light. With the same cuvette and the same light, epsilon and l stay fixed, so absorbance rises **in step** with concentration.

Visual: two coded cuvettes side by side in the cutaway beam, the second with twice as many red dots (8 and 16 dots, key "same cuvette, same light"); on "doubling" the dots double, on "doubles" the readouts tick 0.20 then 0.40 (shape only, tag "pattern, not data"). On "A equals epsilon l c" the equation writes large and handwritten: "A = εlc". Each symbol lights on its spoken name with a short tag under it: A "absorbance", c "concentration (mol/L)", l "cuvette width (light path)" with a pen bracket across the cuvette, ε "how strongly this ion absorbs this light". On "stay fixed" ε and l get a teal lock icon; on "in step" a tag "A ∝ c" writes beside the equation, with a small grey tag under it: "absorbance doubles; the light let through does not simply halve" (author note a). Hold about 2 seconds.

### 4. standards (solutions where you know the answer)
> To turn a reading into a concentration, we need solutions where we already know the answer. These are the **standards**. Each one has a measured amount of thiocyanate and a **big excess** of iron three plus. All that extra iron pushes the equilibrium far to the right, just like adding a reactant in the disturbance videos, so **almost every** thiocyanate ion ends up in the red ion. That means the red ion's concentration is about equal to the thiocyanate concentration in that standard, which we know from how it was made. Five standards give **five known concentrations**, from pale to deep red.

Visual: two painted reagent bottles (`p02-bottle-kscn-v1.png`, colourless, and `p02-bottle-fe-v1.png`, pale yellow; Codex brief items 4 and 5) with coded labels "0.00200 mol/L KSCN" and "0.200 mol/L Fe3+, large excess". ### 5. the calibration graph
> Now measure the absorbance of each standard and plot it, **absorbance up the side** and concentration along the bottom. The blank gives the point at zero. The points fall close to a **straight line**, so we draw the line of best fit through them, not dot to dot. Because we zeroed with the blank, the line passes very close to the origin. Why not just use the formula? The slope you measure depends on this instrument, its filter and these cuvettes, so a book value of epsilon won't quite match yours. Measuring your own standards on the same machine is the **calibration**, and the line only applies inside the range of the standards.

### 6. reading the equilibrium mixture
> Now a real equilibrium. Mix 5.00 millilitres of 0.00200 moles per litre iron three plus with 5.00 millilitres of 0.00200 moles per litre thiocyanate. This time **neither is in excess**, so the mixture settles with all three ions present, and nobody knows how much red ion there is. That is what we measure. Its absorbance is **0.705**. Go **across** from 0.705 to the line, then straight **down** to the concentration axis: **1.50 times ten to the minus four** moles per litre. If you are given the line's equation instead, subtract the intercept from both sides, then divide both sides by the gradient, and you get the same value.

Visual: the task line "Find K for this mixture" writes top-left; the givens card fills in groups: "5.00 mL of 0.00200 mol/L Fe3+", "5.00 mL of 0.00200 mol/L SCN-", "total 10.00 mL", "same temperature as the standards". ### 7a. dilution first
> Before the ICE table, one trap. The bottles were 0.00200 moles per litre, but pouring 5 millilitres into a **10 millilitre** total spreads each ion through twice the volume. So each starting concentration **halves**: 0.00200 times 5.00 over 10.00 is **0.00100** moles per litre, for iron and for thiocyanate. Always use the concentrations after mixing, never the bottle values.

Visual: the dilution strip on the right: two painted 5.00 mL pipettes (`p02-pipette-v1.png`, liquid drawn in code as a masked fill: yellow-tinted Fe3+, clear SCN-) empty into one painted 10.00 mL volumetric flask (`p02-volumetric-flask-v1.png`, coded fill); the liquid is twice as tall and the yellow dots spread out to half the density (tag "same particles, twice the volume"). On "halves" the working writes under the strip: "0.00200 x 5.00 / 10.00 = 0.00100 mol/L", with the 5.00 and 10.00 pen-linked to the two volumes; it is repeated as "SCN-: 0.00100 mol/L" on "for iron and for thiocyanate". ### 7b. ICE table, E row first
> Now the ICE table from earlier: **I for initial**, **C for change**, and **E for equilibrium**. The initial row is 0.00100 for iron, 0.00100 for thiocyanate, and zero for the red ion. The measured value goes in the **equilibrium row** first: 1.50 times ten to the minus four for the red ion. Its change is equilibrium minus initial, so **plus** 1.50 times ten to the minus four. The equation is one to one to one, so iron and thiocyanate each **fall** by the same amount. That leaves **8.50 times ten to the minus four** of each at equilibrium.

Visual: the C6a ICE table replaces the dilution strip, columns Fe3+, SCN-, FeSCN2+ (mol/L), rows Initial, Change, Equilibrium, each row label lighting on its spoken letter. On "initial row" the Initial cells tick to "1.00 x 10^-3", "1.00 x 10^-3", "0" (pen arrows from the givens card). On "equilibrium row" the C6a pen arrow runs from "[FeSCN2+]eq = 1.50 x 10^-4" into the FeSCN2+ Equilibrium cell; on "equilibrium minus initial" the "E - I" bracket draws up to the Change cell, which ticks to "+1.50 x 10^-4" (made colour). On "one to one to one" the coefficient chips "1 : 1 : 1" fly from the equation strip along the Change row and the two reactant Change cells tick to "-1.50 x 10^-4" (used up colour) on "fall". ### 14. key notes (quiet, hold about 75 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. A colourimeter measures absorbance: how much light a coloured solution absorbs. FeSCN2+ is red because it absorbs blue light, so it is measured with blue light (about 447 nm). Set zero first with a blank of water.
2. A = εlc. With the same cuvette (l) and the same light (ε), absorbance is proportional to concentration.
3. Standards: a known amount of SCN- with a large excess of Fe3+, so almost all the SCN- becomes FeSCN2+ and [FeSCN2+] is known. Plot absorbance against [FeSCN2+] and draw the line of best fit (the calibration curve). Use it only inside the range of the standards.
4. Equilibrium mixture: neither reactant in excess. Read [FeSCN2+] from the line (across, then down). Starting concentrations are after mixing: c = c(bottle) x V(added) / V(total).
5. ICE table: measured [FeSCN2+] in the E row first, change 1 : 1 : 1, then K = [FeSCN2+] / ([Fe3+][SCN-]). Repeat with several mixtures; quote the mean, the range and the temperature.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why a colour can carry a number: the red ion absorbs blue light, so more red ions absorb more of the beam (2), and with a fixed cuvette and light that absorbance rises in step with concentration (3). Why the standards are known: a big excess of Fe3+ pushes the equilibrium far right, so almost every SCN- becomes the red ion (4, the C3 and C4a cause recalled in a clause). Why a calibration line instead of the formula: ε belongs to this instrument, filter and cuvette (5). Why the mixture is different: neither reactant in excess, so it settles with all three present and its red ion is the unknown (6). Why the starting values halve: the same particles spread through twice the volume (7a). Why the change row: one to one to one (7b). Why K in the hundreds is not "mostly products": only 15 percent reacted (8). Why repeats: if K is constant, different mixtures must agree, and their spread measures trust (9).
