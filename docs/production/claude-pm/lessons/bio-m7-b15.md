In 1993, the United States government applied to patent a compound from Western Australian smoke bush, and Aboriginal people of the region received no payment or credit. Ten years later, the Jarlmadangah Burru Aboriginal Corporation was named as a co-owner of a patent for pain-relieving compounds from mudjala, shared by Nyikina Mangala Elder John Watson with Griffith University. What made the difference? Learn who holds Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander medical knowledge, what Indigenous cultural and intellectual property means, why a lab result isn't yet a medicine, and why the law let smoke bush happen. Then learn the protocols: consultation (ask first); free, prior and informed consent; collaboration; attribution (credit the knowledge holders by name); and benefit sharing agreed in advance. See them applied to mudjala and to spinifex, a biological material developed with the Dugalunji Aboriginal Corporation and the University of Queensland. Two exam-style questions with brief feedback.

Biology 2017: Year 12 Module 7, Infectious Disease. Inquiry question: "How can the spread of infectious diseases be controlled?"
