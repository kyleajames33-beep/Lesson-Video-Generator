### 4. how it goes wrong

Taking knowledge or resources like this, without consent or a fair share, is called **biopiracy**.

### 6. the protocols: ask first, and consent
> **Protocols** are agreed rules for how outsiders work with a community's knowledge and resources. The first is to ask first: consult the community before the research is designed, so it shapes the question. The second is **free, prior and informed consent**. Free means no pressure. Prior means before any work starts. Informed means the community knows what's planned, including any commercial use. And consent to one use isn't consent to another.

### 10. published frameworks, and why they matter
> These rules are written into published frameworks. The international **Nagoya Protocol** says plants, animals and the traditional knowledge about them should be used only with prior informed consent, on agreed terms, with fair benefit sharing. In Australia, the **AIATSIS Code of Ethics** guides research with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Why does this matter? Justice: knowledge built over generations shouldn't be taken without return. Self-determination: the community decides how its knowledge is used. And continuity: holders pass knowledge on under their own rules, and sharing it outside them can weaken that.

Visual: the board clears to the protocol card (all five rows grey). ### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander medical knowledge (including bush medicine) is held by particular knowledge holders for particular communities and Country; some is restricted. The knowledge has owners. Indigenous cultural and intellectual property (ICIP): a community's right to control its own knowledge, practices, stories and resources.
- Without protocols (biopiracy): plants taken from Country or knowledge read from a record; a compound patented; the community not asked, not named, no share. Why the law allowed it: often no law required consent; a patent needs novelty; a purified compound can count as new even when the plant's use is known; spoken knowledge not written where examiners search can look new.
- Protocols: consultation (ask first, before the research is designed); free, prior and informed consent (consent to one use is not consent to another); collaboration (work together throughout); attribution (acknowledge the knowledge holders as they choose); benefit sharing (agreed in advance, in a form the community chooses).
- The same rules apply to biological materials (fibres, resins, dyes); a fair agreement sets the use, the naming and the benefits, and often gives a veto. Frameworks: the Nagoya Protocol (Australia signed, not ratified); the AIATSIS Code of Ethics (2020); some state laws.
- Why protection matters: justice (no taking without return), self-determination (the community decides), continuity (respect limits on sharing). A lab result is not a medicine.
