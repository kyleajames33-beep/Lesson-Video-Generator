## Script and shots

### 1. hook: a table older than the electron
> Here's something strange. Mendeleev arranged the elements in eighteen sixty-nine, by grouping elements with similar properties. The electron wasn't discovered until eighteen ninety-**seven**. Yet look at the modern table's shape. The left block is two columns wide, the middle block is ten, and the right block is **six**. Those are exactly the capacities of the s, d and p subshells. Let's see **how**.

Visual: opens on v11's final frame; the "Next" chip fades. On the first sentence a small coded timeline writes above the table: "1869: elements arranged by properties". On "seven" it extends: "1897: electron discovered" (grey tag "28 years later"). On "shape" the three "?" brackets pulse; on "two columns wide" the "2?" resolves to "2" and the s columns tint blue; then "10" (d, amber), and on "six" "6" (p, green). On "capacities" small chips "s holds 2", "d holds 10", "p holds 6" fly from the brackets to sit beside them. On "how" the f rows tint grey with a "14" bracket, and the heading writes on: "Groups, periods and blocks" (Revision 2: cue moved from the cut "existed" sentence).

### 2. periods: the outer shell
> Start with the rows, called periods. An element's period number is its highest occupied energy level, its outer **shell**. Across period three, from sodium to argon, every element is filling level three, first three s, then three **p**. Sodium ends in three s one. Argon ends in three p six, with level three's s and p both **full**. Then potassium puts its next electron into level four, and a new period **begins**.

Visual: on "periods" the row labels 1 to 7 write down the left edge (gloss "period: a row"). On "shell" a chip "period = highest occupied level". On "p" the period 3 row outlines in ink and its eight cells show configuration endings as a wave from left to right: "3s¹, 3s², 3s² 3p¹ ... 3s² 3p⁶". On "full" the Na and Ar chips ring. On "begins" the K cell (still lit from v11 with "…4s¹") pulses at the start of row 4, with a curved arrow from Ar to K.

### 3. blocks: the last subshell
> Now the blocks. An element's block tells you the type of subshell its last electron went **into**. The two columns on the left are the s block. The six on the right are the p block. The ten in the middle are the d block, and the two separate rows underneath are the f block, fourteen **wide**. That's why the table has this shape. Each block is as wide as the number of electrons its subshell can **hold**.

Visual: on "into" each lit cell's chip glows its last term ("3p³" green for P, "4s¹" blue for K, "3d⁶" amber for Fe; gloss "block: which subshell fills last"). The block tints deepen one at a time as spoken: s, p, d, and on "wide" the f rows. Grey tags (not spoken): "He: 1s², placed with the noble gases" on helium and "H: a non-metal" on hydrogen. On "hold" the four width brackets glow with their subshell capacities beside them, and a short line writes: "block width = subshell capacity".

### 4. groups: the same outer arrangement
> The columns are groups, and this is where the pattern really pays off. Valence electrons are the electrons in the outer shell. Down group one, lithium ends in two s one, sodium in three s one, and potassium in four s **one**. Different levels, but always one valence electron, in an s subshell. Down group seventeen, every element ends in s two, p five, so seven valence **electrons**. For groups one and two, the number of valence electrons equals the group number. For groups thirteen to eighteen, subtract **ten**. Helium has **two**.

Visual: the column numbers 1 to 18 write along the top (gloss "group: a column"). On the second sentence gloss "valence electrons: in the outer shell". On "one" the group 1 column highlights and a configuration stack writes beside it: "Li 1s² 2s¹", "Na [Ne] 3s¹", "K [Ar] 4s¹", with the last terms aligned and ringed blue (tag "1 valence electron"). On "electrons" group 17 highlights with "F …2s² 2p⁵", "Cl …3s² 3p⁵", "Br …4s² 4p⁵" ringed green (tag "7 valence electrons"). ### 6. reading it, and a classic trap
> Period four, because its highest level is **four**. Group two, because it has two valence electrons, in an s **subshell**. It's calcium. Here's a classic trap. An element with six valence electrons isn't in group six. Group six is in the d block. Six valence electrons, s two p four, puts it in group **sixteen**.

### 7. same arrangement, similar chemistry
> Here's why groups matter so much. A chemical property describes how a substance reacts to form new substances. A physical property can be observed without making a new substance, like state, colour, or conductivity. Elements in a group share their valence arrangement, so they react in similar ways. Every group one metal reacts with water, making hydrogen gas and an alkaline **solution**. The halogens in group seventeen are all reactive non-metals, made of pairs of atoms. And the noble gases, with a full outer shell, hardly react at **all**.

Visual: glosses write at the right: "chemical property: how it reacts to form new substances" and "physical property: observed without making a new substance". On "react in similar ways" the group 1, 17 and 18 columns highlight together. On "all" group 18's cells get a "full outer shell" chip and single-atom dots (tag "hardly react").

### 8. metals, non-metals and metalloids
> Position also tells us about physical properties. Metals sit on the left and in the middle, with just a few valence electrons. They're shiny, they conduct heat and electricity, and they're malleable, which means they can be hammered into shape. Non-metals sit at the top **right**. As solids they're dull and brittle, they don't conduct electricity, and many are gases. Along the staircase between them are the metalloids, like **silicon**. Silicon is shiny like a metal, but brittle like a non-metal, and it conducts electricity only a little. That's why it's used in computer **chips**.

### 9. across period three at room temperature
> Let's walk across period three at room temperature. Sodium, magnesium and aluminium are solid **metals**. Silicon is a solid metalloid. Phosphorus and sulfur are solid **non-metals**. Then chlorine is a pale green gas, and argon is a colourless **gas**. So across a period, elements change from metals to non-metals. And across the whole table, only eleven elements are gases at room temperature, and only two are liquids, bromine and **mercury**. All the rest are solids.

### 10. a surprise: one group, three states
> Here's a surprise. The halogens share their chemistry, but not their state. Fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid, and iodine is a **solid**. Same valence arrangement, different states. Explaining that needs the forces between molecules, which come later in this module. So a group shares its chemistry, but not every physical **property**.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Period (row) = highest occupied energy level. Group (column) = elements with the same outer arrangement. Block = the type of subshell filled last (s, p, d, f); block widths 2, 6, 10, 14 match subshell capacities.
2. Valence electrons: electrons in the outer shell. Groups 1, 2: valence electrons = group number. Groups 13 to 18: group number − 10, except helium (2) (6 valence electrons → group 16, not 6).
