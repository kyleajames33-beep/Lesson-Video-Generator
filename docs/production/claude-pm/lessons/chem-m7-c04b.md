- **Author notes (not learner copy):** (a) Alkane reactivity: C–H (about 413 kJ/mol) and C–C (about 348 kJ/mol) bonds are strong and nearly non-polar, and an alkane has no multiple bond, so halogens do not react with it at room temperature in the dark. "Nothing you can see happens" is accurate at this level. (b) Mechanism (not taught): UV light is absorbed by Br₂ (or Cl₂) and splits it homolytically into atoms (radicals); a chain of propagation steps replaces H with Br and forms HBr; light is used up in initiation and is not regenerated, so it is not a catalyst. The video says only "gives the energy to split bromine molecules apart, and that starts the reaction". Sunlight also works (its UV part). (c) Hook demonstration: a few drops of bromine (or bromine solution) in hexane stay orange-brown in the dark; under a UV lamp or bright sunlight the colour fades over minutes and HBr is given off, which fumes in moist air and turns damp blue litmus red. Hex-1-ene decolourises bromine water within seconds at room temperature without light (c04a author note d: the product in water is mainly the bromo-alcohol; the colour change is what matters). The hook says "almost at once" for the alkene and "slowly" for the alkane, and makes no time claim. Bromine is toxic and corrosive; the demonstration is a teacher's, in a fume cupboard (grey chip; chem-m7-p02 owns safety). (d) Alkyne hydrogenation: with Ni (or Pd, Pt) and enough hydrogen the reaction goes to the alkane, and in practice a mixture forms if hydrogen is limited; stopping cleanly at the alkene needs a poisoned catalyst (Lindlar; site extension). The video writes the two steps and the overall equation with + 2H₂, and says "with just one H₂ for each ethyne, you'd write the first step" as an equation statement, not a claim about what the catalyst does. Conditions as c04a: Ni catalyst, about 150 °C. (e) HBr and ethyne: step 1 gives bromoethene, CH₂=CHBr; step 2 can give 1,1-dibromoethane, CH₃CHBr₂ (the main product, Markovnikov) or 1,2-dibromoethane, CH₂BrCH₂Br. The video names both as possible products, consistent with c04a's treatment of propene, and does not say which forms more. (f) Alkyne hydration (site extension): HC≡CH + H₂O → CH₃CHO (ethanal, via the enol CH₂=CHOH) with dilute H₂SO₄ and Hg²⁺ at about 60 °C; propyne gives propanone. "The first product rearranges into a different family" is true (an enol becomes an aldehyde or ketone). "Water questions in this course are usually about alkenes" reflects the site's own statement that alkyne hydration is not assessed and is hedged on purpose. (g) Bromine water and alkynes: alkynes decolourise bromine water, often more slowly than alkenes; the video says "quickly" only for the comparison with alkanes in the dark and does not compare alkene and alkyne rates. Cycloalkanes behave like alkanes (c04a author note g); not mentioned here. General formula CnH2n−2 is for an open-chain hydrocarbon with one triple bond (dienes share it; c03 author note f); scene 9 says "an alkyne" and chem-m7-g01 Q4 states the open-chain, one-multiple-bond condition. (h) Combustion (site card 4) is out of scope by route: complete combustion of hydrocarbons is Year 11 and Module 7's combustion work is alcohols (chem-m7-c07). (i) Product mixtures: methane with chlorine gives all four chloromethanes and some ethane; "usually gives a mixture" is hedged because a large excess of methane favours chloromethane. Propane gives 1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane in comparable amounts. HSC answers accept one named product with HX. (j) "Excess" in HSC questions means more than the amount needed to react completely. (k) Pronunciation: "one, one, two, two-tetrabromoethane" (commas give short pauses); "one, two-dibromoethene" ("di-BRO-mo-ETH-een"); "bromoethene"; "propyne" as "PRO-pine"; "ethyne" as "ETH-ine"; "UV" as letters; "H Br", "H Cl", "Cl two", "Br two" as letters; "C n H two n minus two" as letters and numbers; "tetrachloromethane" as "TET-ra-KLOR-o-METH-ane".

### 2. why an alkane has nothing to add to
> Here's the difference. An alkene has a weaker second bond it can open, so it adds. An alkane has only **single bonds**, and every carbon already has four. For an alkane to react, a bond has to **break** first, and that takes **energy**.

### 16. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Alkanes have only strong single bonds and no spare bond, so they don't add. With a halogen and UV light they substitute: one H is swapped for a halogen, and HX is the second product. CH₃CH₃ + Br₂ → CH₃CH₂Br + HBr (UV light).
2. UV light is the energy source, not a catalyst: it is energy, not a substance, and it is absorbed. Write "UV light" on the arrow. Substitution can repeat (CH₄ → CH₃Cl → ... → CCl₄) and happen at different carbons, so it gives a mixture: write one product + HX.

4. Alkenes and alkynes decolourise bromine water quickly in the dark; alkanes don't. The test shows a multiple bond, not which one: use the formula (alkene CnH2n, alkyne CnH2n−2).
