### 2. odd functions cancel
> Turn the graph half a turn about the origin, and it lands on **itself**. That's what an odd function does. In algebra, f of minus x equals minus f of x. Here, minus x, all cubed, is minus x cubed, and cos of minus x is cos x, so the sign flips. Now pair each thin strip at x with its partner at minus **x**. They have the same width and the same size, but one is above the axis and one is below. So every pair cancels, and the integral is zero. Check it on a simpler odd function, x cubed, from minus two to two. The primitive, x to the fourth over four, gives four minus four, which is **zero**.

Visual: on "itself" `SymmetryPairs` (mode `'odd'`) copies the right half as a dashed ghost, rotates it 180° about the origin over about 1.2 s, and lands it on the left half; a tag "half-turn about the origin: same graph". On the algebra sentences the right column writes "f(−x) = (−x)³ cos(−x) = −x³ cos x = −f(x)" with "(−x)³ = −x³" and "cos(−x) = cos x" as small grey rows above. Boxed rule: "f odd: ∫₋ₐᵃ f(x) dx = 0".

### 3. even functions double
> Even functions do the opposite. Their graph is a mirror image across the y-axis, so f of minus x equals f of x. Cos x is even. Now each strip at minus x matches its partner at x, with the same sign, so the left half equals the right **half**. That means the integral from minus a to a is twice the integral from zero to a. For cos x, from minus pi over two to pi over two, it's twice the integral from zero to pi over two, which is two times one, or **two**.

### 5. the trapezoidal rule, from shared edges
> Symmetry only helps when the graph has it. Otherwise, if there's no primitive, we estimate, and trapezia gave the best estimates in the strips video. Each trapezium's area is half the strip width, times its two heights added together. Now add four trapezia, side by **side**. Each inside height is an edge of two trapezia, so it's counted **twice**. The two end heights belong to one trapezium each, so they're counted once. That gives the trapezoidal rule: h over two, times the first height, plus two times every inside height, plus the last **height**. Here h is the strip width, b minus a, over n, and n strips need n plus one heights.

Visual: clear change: a generic falling curve (the 1/x panel of scene 6, unlabelled for now) with five heights y₀ to y₄ drawn as vertical ink lines. ## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why an odd integral is 0: the half-turn maps the graph to itself, so strips at ±x are equal and opposite (2). Why an even integral doubles: the mirror makes the halves equal (3). Why a mixed integrand splits: the sum rule plus each part's symmetry (4). Why the interval must be symmetric: the pairing needs a partner for every strip (4). Why inside heights count twice: two trapezia share each inside edge (5). Why five values make four strips: each strip needs two edges, and neighbours share one (5, 6). Why the estimate is too big: concave up along the whole interval puts every chord above the curve (7). Why a tool's answer needs rewriting: notation differs and constants or mod signs may be missing (8). Why a tool's beyond-course answer can be trusted: differentiating returns the integrand, and other methods agree (9).
