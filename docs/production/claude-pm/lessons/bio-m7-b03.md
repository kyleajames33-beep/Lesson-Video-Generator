### 2. the chain: leave, travel, enter
> Every pathogen has the same problem. It has to leave one host, travel, and get into a new host. The way it travels is called its mode of **transmission**, and there are three: **direct contact**, **indirect contact**, and **vector** transmission. Each step is a link in a chain, and every link is a place where the chain can break.

The COVID-19 virus's spike protein binds a receptor on cells lining the airways, and that lets the virus get inside.

### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Every pathogen must leave a host, travel and enter a new host. The way it travels is its mode of transmission.
- Direct contact: host to host with nothing between (skin, body fluids, close-range droplets; tinea, HIV, influenza). Indirect contact: a non-living carrier, such as a fomite (contaminated object), water, food or air (norovirus, cholera, *Salmonella*, tuberculosis). Vector: a living carrier, usually an insect or tick (*Anopheles* mosquitoes carry *Plasmodium*). A fomite is not a vector.
- Entry adaptations get a pathogen in and keep it there: fimbriae grip host cells; viral surface proteins bind specific receptors; tapeworm hooks and suckers anchor to the gut wall.
- Transmission adaptations get it out and across: influenza triggers coughing; *Plasmodium* is injected in mosquito saliva; tapeworm eggs and cysts in undercooked pork; tobacco mosaic virus survives on tools.
- Control must match the route: barriers for direct contact, treat the carrier for indirect contact, break it at the vector for vectors.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (21 Oct) against standard texts (Campbell Biology; OpenStax Microbiology, chapters 15 and 16) and the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Snow, 1854.** Cholera outbreak in Soho, London, late August to September 1854; Snow mapped deaths around the Broad Street pump; the pump handle was removed on 8 September 1854 after Snow presented his evidence to the parish Board of Guardians (St James's). Snow did not know the cause; Pacini described the bacterium in 1854 (not known to Snow; not widely accepted). Narration: hook "the map showed how it travelled: in drinking water"; scene 10 "Snow had the pump's handle taken off, which broke the water link". Neither claims that removing the handle ended the outbreak (it was already declining).
- **Direct contact.** Tinea (dermatophyte fungi) spreads by skin contact (and also from shared items such as towels; the narration's "skin touching skin" is one route, not the only one; acceptable). HIV spreads in blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk: sex, shared needles, and mother to child in pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. Correct.
- **Droplets.** Large respiratory droplets fall within about 1 to 2 m; influenza spreads mainly by droplets at close range, and also by smaller airborne particles and contaminated surfaces (CDC influenza "how flu spreads"). The narration says "a main route for influenza" and "usually counted as direct contact" (the site's and textbooks' convention). No size cut-off is spoken (site error 4). COVID-19 is not assigned a route in the narration (time-sensitive, contested terminology; WHO 2024 "through the air" terminology).
- **Indirect contact.** Norovirus persists on surfaces (fomites) for days to weeks; cholera (*Vibrio cholerae*) by contaminated water; *Salmonella* by undercooked poultry and eggs; tuberculosis and measles by airborne particles that can remain suspended in a room (measles for up to about two hours after an infected person leaves, CDC). "Can hang in a room long after an infected person has left": correct for measles; "long" is unquantified for tuberculosis (acceptable; TB droplet nuclei remain airborne for hours in poorly ventilated rooms).
- **Vectors.** *Plasmodium* (protozoan) carried by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes; it develops in the mosquito (gut wall oocysts, then sporozoites in the salivary glands) and is injected in saliva during a blood meal (CDC malaria biology). "Develops inside the mosquito, so the mosquito is part of its life cycle" and "ends up in the mosquito's salivary glands ... injected with saliva ... straight past the skin": correct.
- **Fimbriae.** Fimbriae (attachment pili) are short protein filaments that bind host cell surface molecules (adhesion), resisting removal by mucus, urine flow and peristalsis; for example uropathogenic *E. coli* type 1 and P fimbriae. "Grip molecules on host cells, so the bacteria aren't washed away by mucus or urine": correct. The painted rod plate shows hair-like surface threads plus one flagellum; the flagellum is not mentioned and no species is claimed.
- **Viral receptor binding.** SARS-CoV-2 spike protein binds ACE2 on host cells, including airway epithelium, followed by fusion at the cell surface or after endocytosis; influenza haemagglutinin binds sialic acid on airway cells. "Binds a receptor on cells lining the airways, and that lets the virus get inside": correct. "Surface proteins that fit receptor molecules on particular host cells" explains host and tissue specificity.
- **Compare (scene 7).** "The bacterium can multiply on that surface, while the virus has to get inside the cell": correct as a general contrast for the cases shown (many bacteria colonise surfaces; some invade cells, so "can" not "does"). Viruses replicate only inside host cells (b01).
- **Pork tapeworm.** *Taenia solium* scolex has a rostellum with a double crown of hooks and four suckers; it attaches to the small intestine wall. Eggs pass in human faeces; pigs swallow them and larvae form cysts (cysticerci) in muscle; people are infected by eating undercooked or raw pork containing cysts; thorough cooking or freezing kills the cysts (WHO taeniasis/cysticercosis fact sheet; CDC). (Author note, not learner copy: people who swallow the eggs can develop cysticercosis; not narrated.) Narration: "The pork tapeworm releases eggs in faeces. In a pig they become cysts in the muscle, and eating undercooked pork starts the cycle again": correct.
- **Influenza and coughing.** Influenza infects the respiratory epithelium; the resulting inflammation triggers coughing and sneezing, which expel virus-laden droplets. Correct. (The site's "millions of viral particles" is not used.)
- **Tobacco mosaic virus.** Very stable rod-shaped virus, spread mechanically through wounds by contaminated tools, hands and plant contact; it survives for long periods in dried plant material and on surfaces; growers disinfect tools (for example with milk or bleach solutions). "Survives on pruning tools, which carry it from plant to plant": correct.
- **Controls.** Barriers and distance for direct contact (gloves, condoms, isolation when sick); water chlorination, cooking, surface cleaning and ventilation for indirect contact; bed nets, repellents and removal of standing water for mosquito vectors (WHO). Surface cleaning has little effect on airborne tuberculosis transmission (CDC TB infection control: ventilation and respiratory protection). "Scrubbing surfaces does little against tuberculosis in the air": correct.
- **Attempt 1.** Ross River virus is an alphavirus carried by *Aedes* and *Culex* mosquitoes, with native animals (especially macropods) as the main reservoir; human-to-human transmission does not occur by direct contact (Queensland Health; Healthdirect). Larvicides kill mosquito larvae in water. Cases rise after heavy rain when breeding sites increase. The item is hypothetical and names no town. (Author note: viraemic people can occasionally infect mosquitoes in epidemics, so the model says "little effect", and the narration says "a link it barely uses".) Feedback swaps the *Anopheles* plate for the generic mosquito plate.
- **Attempt 2.** *V. cholerae* attaches to small-intestine epithelium using the toxin-coregulated pilus; cholera toxin causes profuse watery diarrhoea that sheds large numbers of bacteria; spread by faecally contaminated water and food (WHO cholera fact sheet). Influenza haemagglutinin binds sialic acid receptors on airway epithelial cells. Correct as written.
