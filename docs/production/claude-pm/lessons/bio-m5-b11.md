Main question: *How do you work out how common a characteristic or an allele is in a population, and what can SNP data, and their limits, tell you?*

Stops after: calculating the frequency of a characteristic as a count over the stated total, and presenting it as a table and a bar chart; calculating an allele frequency by counting copies (two per individual for an autosomal gene); explaining why a recessive phenotype frequency gives only the homozygous recessive genotype frequency, because heterozygotes look like dominant homozygotes; describing a SNP as a one-base difference at a known position, each version an allele, and saying that reading both copies shows the genotype directly; saying that most SNPs change nothing but some change a protein; calculating a SNP allele frequency from genotype counts; comparing two groups to identify a trend and calling it an association, not a cause, with the reason (a nearby DNA difference inherited with it); explaining why one SNP cannot show relatedness; naming sample limits (size, representation, consistent categories); applying all of it to unseen clover leaf-mark counts and unseen SNP genotype counts.

### 2. frequency of a characteristic
> Start with the cattle themselves. A frequency tells you how common something is in the group you counted. It's the count divided by the total. Sixty-four out of a hundred cattle are red, so the frequency of red coats is sixty-four per cent. Roan is thirty-two per cent, and white is four. A table keeps the counts, and a bar chart shows the pattern at a glance. But always say what you divided by. Here, it's cattle.

Visual: the sorted grid slides left and shrinks. On "count divided by the total" the line "frequency = count / total counted" writes on in hand type. `TallyTable` builds one row per cue (computed): "red | 64 | out of 100 | 64%", "roan | 32 | out of 100 | 32%", "white | 4 | out of 100 | 4%", each row lighting its block in the grid. On "a bar chart" `FreqBars` draws three bars from the table, each labelled with its per cent, y axis "per cent of cattle counted (n = 100)". On "what you divided by" the "out of 100" column rings amber and a tag writes on: "denominator: cattle counted". Hold one second.

### 3. allele frequency: count the copies

Each roan has one, which makes thirty-two more. That's forty white alleles out of two hundred, so the white allele frequency is twenty per cent, and the red allele is eighty. Four per cent counted only the white cattle, and thirty-six counted animals, not alleles.

### 4. when the alleles hide
> Cattle make this easy, because every genotype looks different. Peas don't. In a field of a hundred pea plants, nine are short. Short is recessive, so those nine must have two short alleles. That's a genotype frequency of nine per cent. But a tall plant could be hiding a short allele, or not, and both kinds look the same. So looks alone can't count every allele. For that, you have to read the DNA itself.

### 5. what a SNP is
> Line up the same stretch of DNA from several people, and most bases match. But at some positions, people differ by just one base. Here, some copies have C, and others have T. That's a single nucleotide polymorphism, or snip for short. Single nucleotide means one base, and polymorphism means more than one form in the population. Each form is an allele. And because both copies are read, every genotype shows directly, including the heterozygous one, with one C and one T.

### 6. how common SNPs are, and when they matter
> Two unrelated people differ at roughly one base in every thousand, so each of us carries millions of snips. Most sit in DNA that doesn't code for a protein, and change nothing we can see. A few do matter. The sickle cell allele from the proteins video is one: a single A changed to T. So snips are markers we can count, and only sometimes causes.

Visual: the read strip zooms out to a long chromosome bar (coded, cream) with dozens of small tick marks scattered along it (grey tag "about 1 base in 1,000 differs between two unrelated people"). ### 7. counting SNP alleles
> Now count them, just like the cattle. Fifty people are tested at this snip. Eighteen have C on both copies, twenty-four have one C and one T, and eight have T on both. Fifty people carry a hundred copies. The T alleles come to twenty-four, plus sixteen, which makes forty. So the T allele frequency is forty per cent. Count alleles, not people.

### 8. comparing groups: association, not cause
> Snips become evidence when you compare groups. Say those fifty people all have a particular disease, and fifty people without it are tested too. In that second group, T is only twenty per cent. That's a trend: T turns up more often with the disease. We call that an association. But it doesn't prove that T causes it. The snip might just sit close to the DNA that really matters, on the same chromosome. Close neighbours are rarely split by crossing over, so they're inherited together.

Visual: the group panel. `GroupBeads`: left group "with the disease (50 people)" as 100 allele beads with 40 T beads lit; right group "without the disease (50 people)" as 100 beads, its table writing on "CC 32, CT 16, TT 2" and 20 T beads lit (computed). ### 9. one SNP is one marker
> And one snip is only one marker. Two populations can share a version at one snip, and still differ at thousands of others. That's why studies of relatedness and ancestry compare many snips at once, and look at the overall pattern.

### 10. the limits of a sample
> Every one of these numbers comes from a sample, and samples have limits. Imagine a whole breed where the white allele really is twenty per cent. Sample twenty cattle, and you might get ten per cent, or thirty. Sample two hundred, and you'll usually stay close to twenty. Small samples bounce around. A sample also has to represent the population: one farm, or one family, may not. And everyone has to sort the same way. Is a red cow with a few white hairs red, or roan?

### 14. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Frequency of a characteristic = count / total counted. State the denominator. Present as a table and a bar chart.
- Allele frequency = copies of that allele / all copies. Autosomal gene: 2 copies per individual (100 individuals = 200 copies).
- Complete dominance: recessive phenotype frequency = homozygous recessive genotype frequency (9% short = 0.09 tt). Carriers can't be seen.
- SNP: a one-base difference at a known DNA position; each version is an allele. Reading both copies gives the genotype. Most change nothing; some matter (sickle cell).
- An allele more common in one group = association, not proof of cause. One SNP is one marker. Limits: sample size, representation, consistent categories.

Memory: Count alleles, not people. Frequency, SNP, limits.
