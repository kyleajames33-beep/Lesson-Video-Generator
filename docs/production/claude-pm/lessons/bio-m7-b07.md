### 15. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

- Antigen: a molecule the immune system can recognise, usually a protein or sugar chain on a pathogen's surface. Receptors and antibodies bind one small part of it (an epitope) with a particular shape.
- Antibody: a Y-shaped protein; two identical binding sites at the arm tips (variable region); stem the same in many different antibodies (constant region). A B cell's receptors are antibodies held in its membrane.
- Each B cell has one receptor shape; random shuffling of receptor genes gives millions of shapes before any infection.
- Clonal selection: the antigen picks out the few B cells that fit; usually a helper T cell's go-ahead is also needed. The cell divides into a clone (days). Many become plasma cells (release antibodies of the selected shape, which the blood carries to the infection); some become memory B cells.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (22 Oct) against the sources named. The Claude science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Burnet.** Frank Macfarlane Burnet, director of the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute, Melbourne, proposed the clonal selection theory in 1957 ("A modification of Jerne's theory of antibody production using the concept of clonal selection", *Australian Journal of Science* 20: 67 to 69); Talmage proposed a similar idea the same year. The 1960 Nobel Prize (with Medawar) was for acquired immunological tolerance (site error 1). "Proposed that it doesn't design anything. The right cells already exist before the infection, and the infection picks them out": a fair plain statement of the theory. Correct.
- **Antigen.** A molecule (usually protein or polysaccharide) that can be specifically bound by an antibody or lymphocyte receptor; receptors bind a small region (epitope). "A molecule that the immune system can recognise and respond to": correct at this level (site error 6). Revision 2: "a receptor doesn't fit the whole antigen, just one small part of its surface", so the line cannot be heard as the antigen being cut up first (B cells bind epitopes on intact antigen; science check finding 2). One bacterium carries many different antigens (proteins, polysaccharides): correct.
- **B cell receptor and antibody.** The B cell receptor is a membrane-bound antibody (immunoglobulin) with an associated signalling complex; each B cell expresses receptors of a single specificity (allelic exclusion). Antibodies are Y-shaped (two heavy and two light chains); two identical antigen-binding sites formed by the variable regions at the arm tips; the constant region (including the Fc stem) is shared within a class (isotype). Revision 2 (science check finding 4, learning review finding 4): "Its stem is the same in many different antibodies", because "type" (class, isotype) was never explained and "every" invites the allotype exception. Correct. "One antibody can grip two copies of the same shape": correct (bivalent).
- **Receptor diversity.** Random V(D)J recombination of receptor gene segments during B cell development in the bone marrow, with junctional diversity, generates a very large repertoire (estimates for the human naive repertoire run well beyond millions). An adult has tens of billions of B cells, so Revision 2 says "billions of B cells, with millions of different receptor shapes" (science check finding 3); "millions of shapes" is a conservative lower bound. "Shuffles pieces of its receptor genes at random": fair plain wording.
- **Selection and activation.** B cells bind intact antigen in lymph node follicles (site error 3). For most protein antigens, full activation needs help from a helper T cell that recognises a peptide of the same antigen displayed by the B cell (linked recognition); some antigens (repetitive polysaccharides) activate B cells without T cell help, so "Usually" is correct (site error 4). The helper T cell giving the go-ahead is one first activated by a dendritic cell showing pieces of the same pathogen (b06 scene 10), so "one of the T cells the dendritic cells alerted to this pathogen" is correct (Revision 2). Usually several clones respond to one pathogen (polyclonal response), so "Only the few" is correct (site error 2).
- **Clonal expansion and timing.** Activated B cells divide repeatedly over several days (in germinal centres, roughly every 6 to 12 hours), producing thousands of cells per clone. In a primary response, specific antibody (mainly IgM first) is usually detectable in blood about 7 to 10 days after exposure, peaking at about 2 weeks. "Over several days, making thousands of identical cells" and attempt 2's "about 7 to 10 days": correct. Author note: somatic hypermutation slightly changes receptor shape within a clone during germinal centre reactions (affinity maturation), so "identical" and "same binding shape" are the HSC simplification; not in learner copy because it would not change any answer at this level.
- **Plasma cells.** Terminally differentiated B cells with extensive rough ER and a prominent Golgi; secretion rates commonly cited at about 2,000 antibody molecules per second per cell (estimates vary up to about 10,000). "Can release thousands of antibody molecules every second": correct, hedged. Secreted antibody has the same variable region (binding site) as the selected cell's receptor (class switching changes the stem, not the binding site). Correct. Revision 2: "Many cells in the clone become plasma cells", not "Most", because the share is not fixed (many activated cells enter germinal centres first; science check finding 1). Secreted antibody reaches the blood (from the lymph node it leaves in the lymph, which drains into the blood) and leaves at leaky inflamed vessels, as b06 taught: "The blood carries them to the infection" (learning review finding 1).
- **Memory B cells.** Long-lived B cells from the clone that persist after the infection and respond faster on re-exposure (b09). "Stay in the body long after the infection": correct.
- **Antibody actions.** Neutralisation (blocking adhesins, viral attachment proteins and toxins), agglutination (bivalent cross-linking), opsonisation (phagocyte Fc receptors bind the antibody's Fc stem), complement activation (screen only). "Antibodies don't kill bacteria by themselves": correct (killing is by phagocytes or complement, which antibodies trigger). Correct.
- **Specificity.** Antibodies bind epitopes that fit their binding sites; unrelated bacteria with different antigens usually are not bound (cross-reactivity between similar antigens exists, so "usually isn't bound"). Correct as hedged.
- **Attempt 1.** Agglutination of bacteria by immune serum (the basis of classic serological tests such as the Widal test); serum contains antibodies but no cells; killed bacteria are immunogenic. Two weeks is enough for a primary antibody response in a mouse. Original wording; correct.
- **Attempt 2.** Selection against instruction (site Explain model); 7 to 10 days for first detection (above). Original; correct.
- **No em dashes** in this file (searched).

Follow the splinter's bacteria into a lymph node and see how B cells target an antigen: what an antigen is, the Y-shaped antibody and its two binding sites, why each B cell carries one receptor shape, how the antigen selects the few B cells that fit (clonal selection), how they multiply into a clone of plasma cells and memory cells, and how antibodies block, clump and coat bacteria so phagocytes can remove them. Two exam-style questions with brief feedback.

Biology 2017: Year 12 Module 7, Infectious Disease. Inquiry question: "How does the human immune system respond to exposure to a pathogen?"
