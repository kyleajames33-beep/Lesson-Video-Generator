### 2. one path, two dials, and a prediction

And how big is the difference in water vapour between the air inside and outside? That difference is the water-vapour gradient. Quick prediction: in bright light, do the stomata open, or close?

### 3. light opens the stomata
> They open. Most plants open their stomata in the light so that carbon dioxide can get in for photosynthesis. The guard cells on either side take up water, swell and bow apart, and the pore widens. But an open door works both ways: with more stomata open, more vapour diffuses out. In the dark, most stomata close, and water loss falls sharply. Light speeds up transpiration, mainly by opening the stomata. Losing water is the price the plant pays for carbon dioxide.

### 4. warmth
> Now keep the light the same, and warm the leaf. Two things happen. The water molecules have more energy, so they evaporate from the cell walls faster. And a warmer leaf's air spaces can hold more water vapour, while the air outside holds the same amount as before. So the difference grows, the gradient gets steeper, and vapour diffuses out faster. Warmth speeds up transpiration by speeding evaporation and steepening the gradient.

### 5. wind and the boundary layer, and a prediction

Quick prediction: on a humid day, there's more water in the air around the leaf. Does the plant lose water faster, or slower?

### 6. humid air slows it
> Slower. That surprises a lot of people, because more water around sounds like more water lost. But transpiration is diffusion, and diffusion needs a difference. Humid air already holds a lot of water vapour, so the air outside is nearly as moist as the air inside. The gradient is flatter, and less vapour diffuses out. So humidity is the odd one out: more of it slows transpiration down. More water around means less water leaving.

### 8. midday and midnight
> Now, back to our wilted plant. In the afternoon, every dial pointed up: bright light, heat, wind and dry air. Water left the leaves faster than the roots could replace it, so the cells lost water, went soft, and the plant drooped. At night, the stomata closed, the air cooled and grew more humid, so water loss slowed right down. The roots caught up, the cells refilled and firmed up, and the plant stood again. That's why farmers often water crops in the cool of the evening: less is lost straight back to the air.

### 9. when the plant closes its stomata
> There's one limit. More heat or wind doesn't always mean more water loss. On a very hot, dry, windy day, if the leaves lose water faster than the roots can supply it, the guard cells lose water too, and the stomata close, even in bright light. Closing them cuts water loss, so the rate falls instead of rising. The cost is that carbon dioxide can't get in either, so photosynthesis slows. The plant responds to the weather. It doesn't just suffer it.

### 12. key notes (quiet, about 60 seconds)
Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Transpiration: water vapour diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata. Its rate depends on (1) how open the stomata are and (2) how steep the water-vapour gradient is (the difference in vapour between the air spaces inside and the air outside).
2. Light ↑ rate: stomata open in light (to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis), so more vapour escapes; most close in the dark.
3. Temperature ↑ rate: faster evaporation, and the warmer leaf's air spaces hold more vapour, so the gradient steepens. Wind ↑ rate: it sweeps away the moist boundary layer, keeping the gradient steep.
4. Humidity ↓ rate: moist outside air flattens the gradient. Fastest (with adequate water supply): bright, hot, windy, dry. Slowest: dark, cool, still, humid.
5. Under water stress (loss faster than supply), guard cells lose water and stomata close: the rate falls, and so does photosynthesis.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (2 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Wilting and overnight recovery (1, 8).** Plants commonly wilt in the afternoon on hot, dry, windy days when transpiration exceeds uptake (temporary wilting) and recover overnight as transpiration falls and uptake restores turgor, provided the soil still holds water. Correct; the hook does not claim recovery in dry soil.
- **Stomata and light (3).** Most plants open stomata in light (blue light and photosynthesis signals) and close most of them in the dark; guard cells take up water (after taking up potassium ions) and their uneven walls make them bow apart. CAM plants do the reverse (author note only; not Year 11 scope here). "Water loss falls sharply" in the dark: correct (cuticular loss continues at a low rate).
- **Temperature (4).** Higher leaf temperature raises evaporation rate and the saturation vapour pressure of the leaf's internal air, increasing the vapour pressure difference to outside air of unchanged vapour content. Correct as worded (site error 1).
- **Boundary layer and wind (5).** A layer of still, moist air at the leaf surface adds resistance to vapour diffusion; wind thins it. Correct. Strong wind can also cool leaves and can trigger stomatal closure (9).
- **Humidity (6).** Higher humidity reduces the vapour gradient and the rate. Correct.
- **Stress closure (9).** Under water deficit, guard cells lose turgor (and abscisic acid signals closure), so stomata close and transpiration and carbon dioxide uptake both fall. Correct. The "rate rising then falling with wind speed" sketch is a labelled illustrative shape; confirm it is acceptable as a qualitative sketch.
- **Irrigation (8).** Watering in the evening or overnight reduces evaporative loss; Australian agencies publish evapotranspiration estimates. Correct ("often", not "always").
- **Midday depression (10c, 10d).** A midday dip in transpiration and stomatal conductance on hot, clear days is well documented. The graph is illustrative; temperatures 16 to 34 °C are plausible for a hot Australian summer day.
- **Seedlings (10a).** The model's changes are standard; a loose cover in full sun can overheat plants (named as a weak answer).
