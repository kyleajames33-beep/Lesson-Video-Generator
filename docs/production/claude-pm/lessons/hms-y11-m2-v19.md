### 3. rank by evidence, not by confidence
> Sources don't deserve equal weight, so rank them. At the top is a **systematic review**, which gathers every study on a question by a stated method. Next, a **peer-reviewed** study, checked by other researchers before it's published. Then textbooks and sporting bodies, accurate but often a few years behind. Then coaching magazines and journalism, written to be interesting, so the finding often sounds stronger than the study. And at the bottom, social media and coach opinion. Low doesn't mean worthless. A coach's opinion is weak evidence about what works, but strong evidence about what coaches do. Then ask four questions of any source. Who was studied? How many? Who paid for it? And is this the study, or someone's account of it?

### 4. four ways a finding gets stretched
> Now the four statements. Each one sits on real research, and three have been stretched. A: the real study found the best violinists had averaged about ten thousand hours of **deliberate practice**, effortful work on weaknesses with feedback, by about age twenty. An average became a requirement, and deliberate practice became just hours. C: elite athletes often did specialise early. But the ones who specialised and quit were never counted. That's a **correlation**, two things going together, read as a cause. Studies that follow everyone tend to favour trying several sports. D: visualising does help, alongside real practice, once you have a movement to rehearse. The qualifier got dropped. And the fourth stretch is a result from a narrow **sample**, say twelve elite adults, sold as true for everyone. B is the real finding. It's specific, it admits its own cost, and it's inconvenient: it tells coaches the better session looks worse. Convenient, round-numbered claims deserve the most suspicion. Even so, in real sport settings the effect is smaller than in the lab.

### 5. from a finding to Tuesday evening
> The syllabus asks how research is applied in practice. "Coaches should vary their practice" isn't an application. It's the finding with should added. ### 6. the next question, and the honest gap
> Then propose further research questions, which shows you actually read. Good questions come from four places. The sample: does this hold for fourteen-year-old beginners, not adults? A disagreement: why does mixed practice help in one study and not another? The measure: retention was tested after a day, so does it last a season? And the practice gap: if the evidence is clear, why don't coaches use it? Often it's because it looks worse and is harder to run. And report gaps honestly. If you found little on your skill, or a claim you couldn't trace to any study, say so. That's a finding too.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **10,000 hours (4).** Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993): the best violin students' estimated accumulated deliberate practice of about 10,000 hours by about age 20, an average with wide individual variation; "deliberate practice" as defined; the popular "10,000-hour rule" as a later distortion.
- **Specialisation (4).** Studies of elite athletes alone omit those who specialised early and dropped out (survivorship); cohort and review evidence generally associates early diversification with equal or better long-term outcomes and lower dropout and overuse injury, with exceptions in early-peaking sports (for example gymnastics). Check "tend to favour trying several sports" is fair.
- **Mental practice (4).** Improves performance more than no practice and less than physical practice, best combined with it and with an existing movement pattern.
- **Contextual interference (4, 5).** Shea and Morgan (1979) onward; effects smaller and less consistent in applied sport settings than in laboratory tasks (check the wording "smaller than in the lab").
- **Sources (3).** Systematic review and peer review as defined; the five-level hierarchy (site's working hierarchy, not a formal standard).
