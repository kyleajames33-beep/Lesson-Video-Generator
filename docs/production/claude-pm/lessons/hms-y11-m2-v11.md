### 2. three fuels, three jobs
> Nutrition is the supply chain for those fuels. The three **macronutrients**, the nutrients we eat in large amounts for energy, do very different jobs. Carbohydrate has about seventeen kilojoules per gram, in a small store, as glycogen. It's the only fuel that can be broken down without oxygen, so it feeds glycolysis and the aerobic system. Fat has about thirty-seven, in a huge store, but it only feeds the aerobic system. Protein has about seventeen too, but no fuel store. Its real job is building and repairing muscle, enzymes and haemoglobin, and it adds only a small share of exercise energy.

### 3. intensity decides the mix
> From last time, intensity decides the mix. At rest, fat supplies most of the energy. At about sixty-five per cent of maximum, the two are roughly equal. That's the **crossover**. Above about eighty-five per cent, carbohydrate supplies almost all of it. Now the puzzle. The runner spends long stretches above the crossover, so most of their energy comes from a store that lasts about ninety minutes. Carbohydrate decides whether they finish the session. The shot putter's efforts last seconds, run on phosphocreatine, and cost little in total. Their priority is protein, to build muscle. So the same meals can't suit both, and it's the runner who's short-changed.

### 4. per kilogram

Three to five, times sixty, is one hundred and eighty to three hundred grams. A ninety-five-kilogram rower in heavy training: six to ten, times ninety-five, is five hundred and seventy to nine hundred and fifty grams. On the same diet, the netballer is covered, and the rower gets about a third of what they need.

### 5. a ceiling and a floor
> Protein rises from about one point two grams per kilogram to about two in heavy training. But two is roughly a ceiling. Beyond it, the extra isn't stored as muscle. It's burned, or stored as fat. The training is the signal to grow, and protein is the material. Fat works the other way. It has a floor, about twenty to thirty-five per cent of energy, and rarely below twenty. Too little, and the body can't absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, or make some hormones well. Very low intakes can also lead to low energy availability, which you met earlier.

### 6. iron: no energy, but a limit removed
> Now the iron puzzle. **Micronutrients**, the vitamins and minerals we need in tiny amounts, carry no energy. They're part of the machinery that releases it. Iron sits at the centre of haemoglobin, and of myoglobin in muscle. It's what oxygen binds to. Without enough iron, less oxygen reaches the mitochondria, so the aerobic system is capped lower. At the same pace, more energy has to come from the anaerobic systems, and fatigue comes sooner. So iron doesn't give energy. It removes a limit. But only if you're short of it. Extra iron gives a well-stocked athlete nothing, and too much does harm. A blood test decides, not a label.

### 8. water

In team sport, the first sign is often a skill error. What to drink, and when, comes later.

## Numbers or facts to verify

Self-check (24 Nov). The Codex science check must confirm each before voicing.
- **Macronutrients (2).** Carbohydrate and protein about 17 kJ/g, fat about 37 kJ/g; carbohydrate the only anaerobic fuel; fat aerobic only; protein mainly structural with a small exercise-energy contribution.
- **Fuel mix (3).** Crossover near 60 to 65% of maximum; carbohydrate nearly all above about 85%; glycogen for about 90 min to 2 h of hard work; throwers' nutritional priority on protein and total energy.
- **Requirements (4, 5).** Carbohydrate 3 to 5 g/kg/day (light) and 6 to 10 g/kg/day (heavy endurance) (ACSM, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and Dietitians of Canada joint position stand, 2016; AIS guidance); protein 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day; benefit plateau near 2 g/kg; fat 20 to 35% of energy; fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K; low energy availability.
- **Micronutrients (6, 7).** Iron in haemoglobin and myoglobin; iron deficiency impairs endurance; supplementation helps only when deficient, excess harmful; B vitamins as coenzymes; calcium in contraction and bone, low intake affecting bone rather than contraction (site correction 20); vitamin D and calcium absorption; vitamin C and non-haem iron absorption; vitamins C and E as antioxidants (author note: high-dose antioxidant supplements may blunt some training adaptations; not learner copy); sodium and potassium.
- **Water (8).** Sweat reduces plasma volume; stroke volume falls and heart rate rises (cardiovascular drift); skin blood flow and heat loss; about 2% body mass loss impairs endurance and cognitive performance.
