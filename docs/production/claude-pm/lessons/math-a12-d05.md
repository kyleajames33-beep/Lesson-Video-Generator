### Revision 3 redesign (7 Nov 2026; these bullets override older bullets below where they conflict)

- **Anchor: a Ferris wheel seat (painted plates, live).** A Ferris wheel turns steadily. Measure heights in units of its radius and start a seat level with the hub, on the right, going up. After the wheel turns x radians the seat's height above the hub is sin x. This is not decoration: the wheel is the unit circle the sine curve comes from, and every step of the lesson has a picture on it.
  - **height = sin x**: the seat's height above the hub;
  - **gradient = how fast the seat climbs** per radian turned (the wheel turns steadily, so this is proportional to its climbing speed): fastest as the seat passes the hub's height, zero for an instant at the top and the bottom. The traced gradient graph is the seat's climbing rate;
  - **why radians (scene 5)**: in radians, the seat's angle turned equals the distance it has travelled along the rim (arc = x, radius 1). Near the start it is moving straight up, so its height grows as fast as that distance: slope 1;
  - **why the minus in cos′ (scene 7, screen tag)**: cos x is the seat's distance to the right of the hub (and the height of a seat that started a quarter turn earlier, at the top). When the seat is above the hub it is moving left, so cos x is falling: −sin x.
  The anchor appears in scene 1 (narrated), runs as a live thumbnail in scenes 2 to 4, becomes the unit circle in scene 5, gives the cos x picture in scenes 6 and 7 (tags), and its answer is narrated in scene 3, where the landmarks are read ("on the wheel, the seat moves up or down fastest as it passes the hub's height, and for an instant it isn't climbing at all, at the top and the bottom").
- **Why this anchor.** The Ferris wheel is the most natural everyday picture of sine (owner suggestion list), it explains radians and the slope 1 at the origin directly, and it differs from d02 (money), d03 (app users) and d04 (music and sound). No biology. - **Learner question kept (route).** "How do the sine and cosine graphs reveal their derivatives?" The answer, built in order: read the tangent slope of y = sin x at its landmarks (crossings steepest, peaks and troughs flat), plot those slopes as a gradient graph, and recognise it as y = cos x; the same for y = cos x gives the sine curve upside down, −sin x. Two causes explain the details: the slope at 0 is exactly 1 because, in radians, sin x is almost equal to the arc length x for a small angle; and the minus sign in cos′ follows because cos x is sin x slid left by π/2, and a slide keeps every slope.

The positive check: just after 0, cos x falls while sin x is positive, so "+sin x" would give the wrong sign (crossed chip, small).

- **Author notes (not learner copy):**
  (a) The chords in scene 2 have gradient (sin h − 0)/(h − 0) = (sin h)/h: 0.958851 (h = ½), 0.998334 (h = 0.1), 0.999983 (h = 0.01). The limit (sin h)/h → 1 as h → 0 (h in radians) is the slope of sin x at 0. The video shows this graphically and with the unit-circle picture (sin h < h < tan h for 0 < h < π/2 is the standard proof, not used). ### 5. why radians

So the slope at zero would be only about point zero one seven **five**. The rule only works with x in **radians**.
