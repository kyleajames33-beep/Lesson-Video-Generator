## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "How do the sine and cosine graphs reveal their derivatives?" The answer, built in order: read the tangent slope of y = sin x at its landmarks (crossings steepest, peaks and troughs flat), plot those slopes as a gradient graph, and recognise it as y = cos x; the same for y = cos x gives the sine curve upside down, −sin x. Two causes explain the details: the slope at 0 is exactly 1 because, in radians, sin x is almost equal to the arc length x for a small angle; and the minus sign in cos′ follows because cos x is sin x slid left by π/2, and a slide keeps every slope.
- **Cause before rule (owner quality bar, d01 to d04 template).** The slope at 0 is measured with chords first (2), the landmarks are read from the shape and its symmetry (3), the gradient graph is traced and checked at π/3 (4), and only then is the rule named. The site states the three rules first (card 02) and reads the graph afterwards (card 05); this video reverses that order.
- **The hook surprise (the d04 handoff, extended).** d04 ended on y = sin x with a tangent at the origin and "gradient ?". The hook draws the line y = x through the origin: the tangent lies along it, so the slope looks like exactly 1. - **Landmarks from the shape.** π/2 and 3π/2: flat (peak and trough), slope 0. π: by the symmetry of the curve about its peak (sin(π − x) = sin x), it falls through π as steeply as it rose through 0, slope −1. 2π: the wave repeats, slope 1. The narration gives each cause in a clause.

- **Why radians (route start "with x in radians"; pack misconception 1).** A small unit-circle inset shows an angle of 0.3 radians: the arc it cuts off has length 0.3 (the meaning of a radian, recalled), and the height sin 0.3 ≈ 0.296 is almost the same. - **Cosine's gradient graph.** The same tracer on y = cos x: slope 0 at 0 (peak), −1 at π/2 (steepest fall), 0 at π (trough), 1 at 3π/2 (steepest rise), 0 at 2π. The trace is the sine curve upside down; a dashed −sin x lands on it.
- **Why the minus sign (graph cause; site key term "cosine starts flat and initially decreases").** cos x = sin(x + π/2): the cosine curve is the sine curve slid left by π/2 (Year 11 graph; shown by `transform.xShift` 0 → −π/2 with a dashed sine ghost). A slide moves each tangent along with the curve and keeps its slope, so the gradient graph slides by the same amount: cos x's gradient graph is cos x slid left by π/2, which lands on the upside-down sine curve. The positive check: just after 0, cos x falls while sin x is positive, so "+sin x" would give the wrong sign (crossed chip, small).

### 2. measure: the slope at zero
> Check that first slope properly. One point can't give a slope on its own, so, as in Year 11, take a second point on the curve, just to the right of zero, and find the gradient of the chord. With the point at x equals a half, it's about point nine **six**. At point one, about point nine nine **eight**. Bring it closer still, and the gradient settles on exactly **one**. So, with x in radians, the sine curve leaves the origin with slope one.

### 3. the landmarks: flat at peaks, steepest at crossings
> Now read the slope at the other landmarks. At pi over two, the peak, the curve is flat, so the slope is **zero**. And at two pi, the wave starts over, climbing with slope **one**. Peaks and troughs are flat, and the crossings are the **steepest**.

### 5. why radians
> Why do radians matter? On a circle of radius one, an angle of x radians cuts off an arc of length **x**. That's what a radian means. For a small angle, the height, sine x, is almost the same as that arc. So near zero, sine x is close to x, and the slope there is **one**. In degrees, the same graph gets stretched sideways by about fifty seven point **three**. A sideways stretch divides every slope, at matching points, by that number. So the slope at zero would be only about point zero one seven **five**. The rule only works with x in **radians**.
