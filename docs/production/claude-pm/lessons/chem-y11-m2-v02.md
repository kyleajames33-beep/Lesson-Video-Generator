### 1. hook: too many to count
> Last time, we balanced two M g plus O two makes two M g O. So two magnesium atoms react with one oxygen molecule. But this zero point two four of a gram of ribbon holds thousands of billions of billions of magnesium **atoms**. Nobody can count that one at a time. And one carbon atom has a mass of about two times ten to the minus twenty-three grams, far too small for any **balance**. So how do chemists count particles they can't even **see**?

### 3. the mole and the Avogadro constant
> One mole is six point zero two two times ten to the twenty-three particles. That number is called the Avogadro constant, after the Italian scientist Amedeo **Avogadro**. It's written N A, and its unit is per mole, because it tells you how many particles are in each **mole**. When we count a substance in moles, that's its chemical amount. Its symbol is a small n, and its unit is the mole, written m o **l**.

### 6. N and n

The number of particles equals the moles times N **A**. And going back, the moles equal the number of particles divided by N A.

### 10. worked example: atoms to moles

### 11. always name the particle
> Always say what you're counting, because one particle can contain others. Half a mole of carbon dioxide is three point zero one times ten to the twenty-three **molecules**. But each molecule has two oxygen atoms, so there are twice as many oxygen atoms. That's six point zero two two times ten to the twenty-three, a whole mole of oxygen **atoms**. Ionic compounds don't form molecules, so we count formula units, the simplest ratio of ions in the **formula**. One mole of sodium chloride holds one mole of sodium ions and one mole of chloride ions, two moles of ions in **total**.
