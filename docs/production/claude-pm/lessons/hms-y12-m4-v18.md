### 2. recovery has two jobs
> Training works by disturbing the body, then letting it rebuild stronger, as you've seen. So recovery isn't just wiping out the effects of training. It has two jobs, and they can pull apart. One is restoring **readiness**: getting the athlete able to perform well again, soon. That matters most in tournaments, congested fixtures and the season. The other is allowing adaptation: letting the rebuild the training started run its course. That matters most in pre-season and development blocks, where the session's point was to change the athlete. Here's the catch. A strategy that speeds up readiness by damping the body's response to training may also shrink the adaptation that response was going to produce. And underneath both jobs sit the foundations from earlier: sleep, food and fluid, the biggest effects of all.

### 3. physiological strategies: cool-down and hydrotherapy
> The syllabus names two physiological strategies. The **cool-down** you met earlier: light activity keeps the muscle pump going so blood doesn't pool, helps clear by-products, and returns the body to rest gradually, though it doesn't prevent next-day soreness. **Active recovery** the next day, light aerobic work, keeps blood flowing without adding load, as long as it stays easy. Then **hydrotherapy**, using water. Cold-water immersion narrows blood vessels and cools tissue, limiting swelling and inflammation. The evidence fairly consistently shows less soreness and fatigue over the next day or two. But used regularly after resistance training, it appears to reduce the gains the training produces. Hot water raises blood flow and relaxes, but not on a fresh injury, where it adds swelling. Alternating hot and cold makes athletes feel better, but the evidence beyond that is weaker. And remember, feeling better isn't the same as recovering better.

### 4. psychological recovery, including relaxation
> Fatigue isn't only in the muscles. After a hard week, an athlete can still produce force and perform badly, because attention, motivation and decision-making are tired too. So the syllabus names psychological recovery. **Relaxation**, slow breathing and progressive muscle relaxation, lowers arousal, heart rate and tension. Earlier it helped before competition. Here, the same tool helps after it, especially after an evening game, when high arousal is the most common reason athletes sleep badly. Mindfulness trains attention to stop replaying a loss or an error. **Psychological detachment** means deliberately switching off from sport between sessions, so rest actually restores. And a short debrief soon after the game, then closing it, stops the athlete carrying an unfinished performance into the night.

### 5. justify, don't describe
> The verb is **justify**, and that means arguing this strategy suits this athlete now. It has four parts. Purpose: what is this athlete trying to achieve right now? Mechanism: how would the strategy achieve it? Evidence: how well supported is that, and what was actually measured? And cost and alternative: what does it cost, and what else could that time do? Take the footballer. Purpose: be ready in thirty-six hours, accepting some cost to adaptation. Mechanism: cold limits swelling and soreness. Evidence: fairly consistent for soreness, less certain for performance. Cost: it's uncomfortable and needs facilities, and an extra hour of sleep is free and does more. So it's justified for a congested week. Now the lifter. The purpose is adaptation, and cold blunts it, so the same strategy isn't justified. That's the hook.

### 6. the recovery hierarchy
> So recovery is a hierarchy, not a menu. Add strategies in order of how big their effect is and how little they cost. At the base is sleep, the largest effect available, and it's free. Nothing above it makes up for it missing. Next, food and rehydration after the session, cheap and powerful, and more urgent the sooner the next effort comes. Then managing the training load itself. Only then come the strategies in this video: cool-downs and active recovery, psychological strategies, and hydrotherapy near the top, useful in the right week, but small and costly next to the base. An athlete sleeping six hours who buys an ice bath has built the pyramid upside down.
