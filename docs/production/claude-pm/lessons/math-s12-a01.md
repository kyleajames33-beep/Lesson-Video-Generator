### 1. hook: two driving schools
> Say you've got your learner licence, and you need driving lessons. Two driving schools send you quotes. School A charges a ninety dollar booking fee, then sixty-five dollars a lesson. School B has no booking fee, but charges eighty dollars a lesson. So which one is cheaper? A has the cheaper lessons, but B has nothing to pay up front. The honest answer is, it **depends** on how many lessons you'll need. Draw both prices as lines on one graph, and where they cross tells us when the two schools cost the same. So how do two lines on a graph solve a pair of equations at **once**?

### 3. two lines, one crossing

### 4. why the crossing solves both
> Here's why the crossing matters. Every point on a line makes that line's equation true. So a point on School A's line fits A's equation, and a point on B's line fits B's equation. The crossing is the only point on both lines, so it's the only pair that makes both equations true at the same **time**. That's what simultaneous equations means, two equations that have to be true together. And the solution is a pair of numbers, not one number. Here, n equals six and C equals four hundred and eighty. Check it in both. Ninety plus sixty-five times six is four hundred and **eighty**. Eighty times six is four hundred and eighty too. Both are true, so the crossing is the **solution**.

### 5. worked example: tables, graph, check
> Here's the method on its own. Solve y equals two x minus one, and y equals minus x plus five, graphically. Step one, a small table for each line. For y equals two x minus one, x equals zero, one and two give minus one, one and **three**. For y equals minus x plus five, x equals zero, two and four give five, three and **one**. Step two, **plot** at least two points for each line, and **rule** a line through them. Step three, read where they cross. They meet at two, **three**, so x equals two and y equals three. Step four, check in both equations. Two times two, minus one, is three. Minus two, plus five, is three. Both **work**. Checking in only one equation is the slip. A point can sit on one line, and miss the **other**.

### 7. no crossing, or every point
> Do two lines always cross exactly once? Not quite. If two lines have the same gradient but different y-intercepts, like y equals two minus x, and y equals three minus x, they're parallel. They never meet, so there's no **solution**. And if two equations describe the same line, they share every point. Take x plus y equals two, and two x plus two y equals four. The second is just the first, doubled, so every point on the line works, and there are infinitely many **solutions**.
