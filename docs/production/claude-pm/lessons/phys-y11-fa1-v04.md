### 2. distance: every leg counts
> Distance is the easy one. Every metre of path counts, whichever way it goes, so you just add up every leg. A leg is one straight part of the trip. Eight kilometres, then six, then three, in any directions you like, makes seventeen kilometres. Turning back doesn't take any **off**. Distance can only **grow**.

Visual: board clears to a plan view with three legs pointing in different directions (a zigzag; no compass values needed). ### 3. along one line: let the signs cancel
> Displacement is different. It only cares where you end up, so legs in opposite directions should cancel. A sign convention does that for us. We choose one direction as positive, and the opposite one counts as negative. A car drives twelve kilometres east, then turns around and drives five kilometres west. First, write down the **convention**: east is positive. The distance is twelve plus five, seventeen kilometres. The displacement is plus twelve, plus minus five, which is plus seven **kilometres**. And plus seven means seven kilometres east. It's smaller than the distance because the car turned **back**.

### 6. two directions: total each one separately
> Now journeys that turn corners. Here's the key idea. East and west legs can cancel each other. North and south legs can cancel each other. But an east leg can't cancel a north leg, because they're at right angles. That's last video's point: perpendicular parts act **independently**. So we total each direction on its own, with signs. Then we combine the two totals, which meet at a right **angle**.

Visual: board clears to a plan view with compass. ### 7. worked example: the totals

North-south: plus three, minus five, so minus two point zero, two kilometres **south**. Four legs, but only two totals matter.

### 8. worked example: combine and name the direction
> Now combine them. Four kilometres east and two kilometres south meet at a right angle, so the displacement is the square root of four squared plus two squared, the square root of twenty, about four point five **kilometres**. For the direction, tan inverse of two over four is twenty-seven degrees. Read the way from the drawing: it points east, tilted toward the south, so it's twenty-seven degrees south of **east**. That's four point five kilometres, against a distance of sixteen. If a leg ever points at an angle, resolve it into east and north parts first, then total.

### 9. subtracting: final position minus initial position
> One more tool. Sometimes you're given positions, measured from a reference point, and you want the displacement between them. A drone's checkpoint A is three point zero metres east and four point zero north of its launch pad. Checkpoint B is seven point zero east and one point zero north. The displacement from A to B is B's position minus A's **position**. East: seven minus three, four point zero metres east. North: one minus four, minus three point zero, so three metres **south**. That's five point zero metres, at thirty-seven degrees south of east. And it's the same arrow you get by flipping A's position and adding it to B's, just like last time. Whatever route the drone takes from A to B, only the two end points **matter**.

Heading on screen: "Key notes: what to write in your book".
> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Total distance: add the length of every leg (no signs). Net displacement: the single arrow from start to finish. Size of displacement ≤ distance.
2. One line: write a sign convention first (for example, east = +), add the signed legs, then state the direction in words (−3.0 km means 3.0 km west if east is +).
3. Two dimensions: total each direction separately with signs, then combine: |s| = √(s_x² + s_y²), θ = tan⁻¹(|s_y| / |s_x|), and name the direction from the drawing (for example, 27° south of east). Resolve any angled leg first.

## Teaching checks

- **Does it explain causes?** Why distance adds every leg: every metre of path counts (2). Why opposite legs cancel in displacement: only the end position matters (1, 3). Why a sign convention: it turns opposite directions into numbers that cancel (3). Why each direction is totalled separately: perpendicular legs can't cancel each other (6). Why Pythagoras and tan⁻¹ at the end: the two totals are at right angles (8). Why final minus initial: the change in position is what you add to the first position to reach the second (9, v02).
