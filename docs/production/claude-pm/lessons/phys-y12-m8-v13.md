## Decisions

- **Learner question (route wording kept).** "Why do orbital probabilities replace literal electron paths?" The answer, built in order: Bohr's paths failed beyond hydrogen and clashed with the electron's wave behaviour (2); Schrödinger treated the electron fully as a three-dimensional wave, so only certain standing-wave patterns fit, each with its own energy (3, 4); the wave gives the probability of finding the electron at each point, not a position (5); measurements find single points, which build a cloud (6); the model gives no path at all (7).
- **Hook (v12's handoff).** The dots on v12's screen: each electron lands in one place, the pattern is wave-like. "Bohr drew a neat circle, at a definite radius."
- **Bohr's two problems (site "Assess 6 marks" step 1).** (1) It only worked for hydrogen; helium's spectrum is predicted wrongly. (2) It mixed a particle's path with a wave's rules (v12's standing waves on a sharp circle).
- **Schrödinger's equation (site "replace-orbits-with-orbitals").** Named and described ("an equation for how an electron's wave behaves around a nucleus, in three dimensions"); Ĥψ = Eψ is not shown (site Core: "no operator or equation detail is needed").
- **Guitar analogy (Year 11 standing waves; reused plate).** A string fixed at both ends only sustains standing waves that fit, so it plays particular notes; the electron's wave is held in by the nucleus's pull, so only certain three-dimensional standing waves fit, so only certain energies. Limit (author note a): the atom has no fixed ends; the "fit" is that the wave must die away far from the nucleus. The narration says "held in by the nucleus's pull", not "fixed ends".
- **Probability (site "replace-orbits-with-orbitals"; Born named in words).** "The square of the wave's size, at each point, gives the probability per unit volume of finding the electron there." The symbol |ψ|² appears on screen once as a label, not used in working.
- **Orbital from many detections (new picture; v12's dot build-up reused).** Each measurement on an identical atom finds one point; thousands make the 1s cloud. "It isn't a blurry photo of a moving electron." The kit's 1s density slice (`m8-quantum-atom`) is shown after the dots, labelled "probability per volume (slice)", within its stated limits.
- **No path (site "separate-orbits-from-orbitals" misconception).** "The model doesn't give a path at all." Uncertainty is not taught (route stop; open decision 1).
- **Shapes (site quantum numbers kept qualitative).** 1s sphere, 2s larger sphere, three 2p dumbbells along three directions; "help explain how atoms bond in chemistry". Letters s and p are named only as labels; no quantum numbers or capacities.
- **Contrast (site Figure 2, Q2(a), "Assess 6 marks" step 3).** Four rows: shape (flat circle, three-dimensional region), position (definite place at every moment, only a chance at each point), radius (one exact radius, no exact radius), quantisation (assumed, comes out of the wave equation).
- **Memory aid (scene 10): "An orbit is where the electron goes. An orbital is where it's likely to be: the extra 'al' is for all the places it might be."** See "Memory aid check".
- **Assessment (site "Assess 6 marks" steps 4 to 6).** Strengths: quantisation from one equation; line brightness predicted (Bohr's model could not); the same method for many-electron atoms, the periodic table and bonding. Limits: no picture of motion; exact solutions only for hydrogen (author note b); the meaning of the wave still debated. Verdict: "a new foundation, not a small fix" (site "foundational rather than incremental").
- **Attempts (original wording, HSC style).** A1 (2 marks): distinguish a Bohr orbit and an orbital. Crossed: "an orbital is the fuzzy path the electron travels along". A2 (4 marks): assess Schrödinger's contribution. Crossed: "Schrödinger worked out the electron's exact path".

- **Cause chain taught, in order:** (1) Bohr's model failed beyond hydrogen and put a wave-like electron on a sharp path (2); (2) treating the electron as a three-dimensional wave held in by the nucleus allows only certain standing-wave patterns, each with one energy, so energy quantisation follows (3, 4); (3) the wave's size squared gives the probability of finding the electron at each point (5); (4) single measurements find single points, which build up the orbital (6); (5) the model gives probabilities, not a path (7); (6) the same method gives shapes and works for many-electron atoms (8, 11).

### 2. Bohr's two problems
> First, Bohr's model only worked for hydrogen. Add a second electron, as in helium, and its predictions go wrong. Second, an electron behaves like a wave, and a wave isn't at one point. ### 5. what the wave means
> But what does the wave actually tell us? Max Born gave the answer that stuck. Where the wave is large, you're likely to find the electron. Where it's small, you rarely will. More exactly, the square of the wave's size at each point gives the probability per unit volume of finding the electron **there**. So where the wave is twice as big, the electron is four times as likely to be **found**.

### 9. orbit against orbital
> Let's line up the two pictures. A Bohr orbit is a flat circle at one exact radius, with the electron at a definite place at every moment. In Schrödinger's model, it comes out of the wave **equation**. The energy levels are the same, so everything you learned about hydrogen's spectrum still **holds**.

And the same method works for atoms with many electrons, which explains the layout of the periodic table, and chemical **bonding**.

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Bohr's model: definite circular orbits, quantisation assumed; worked for hydrogen only.
2. Schrödinger (1926): treated the electron as a 3D wave; a wave equation whose allowed solutions (standing waves that fit) each have one energy. Hydrogen: E_n = −13.6/n² eV, derived, not assumed.
3. The square of the wave's size (|ψ|²) gives the probability per unit volume of finding the electron at each point. An orbital is a 3D region showing where the electron is likely to be found. No path.
4. Orbit: 2D, one exact radius, definite position. Orbital: 3D, no exact radius, probability only.
5. Assessment: foundational (quantisation derived; predicts line brightness; works for many-electron atoms; orbital shapes explain bonding). Limits: no picture of motion; exact only for hydrogen; meaning of the wave debated.

Memory aid (large, on the notes screen): *An orbit is where the electron goes. An orbital is where it's likely to be: "al" for all the places it might be.*

## Practice questions and model answers

**Attempt 1 (scene 13; original wording, HSC style, 2 marks suggested, not an official marking scheme).** "Distinguish between an electron orbit in Bohr's model of the atom and an orbital in Schrödinger's model."
Model answer: A Bohr orbit is a definite, two-dimensional circular path at a fixed radius, with the electron at a definite position at every instant. An orbital is a three-dimensional region of space describing the probability of finding the electron at each point; it has no exact radius and specifies no path. Classic mistake (crossed): "an orbital is the fuzzy path the electron travels".

**Attempt 2 (scene 15; original wording, HSC style, 4 marks).** "Assess the contribution of Schrödinger to the current model of the atom."
Model answer: Bohr's model assumed quantised circular orbits and worked quantitatively only for hydrogen. Schrödinger treated the electron as a three-dimensional matter wave and formulated a wave equation whose allowed solutions give the atom's energy levels (for hydrogen −13.6/n² eV) without assuming them, and describe orbitals: regions giving the probability of finding the electron, not paths. The method extends to many-electron atoms and underpins the periodic table and chemical bonding. Its limits: no visualisable picture of the electron's motion; exact solutions only for hydrogen, so approximations are needed; the meaning of the wave is still debated. Overall the contribution is foundational: it is the basis of the current model. Classic mistake (crossed): "Schrödinger worked out the electron's exact path".
