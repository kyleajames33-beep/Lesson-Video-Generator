### 2. combustion: every element ends up with oxygen
> Burning has a chemistry name, combustion. It's a reaction between a fuel and oxygen that releases energy as heat and light. Burner gas is natural gas, which is mostly methane, C H four, a hydrocarbon, made of only carbon and hydrogen. Here's the key to predicting the products. Every element in the fuel ends up joined to oxygen. The carbon becomes an oxide of carbon, and the hydrogen becomes water, which is an oxide of **hydrogen**.

Visual: heading "Combustion"; gloss chips "combustion: a fuel reacts with oxygen, releasing heat and light" and "hydrocarbon: only carbon and hydrogen". ### 3. complete combustion, and the tests
> When there's plenty of oxygen, every carbon atom gets two oxygen atoms, and becomes carbon dioxide. Every pair of hydrogen atoms gets one oxygen atom, and becomes water. That's complete combustion, and it's what the blue flame is doing. You can test the products. The mist on the cold dish is water, and the gas turns limewater **milky**, so it's carbon dioxide.

Visual: the bins fill: C plus two O tiles → "CO₂"; two H plus one O → "H₂O"; gloss chip "complete combustion: enough oxygen for all the carbon to become CO₂" (teal). The clean basin with its mist pulses (tag "water"). `m6-limewater-clear-v1.png` appears small; on "milky" it cross-fades to `m6-limewater-milky-v1.png` (chip "CO₂: limewater turns milky", from m3-v02).

### 4. balance methane: oxygen last
> To balance it, do carbon first, then hydrogen, and leave oxygen until last. Oxygen gas is an element on its own, so changing its number doesn't upset anything else. One carbon gives one carbon dioxide. Four hydrogens give two waters. Now count the oxygen on the right: two in carbon dioxide, and two in the waters, makes four. That's two oxygen **molecules**. And because water leaves a flame as steam, it's written as a gas.

Visual: line "CH₄(g) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(g)" with atom tiles; the order strip "C → H → O last" writes above. C tally green at once; teal "2" before H₂O, H tally green; the right-hand O tiles count up "2 + 2 = 4"; on "molecules" teal "2" before O₂ and the full tally turns green: "CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g)". ### 5. prediction (countdown, 3 seconds)

### 6. half a molecule, so double

### 7. close the air hole
> Now close the air hole. Less air mixes with the gas, so there isn't enough oxygen to give every carbon atom two. Some carbon atoms get only one, and become carbon monoxide, C **O**. Some get none at all, and stay as tiny specks of carbon, which is soot. Those hot specks glow, and that's what makes the flame **yellow**. Burning without enough oxygen is called incomplete combustion. The hydrogen still ends up as water.

### 8. the equations, and a way to remember
> The equations follow the same counting. With some oxygen missing, two methane plus three oxygen gives two carbon monoxide plus four water. With even less, methane plus one oxygen gives soot plus two water. The less oxygen there is for each methane, the further down the ladder the carbon goes. So here's the way to remember it. Plenty of oxygen, carbon takes two. Less oxygen, it takes one. Least, it takes **none**.

### 9. why it matters
> This matters. Carbon monoxide is a poisonous gas with no colour and no smell, so you can't tell it's there. Soot dirties surfaces and damages lungs, and incomplete burning wastes energy too. That's why gas heaters need good ventilation, and homes use carbon monoxide alarms. In the lab, combustion is done with your teacher supervising, safety glasses on, long hair tied back, in a well-ventilated room, and the Bunsen goes back to the yellow safety flame whenever you're not **heating**.

### 13. feedback 1 (brief)
> Five carbons give five carbon dioxides. Twelve hydrogens give six waters. That's ten plus six, sixteen oxygen atoms, so eight oxygen molecules. C five H twelve plus eight O two gives five C O two plus six H two O, all gases. Writing sixteen O two counts atoms, not **molecules**.

### 17. key notes (quiet, hold about 60 seconds)

> Here are the key notes. Pause the video if you need more time to copy them.

1. Combustion: a fuel reacts with oxygen, releasing heat and light. Every element in the fuel ends up as its oxide.
2. Complete (plenty of O₂, blue flame): hydrocarbon → CO₂ + H₂O. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Balance C, then H, then O last; double to clear a half.
