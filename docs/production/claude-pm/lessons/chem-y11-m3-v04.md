### 1. hook: two flames, two dishes
> Here's the same burner gas, burning two ways. With the air hole open, the flame is blue and hard to see. Close the air hole, and you get a tall yellow flame. Now hold a cold white dish over each one for a few seconds. Over the blue flame, the dish just mists up with a little water. Over the yellow flame, its underside turns **black**. That black is soot, which is carbon. Same fuel, different **products**. So what changed?

### 2. combustion: what the carbon and hydrogen become
> Burning has a chemistry name, combustion. It's a reaction between a fuel and oxygen that releases energy as heat and light. Burner gas is natural gas, which is mostly methane, C H four, a hydrocarbon, made of only carbon and hydrogen. Here's the key to predicting the products. When a hydrocarbon burns, its carbon takes oxygen from the air, as much as the supply allows, and its hydrogen becomes water, an oxide of **hydrogen**.

### 3. complete combustion, and the tests
> When there's plenty of oxygen, every carbon atom gets two oxygen atoms, and becomes carbon dioxide. Every pair of hydrogen atoms gets one oxygen atom, and becomes water. That's complete combustion, and it's what the blue flame is doing. You can test the products. The mist on the cold dish is water, and the gas turns limewater **milky**, so it's carbon dioxide.

Visual: the bins fill: C plus two O tiles → "CO₂"; two H plus one O → "H₂O"; gloss chip "complete combustion: enough oxygen for all the carbon to become CO₂" (teal). ### 4. balance methane: oxygen last
> To balance it, do carbon first, then hydrogen, and leave oxygen until last. Oxygen gas is an element on its own, so changing its number doesn't upset anything else. One carbon gives one carbon dioxide. Four hydrogens give two waters. Now count the oxygen on the right: two in carbon dioxide, and two in the waters, makes four. That's two oxygen **molecules**. And because water leaves a flame as steam, it's written as a gas.

### 5. prediction (countdown, 3 seconds)
> Quick prediction. Butane, C four H ten, the gas in camping stoves. Carbon gives four carbon dioxides, and hydrogen gives five waters. That's thirteen oxygen atoms on the right. How many oxygen molecules is **that**?

### 6. half a molecule, so double
> Six and a half, because thirteen oxygen atoms make six and a half pairs. But half a molecule can't react, so double everything. Two butane plus thirteen oxygen gives eight carbon dioxide plus ten **water**. Check: eight carbon, twenty hydrogen and twenty-six oxygen on each **side**.

### 7. close the air hole
> Now close the air hole. Less air mixes with the gas, so there isn't enough oxygen to give every carbon atom two. Some carbon atoms get only one, and become carbon monoxide, C **O**. Some get none at all, and stay as tiny specks of carbon, which is soot. Those hot specks glow, and that's what makes the flame **yellow**. Burning without enough oxygen is called incomplete combustion. The hydrogen still ends up as water.

### 8. the equations, and a way to remember
> The equations follow the same counting. With some oxygen missing, two methane plus three oxygen gives two carbon monoxide plus four water. With even less, methane plus one oxygen gives soot plus two water. The less oxygen there is for each methane, the further down the ladder the carbon goes. So here's the way to remember it. Plenty of oxygen, carbon takes two. Less oxygen, it takes one. Least, it takes **none**.

### 9. why it matters
> This matters. Carbon monoxide is a poisonous gas with no colour and no smell, so you can't tell it's there. Soot dirties surfaces, and incomplete burning wastes energy too. That's why gas heaters need good ventilation, and homes use carbon monoxide alarms.
