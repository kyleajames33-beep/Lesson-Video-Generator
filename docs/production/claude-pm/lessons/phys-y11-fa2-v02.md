## Decisions

- **Learner question kept (route).** "How can a rope and spring model particle motion and energy transfer?" The answer, built in order: a claim needs measured evidence (1); the question and a hypothesis with a reason (2); the spring models both wave types and the rope models transverse waves, so each is chosen for what it shows (3); the variables (4); a fixed camera above a scale records the marked coil's position frame by frame (5); the transverse run's trace goes across and comes back (6); the longitudinal trace goes along and comes back (7, 8); in every run the largest motion lines up with the source's motion, and the final position matches home within the reading uncertainty, while the disturbance reaches the far end (9); the pulse shrinks along the spring because the floor takes energy (10); the model's limits and improvements (11); risks (12).
- **The hook (v01's claim, made testable).** "v01 said the tape comes back home. Will it, exactly?" 3 s prediction: "exactly home?", "a little forward?", "carried along?". The data answer it: home within ±1 cm in all six runs (9). The point: "it looks like it comes back" becomes a measurement with an uncertainty.
- **Method (site activity 1, extended).** A metal spring stretched to 4.00 m along a smooth floor; its far end tied low to a retort stand's rod; its near end on a wooden handle. Purple tape on the coil at the 2.00 m mark (the marked coil) and a second tape at 3.80 m (to show the disturbance arrives). A floor grid of tape at 0.10 m squares under the spring, in the same plane, as the scale. A phone fixed on a tall tripod directly above the 2.00 m mark, filming at 120 frames per second. Transverse runs: the handle moves 20 cm sideways and back, once, in about a quarter of a second. Longitudinal runs: the handle moves 20 cm along the spring and back, once. Each frame, the tape's position is read against the grid. The rope (a long cotton rope, the same length) is shown only as a model choice in scene 3: it shows transverse waves clearly, but its pieces cannot be seen squeezing, so it cannot model a longitudinal wave.
- **Illustrative data (generated, labelled).** Reading uncertainty ±1 cm (positions read to the nearest centimetre from a slightly blurred tape). Transverse, three trials: largest sideways motion 15, 14, 16 cm (mean 15 cm, range 2 cm, ±1 cm); largest along motion 0, 1, 1 cm; final offset from home 0, 1, 0 cm. Longitudinal, three trials: largest along motion 12, 13, 11 cm (mean 12 cm); largest sideways motion 1, 0, 1 cm; final offset 0, 0, 1 cm. Trend (transverse, means): largest sideways motion 17 cm at 1.00 m, 15 cm at 2.00 m and 12 cm at 3.00 m. Said aloud in scene 6: "These numbers are illustrative, made up to show the method, not real results." Every table, trace and readout carries the grey tag "illustrative data".
- **Representations (`ci0fca96de`, the "models" part left by FA1 v24).** The physical models (spring, rope) with what each can and cannot show; a path trace of the tape's positions (one dot per frame, in plan view); a results table with units in the headings. Path traces replace graphs against time, which v06 owns (open decision 2).
- **Analysis (relate to energy transfer).** In transverse runs the largest motion is across the spring, more than fifteen times its along motion; in longitudinal runs it is along the spring, more than fifteen times its across motion (author note c). Every final offset is zero within ±1 cm, while the disturbance travels 4.00 m and the 3.80 m tape moves after it. So the tape oscillates about home, across or along the direction of energy transfer, and is not carried. The data support the hypothesis; they do not prove it for every medium (FA1 v24's "support, not prove").
- **The trend (`ci7afa7b23` applied).** The pulse's sideways motion shrinks from 17 to 12 cm between 1.00 m and 3.00 m. Cause: friction between the coils and the floor turns some of the pulse's energy into heat at each step, so less is passed on. This is also why the far end must be close enough for the pulse to arrive.
- **The model's limits (`cibc9c30ac`).** (1) The spring's coils are joined, but air molecules are not: they pass the motion on by colliding, and they also move randomly and fast all the time; the spring models the average back-and-forth motion only. (2) Floor friction drains energy (the trend). (3) A single pulse is not a continuous wave. (4) The coils are huge and slow, which is why we can see them: this is the model's strength. Improvements: hang the spring from long threads to remove floor friction; keep the camera fixed and square to the floor, with the scale in the spring's plane; use a higher frame rate to sharpen the tape.
- **Prediction 2 (3 s, scene 7).** "Push and pull the handle: what shape will the tape's trace make?" Along the spring and back (8).
- **Risk assessment (`cif90164e3`, partial; site has no risk line).** Three spoken rows, hazard, harm and control: (1) the stretched spring released (it snaps back, tangles and can strike hands or face: hold both ends, and walk the ends together before letting go); (2) a spring or rope across the floor (a trip: set up in a clear area away from doors and walkways, and walk around it, not over it); (3) the rope's moving end and the spring's handle (whipping into eyes or faces: keep movements small, and stand to the side, clear of the rope). Teacher notes, not shown: overstretching a metal spring permanently kinks it (equipment damage); only the spoken row is shown; earlier rows collapse to one-word grey chips (FA1 v24).

## Script and shots

Cue words in **bold** trigger the visual change. Exact frames are set from the recording's word timings. Colour key as Decisions.

### 1. hook (does the tape really come back?)
> Last time, we watched a marked coil on a spring go back and forth, and come back home. But watching isn't the same as measuring. Today we test it ourselves, with a real spring and a rope. When a pulse passes, will the tape end up exactly home, a little further along, or carried right **along**?

Visual: opens on v01's last frame (the spring on the floor grid with the purple tape on its middle coil; the phone on its tall tripod; a coiled rope beside it; the "Next" chip). The chip becomes the heading: "The wave-modelling investigation". A grey dashed "prediction" ring sits on the tape's home mark. On "along" chips "exactly home?", "a little along?", "carried along?" and a 3-second countdown ring.

### 2. question and hypothesis
> Start with a question we can test. When a pulse travels along a spring, which way does a marked coil move, and does it end up back where it started? Then a hypothesis, a prediction with a reason. Our hypothesis: the coil will move across the spring for a sideways pulse, and along it for a push and pull, and it'll end up home, because its neighbours pull it back while passing the energy **on**.

Visual: a planning panel at the right: "Question: which way does the marked coil move, and does it end up home?" writes first. On "hypothesis" a gloss "hypothesis: a prediction with a reason, that a test can check". Rows, one per clause: "sideways pulse → coil moves across"; "push and pull → coil moves along"; "ends up home"; on "on" the reason "because: neighbours pull it back while passing the energy on (v01)". The whole hypothesis box is tagged "prediction" in grey and stays tagged until scene 9.

### 3. choosing the models
> The rope shows a transverse wave clearly, but you can't see its pieces squeeze together, so it can't model a longitudinal wave. The spring can do both, and its coils are big and slow enough to film. So the spring is our main **model**. We stretch it to four metres along a smooth floor, tie the far end to a stand, and fix the near end to a handle. Purple tape marks the coil at the two metre mark, and a second tape sits near the far **end**.

Visual: two cards side by side: the rope (coded) with a ribbon, a sideways wave running along it, tick "transverse"; a small squeeze attempt that shows nothing visible, cross "longitudinal: can't see it squeeze". The spring, with ticks "transverse" and "longitudinal" and "coils big and slow: easy to film". On "model" the spring card enlarges into the plan view: the spring stretched along the grid; scale chip "4.00 m"; far end tied low to the retort stand's rod (`y11p-retort-stand-v1.png`); near end on the wooden handle (`phys-y11-spring-handle-v1.png`). On "end" labels: "marked coil, 2.00 m" (purple tape) and "second tape, 3.80 m".

### 4. variables
> The independent variable is the direction we move the handle: sideways, or along the spring. The dependent variables are how far the marked coil moves across and along, and where it ends up. Everything else stays the same: the same spring, stretched the same distance, the same marked coil, and the same handle movement, twenty centimetres out and back, **once**. Then any change in how the coil moves comes from the handle's direction.

Visual: a variables panel builds, one row per sentence (FA1 v24 style): "independent: handle direction (sideways or along)" with the handle's two possible motion arrows; "dependent: coil's largest motion across and along; final position"; "controlled:" with chips "same spring", "stretched to 4.00 m", "same marked coil (2.00 m)", "handle: 20 cm out and back, once" (dimension mark at the handle); on "once" the handle's path ghost shows one out-and-back.

### 5. recording the motion systematically
> We film the coil from directly above, with the phone fixed on a tripod, so every frame is taken the same way. The floor grid, ten centimetre squares in the spring's plane, is our scale. The phone records a hundred and twenty frames per **second**. Then we step through one frame at a time, recording the tape's position. We read each position to the nearest centimetre, so our reading uncertainty is plus or minus one **centimetre**.

Visual: a side-view inset: the phone on its tripod above the 2.00 m mark, a dashed field-of-view cone down to the floor, tag "fixed, square to the floor". The grid glows (dimension "0.10 m"). On "second" a filmstrip slides out below (coded frame boxes) with a counter "frame 1 of ..., 120 frames per second" (gloss "frame rate: pictures per second"). On "centimetre" a ring of radius 1 cm draws round the home mark, labelled "reading uncertainty ±1 cm".

### 6. one transverse run
> Here's one transverse run. These numbers are illustrative, made up to show the method, not real results. Each dot is the tape's position in one frame, so the dots make a path trace. The tape moves fifteen centimetres sideways, hardly moves along at all, and comes back to **home**. Then the second tape near the far end does the same, a moment later. The disturbance, and its energy, reached the far end, but the marked coil didn't **travel**.

The handle moves sideways once (tag "slowed down"); the pulse runs along the spring. ### 7. prediction: the longitudinal trace
> Now move the handle along the spring, in and out. What shape will the tape's path trace **make**?

### 8. the longitudinal run and the repeats
> A line along the spring, and back home. The tape moves twelve centimetres along, and at **most** a centimetre across. We run each direction three times. For the sideways pulses, the largest sideways motion is fifteen, fourteen and sixteen centimetres, a mean of fifteen, plus or minus one, half the range. For the push and pull, the largest motion along is twelve, thirteen and eleven, a mean of twelve, plus or minus **one**. And in all six runs, the tape ends up within one centimetre of home.

Visual: the handle moves along once (tag "slowed down"); a squeeze runs down the spring; the trace builds along the spring and back; readouts "largest along: 12 cm", "largest across: 1 cm"; the last dot lands in the ring; the "a line along" sketch fills. ### 9. what the data show
> In every run, the coil's biggest motion follows the handle's direction, more than fifteen times its motion the other way. Sideways pulses give across motion, perpendicular to the energy's path: **transverse**. Push and pull gives along motion, parallel to it: **longitudinal**. And the coil always ends up home, within our uncertainty, while the energy travels four metres to the far end. That supports our hypothesis. It doesn't prove it for every material, but it's strong **evidence**.
