## 3. Key ideas in order

1. Australia's Giant Animals: During the Pleistocene, Australia was home to a range of giant animals, its megafauna, and Aboriginal Peoples were already living here while many of these animals still walked the land.
2. Rock Art and Oral Traditions: Aboriginal evidence for megafauna comes mainly through rock art that depicts the animals and oral traditions that describe creatures matching the extinct species.
3. Why Multiple Sources Matter: No single source gives the whole picture; combining Aboriginal knowledge with the fossil record produces a fuller and more reliable account of the megafauna.
4. Why this matters: two-way science: Australian researchers increasingly work in genuine partnership with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, combining Western scientific methods with Indigenous knowledge held over tens of thousands of years.
5. Sources for the examples in this lesson: Every case study above is drawn from published work, so that you can check it rather than take it on trust.

## 4. Key terms

- Megafauna: Large-bodied animals; in Australia, the giant marsupials, birds and reptiles that lived in the Pleistocene and are now extinct.
- Palaeontological evidence: Evidence about past life, traditionally from fossils, but also including records of animals made by people who lived alongside them.
- Oral tradition: Knowledge passed down by spoken accounts across generations; in Aboriginal cultures, a rigorous and long-lived record of Country and its animals.
- Rock art: Images created on rock surfaces; some Aboriginal rock art depicts animals, including extinct megafauna.
- Diprotodon: The largest known marsupial, a giant wombat-relative roughly the size of a car, now extinct.
