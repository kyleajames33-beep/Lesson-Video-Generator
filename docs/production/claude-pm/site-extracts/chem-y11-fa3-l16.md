## 3. Key ideas in order

1. The Thermometer and the Insulated Cup: You cannot see energy, but you can see what it does to a thermometer: if a reaction warms its surroundings the reading climbs, and if it cools them the reading drops.
2. Method, Variables and the Measuring Equation: The core measurement is simple: record the temperature before the reaction starts, then track it until it reaches its highest point (exothermic) or lowest point (endothermic), and take the difference.
3. Sources of Error and Improving Reliability: No simple calorimeter is perfect: some of the reaction's energy always leaks away, so the temperature change you read is almost always smaller than the true change.
4. Worked Example, Reading a Temperature Change: A student adds NaOH(aq) to HCl(aq) in a coffee-cup calorimeter holding 50.0 g of solution.

## 4. Key terms

- Calorimeter: A device used to measure heat changes; in this practical a simple insulated cup with a lid and thermometer (a "coffee-cup calorimeter").
- Temperature change ($\Delta T$): The final temperature minus the initial temperature; positive for exothermic reactions and negative for endothermic ones.
- Exothermic / endothermic: Exothermic reactions release energy to the surroundings (temperature rises); endothermic reactions absorb energy (temperature falls).
- Controlled variable: A quantity kept the same across trials (for example volume and concentration) so the comparison is fair.

## 5. Worked examples

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- Misconceptions To Fix watch out ✗ Wrong: A reaction that gets cold is releasing its "coldness" into the cup. ✓ Right: A reaction that gets cold is endothermic, it is absorbing thermal energy from the water and surroundings, which is why the thermometer reads a lower temperature. ✗ Wrong: Using an open beaker gives the same result as an insulated cup. ✓ Right: An open beaker loses heat to the surroundings, so the measured $\Delta T$ is too small.
