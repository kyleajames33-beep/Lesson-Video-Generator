## 3. Key ideas in order

1. Frequency is how common a trait is in a sample (60 of 100 gives 60%), describing a pattern across a group, not every individual.
2. Allele frequency is one allele's copies divided by all gene copies in the population (2N copies for N individuals).
3. For a fully recessive trait, the recessive phenotype frequency equals the homozygous recessive genotype frequency.
4. Conclusions depend on sample size, representation, consistent definitions and freedom from bias.
5. A SNP is a one-base difference at a specific DNA position that can act as a comparison marker.
6. One SNP can suggest similarity or difference but cannot prove complete relatedness or separation.

## 4. Key terms

- Frequency data: Data showing how common a characteristic or allele is within a sample or population.
- SNP: Single nucleotide polymorphism, a one-base difference at a specific DNA position.

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- "A difference in a data table always means a biologically meaningful difference for the whole species." Page: the sample may be too small or unrepresentative.
- "One differing SNP proves two populations are completely unrelated." Page: "one SNP = one marker only".
- "A change in allele frequency always means natural selection." Page: changes come from selection, genetic drift, mutation and gene flow.

Possible site errors: (a) "Senate hearings followed and produced the Belmont Report in 1979": the Belmont Report was written by the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects, set up under the National Research Act of 1974, not produced by Senate hearings. (b) "the three principles of biomedical ethics: autonomy (informed consent), beneficence..., and justice": the Belmont Report names respect for persons, beneficence and justice as its basic ethical principles for research on human subjects; "autonomy" is not its term.
