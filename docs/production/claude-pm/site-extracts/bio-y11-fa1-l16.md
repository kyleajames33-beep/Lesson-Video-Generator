## 3. Key ideas in order

1. The Lock-and-Key Model: The lock-and-key model explains enzyme specificity by saying the active site has a fixed, exact shape that only the correct substrate can fit, just as one key fits one lock.
2. The Induced Fit Model: The induced fit model improves on lock-and-key by saying the active site is flexible and changes shape slightly as the correct substrate binds, moulding around it for a tighter, more effective fit.
3. Comparing the Two Models: Both models explain why enzymes are specific, but they differ in whether the active site is rigid or flexible, and knowing both lets you discuss how scientific models are refined by evidence.
4. Designing drugs that fit the lock: Many modern medicines are enzyme inhibitors, molecules designed to fit an enzyme's active site and block its substrate.

## 4. Key terms

- Active site: The specifically shaped region of an enzyme where the substrate binds and the reaction occurs.
- Lock-and-key model: A model in which the substrate fits an exactly complementary, rigid active site like a key in a lock.
- Induced fit model: A model in which the active site slightly changes shape to grip the substrate more tightly as it binds.
- Enzyme–substrate complex: The temporary structure formed when an enzyme and its substrate are bound together.
- Complementary: Having matching shapes that fit together, like a hand and a glove.
