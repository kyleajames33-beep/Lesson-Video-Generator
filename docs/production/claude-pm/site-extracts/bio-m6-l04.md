## 3. Key ideas in order

1. Chromosomal mutations alter large regions or chromosome number, potentially affecting many genes.
2. Deletion removes a segment, duplication adds a copy, inversion reverses a segment, and translocation moves it.
3. These changes can alter gene content, dosage, order, breakpoints or regulatory context.
4. Nondisjunction can change whole-chromosome number; Down syndrome with an extra chromosome 21 illustrates gene dosage.
5. A larger affected region can have broader effects, although exact phenotype depends on the genes and breakpoints involved.

## 4. Key terms

- Chromosomal mutation: “A mutation involving large-scale changes to chromosome structure, and sometimes chromosome number, rather than a single base sequence.”
- Deletion: A chromosome segment is lost.
- Duplication: A chromosome segment is copied twice, increasing gene dosage.
- Inversion: A segment reattaches in reverse orientation.
- Translocation: A segment moves to a different position, often another chromosome.
- Gene dosage: Number of gene copies, which can affect product amount.

## 5. Worked examples

An early Down syndrome karyotype has 47 rather than 46 chromosomes, with an extra chromosome 21 from meiotic nondisjunction. The comparison activity asks which is more likely to affect many genes: a missense substitution in one gene or deletion of a large chromosome segment, and asks why the outcome cannot be predicted perfectly. The visualiser asks students to classify segment order and identify changes retaining the same total amount of DNA.

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- Chromosomal mutation is another name for a one-base change. It acts at chromosome-segment or whole-chromosome scale.
- All structural mutations have identical effects. Deletion, duplication, inversion and translocation act differently.
- A bigger change has a perfectly predictable severity. Consequences depend on which genes and controls are affected.
