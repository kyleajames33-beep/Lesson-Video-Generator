## 3. Key ideas in order

1. The Equation and Its Units: Calorimetry rests on one idea: the more substance you heat, the harder it is to heat, and the bigger the temperature jump, the more energy it took.
2. Solving for Heat Energy (q): When you know $m$, $c$ and $\Delta T$, finding $q$ is just careful substitution, list the values, watch the units, then multiply.
3. Rearranging for m, c or ΔT: Because $q = mc\Delta T$ is a simple product, you can divide both sides by whichever quantities are known to isolate the one you want.

## 4. Key terms

- Heat energy ($q$): The thermal energy transferred to or from a substance, measured in joules (J) or kilojoules (kJ).
- Specific heat capacity ($c$): The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C, in J g -1 °C -1. For water, $c = 4.18$.
- Temperature change ($\Delta T$): The difference between the final and initial temperature, $\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}$, in °C.
- Calorimetry: Measuring heat transferred in a reaction by recording the temperature change of a known mass of substance (usually water).

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- Misconceptions To Fix watch out ✗ Wrong: The mass in $q = mc\Delta T$ is the mass of the fuel or chemical that reacts. ✓ Right: $m$ is the mass of the substance being heated, usually the water (or solution) absorbing the heat, not the reactant. ✗ Wrong: You must convert °C to kelvin before using $\Delta T$. ✓ Right: A change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K, so $\Delta T$ is the same number either way, no conversion is needed.
