## 3. Key ideas in order

1. Emerging biotechnology trends toward more targeted intervention through gene editing, synthetic biology, precision breeding and disease screening.
2. Potential benefits include resilient crops, earlier disease detection, targeted treatment and specialised industrial production.
3. Current clinical capability must be separated from a claim of universal safety, effectiveness or access.
4. The page assesses future benefit against need, feasibility and fairness, including regulation, cost and scale.

## 4. Key terms

- Gene editing: Targeted alteration of DNA sequence at a chosen location.
- Synthetic biology: Design or redesign of biological systems for specific purposes.
- Precision breeding: Genetic knowledge or targeted tools used to guide breeding outcomes.
- Disease screening: Biotechnology used to identify genetic risks or markers earlier.
- Targeted therapy: Treatment directed at a particular molecular or genetic feature.
- Feasibility: Whether a technology is practical, effective, safe and scalable.

## 5. Worked examples

An ex vivo sickle-cell treatment extracts blood-forming stem cells, edits BCL11A regulation to reactivate foetal haemoglobin and reinfuses the cells. The page says early participants had major reductions in severe events; the sickle mutation itself was not corrected. The matching activity pairs gene editing with targeted change, screening with earlier detection, precision breeding with desired traits, and synthetic biology with specialised production.

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- An advanced technique guarantees social benefit. Safety, cost, access and reproducibility matter.
- CRISPR has no off-target effects. The page says guide RNAs may bind unintended sequences.
- Every emerging technology is already a universal solution. Current evidence and future prediction are distinguished.
