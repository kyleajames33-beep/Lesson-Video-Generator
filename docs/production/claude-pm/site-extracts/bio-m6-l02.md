## 3. Key ideas in order

1. A mutagen increases the chance of DNA damage or copying error; a lasting mutation results when an unrepaired sequence change is copied.
2. UV radiation can form abnormal bonds between neighbouring bases, while ionising radiation can break DNA strands.
3. Chemicals can modify bases, mimic bases or interfere with DNA replication.
4. Natural sources include background radiation and some viruses that insert genetic material into host DNA.
5. The page plans a UV exposure investigation with a zero-second control, repeated trials and colony counts; it says fewer colonies alone cannot distinguish mutation from cell death.

## 4. Key terms

- Mutagen: “An agent that increases the rate of mutation by damaging DNA or interfering with replication.”
- UV radiation: Radiation that can damage DNA by forming abnormal bonds between neighbouring bases.
- Ionising radiation: High-energy radiation that can remove electrons from atoms and cause DNA strand breaks.
- Chemical mutagen: A chemical that alters bases, causes mispairing or disrupts replication.
- Background radiation: Naturally occurring low-level ionising radiation.
- Insertion effect: Foreign genetic material inserted into DNA may disrupt a gene or its regulation.

## 5. Worked examples

The UV and baker’s yeast investigation varies exposure time at 0, 15, 30, 60 and 120 seconds at fixed lamp distance. Mean colonies per plate across three trials are 142, 98, 61, 24 and 5 respectively. The page also offers radish or mung bean germination as an alternative. It identifies exposure time as independent, viable colonies or germination as dependent, and inoculum, medium, lamp and incubation conditions as controls.

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- A mutagen is the same as a mutation. The first is an agent; the second is a DNA sequence change.
- Exposure guarantees mutation. Repair can prevent a lasting change.
- Natural mutagens are harmless. Background radiation and some viruses can damage DNA.
- Only germ-line cells are affected. Somatic cells can also acquire mutations.

## 7. Practical or real-world examples used

Sunlight and skin-cell UV exposure; X-rays and gamma rays; base-analogue chemicals; viruses; background radiation; Radiation Effects Research Foundation survivors; shielded UV lamp and yeast agar plates.
