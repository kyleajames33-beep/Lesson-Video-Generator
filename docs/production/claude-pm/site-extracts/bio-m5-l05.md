## 1. Title and main question

Manipulating Reproduction in Agriculture. How does controlling reproduction improve agriculture, and what biological and ethical risks accompany it?

## 2. Syllabus content covered

2017, BIO12-12: "evaluate the impact of scientific knowledge on the manipulation of plant and animal reproduction in agriculture (ACSBL074)".

## 3. Key ideas in order

1. Selective breeding chooses parents with desirable traits, accumulating favourable alleles but potentially shrinking the gene pool.
2. Controlled mating, artificial insemination and embryo transfer control which animals reproduce and spread chosen genetics efficiently.
3. Controlled pollination prevents unwanted pollen and combines traits from chosen plant parents.
4. Productivity, uniformity, disease resistance and faster trait spread must be weighed against reduced diversity, shared vulnerability and animal welfare.
5. Extended responses judge productivity, genetic health and sustainability, then consider conservation through seed banks, heritage breeds and outcrossing.

## 4. Key terms

- Selective breeding: Choosing parents with desirable characteristics so those traits are more likely to appear in offspring.
- Artificial insemination: Introducing semen into the female reproductive tract without natural mating.
- Controlled mating: Managing which animals reproduce together to influence inherited traits.
- Controlled pollination: Deliberately transferring pollen between selected plants to control inheritance.
- Embryo transfer: Placing an embryo from one female into another female for development.
- Gene pool: The total variety of alleles present in a population.

## 5. Worked examples

Activity answers:

- One champion bull's semen across hundreds of dairy cows: artificial insemination; rapid spread of proven genetics, reduced gene pool if the sire is overused.
- Hand-transferred pollen between two apple varieties: controlled pollination; combines chosen traits, crop uniformity may increase shared vulnerability.
- Prize-winning ewe's embryos in surrogate ewes: embryo transfer; more offspring from a valuable female, intensive management and welfare concerns.
- Fastest-growing pigs mated each generation: selective breeding / controlled mating; faster growth-rate improvement, other traits and diversity may be reduced.

Goal-to-method answers: one elite male to many females, artificial insemination; more offspring from one valuable female, embryo transfer; disease resistance plus high plant yield, controlled pollination; improved milk yield over generations, controlled mating with selective breeding.

Evaluate (7 marks): judge productivity, genetic health and sustainability. Answer: productivity improves while diversity falls through the same selection mechanism; seed banks, heritage breeds and outcrossing manage the cost.

Assess (6 marks): judge likelihood, severity and reversibility of diversity loss. Answer: moderate likelihood but severe, largely irreversible consequences justify precaution; preserved diversity outside the commercial population determines recovery.

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- Selective breeding has no health consequences: intensive selection narrows the gene pool, increases homozygosity and can fix deleterious recessive alleles.
- Agricultural manipulation has only benefits: evaluation must include diversity, disease vulnerability and welfare.
- Cloning always increases diversity: the page's true/false check rejects this.

## 7. Practical or real-world examples used

Dairy cattle, wheat rust resistance, apple varieties, ewes and pigs. Dolly: adult mammary cell from a 6-year-old Finn Dorset sheep, SCNT, 1997, 277 attempts, lifespan 6.5 years. Pugs' skull deformities and German Shepherds' hip dysplasia illustrate selection costs. Irish potato famine, Gros Michel bananas and Svalbard seed bank support the risk assessment.
