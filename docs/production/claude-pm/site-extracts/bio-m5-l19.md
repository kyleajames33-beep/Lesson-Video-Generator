## 3. Key ideas in order

1. Population genetics predicts group-level risk patterns, relatedness trends and allele distribution trends more reliably than exact individual outcomes.
2. A genotype "may increase risk without guaranteeing phenotype", because environment, gene interactions, mutation and future change all add uncertainty.
3. "Higher probability" is not a "certain outcome".
4. Module 5 is one chain: reproduction, then cell processes that preserve continuity and create variation, then gene expression, then inheritance patterns.
5. Hardy-Weinberg is a null model: deviation from expected proportions shows that a force is acting.

## 4. Key terms

- Risk pattern: A trend showing relative likelihood of an inherited condition or variant in a population or group.
- Prediction: A scientifically supported expectation based on evidence and assumptions.
- Synthesis: Bringing ideas from across the module together into one coherent understanding.

## 5. Worked examples

deCODE Genetics (hook and card): "Between 1996 and 2003", "290,000 Icelanders, 96% of all Icelandic adults"; lineages "back to 870 CE"; data access sold "to Hoffman-La Roche for $200 million" in 1998, under opt-out consent.

Evaluate (7 marks) model answer: criteria are whether model assumptions hold, the timescale and the population size; Hardy-Weinberg "matches observation closely in large populations".

## 6. Misconceptions the page addresses

- "Having a gene variant associated with a disease means you will definitely develop that disease." Page: such variants usually increase risk probabilistically; "Genetic risk is not genetic destiny."
- "Hardy-Weinberg is useless because its assumptions are rarely met." Page: the assumptions failing "is not a defect but the mechanism of the method".

Possible site errors: (a) "enrolled 290,000 Icelanders, 96% of all Icelandic adults": no source on the page; check before use. (b) The 2003 Supreme Court ruling is described two ways: the hook says it concerned "the privacy rights of deceased individuals' relatives", while the card says a woman challenged the database because her deceased father's information could be inferred, and "The court agreed." The accounts differ and neither is sourced. (c) "BRCA1 mutations confer ~70% lifetime breast cancer risk": reported estimates vary (roughly 55 to 72 per cent), so 70% is the upper end presented as one figure. (d) "was breached by opt-out consent": a judgement presented as fact, since the Belmont Report is a US research-ethics document.
