## 2. Site order, ideas, examples, practice and memory aids

**Teaching order.**

**L18:**

1. Back titration suits insoluble or slow-reacting samples and cases without a clear direct endpoint.
2. Add a known excess of standard reagent, let it react, titrate what remains, subtract moles and use the balanced-equation ratio.
3. The page emphasises the 1:2 CaCO₃:HCl and 1:3 Al(OH)₃:HCl ratios and removal of dissolved CO₂ before titrating residual acid.
4. Conductometric titration tracks electrical conductance as ions are consumed and produced.
5. HCl/NaOH gives a decreasing then increasing V-shaped curve with a minimum at equivalence; CH₃COOH/NaOH rises before and after equivalence with a slope change.
6. The page uses conductometry where coloured or turbid solutions make a visual indicator hard to read.

**Worked examples and numbers from the extracts.**

**L18:**

- A 620 mg antacid tablet labelled 500 mg CaCO₃ reacts with 25.00 mL of 0.500 mol/L HCl. Excess acid takes 18.40 mL of 0.250 mol/L NaOH. The page gives 395.4 mg CaCO₃, or 63.8% by tablet mass.
- A 0.850 g Al(OH)₃ tablet reacts with 30.00 mL of 0.600 mol/L HCl. Excess takes 22.45 mL of 0.200 mol/L NaOH (average of 22.45, 22.50 and 22.40 mL). The page gives 41.3% Al(OH)₃ by mass.
- An extended design task compares NaHCO₃ and CaCO₃ antacids, requesting a back titration for CaCO₃ and a conductometric curve for NaHCO₃.

**Practice.**

**L18:** Multiple choice, back-titration arithmetic, conductance explanation and practical design. Representative question: “A 0.850 g sample of an antacid containing Al(OH)₃ is dissolved in 30.00 mL of 0.600 mol/L HCl. The excess HCl is back-titrated with 0.200 mol/L NaOH, requiring an average titre of 22.45 mL. Calculate the percentage by mass of Al(OH)₃ in the tablet.”

The extracts record no named mnemonic unless shown above. Keep any useful contrast or question from the page; do not manufacture a memory aid.

## 4. Exam traps and student mistakes

**L18:**

- Subtract volumes directly; first convert both reagents to moles.
- Use a 1:1 CaCO₃:HCl ratio; the balanced equation gives 1:2.
- Added NaOH must always raise conductance; in HCl titration highly mobile H⁺ is replaced by less mobile Na⁺.
- Equivalence is the conductance maximum; it is the minimum for the page's strong acid/strong base case.

These traps come from the linked site lessons. They are not attributed to NESA markers without a specific exam report.
