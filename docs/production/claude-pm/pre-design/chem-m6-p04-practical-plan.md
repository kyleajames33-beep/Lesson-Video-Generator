# Chemistry Module 6 P04 practical plan (draft for Claude's review)

**Aim and route.** Use matched concentrations and measured pH to distinguish acid/base strength from concentration. `C6-IQ2-03`: “conduct an investigation to demonstrate the use of pH to indicate the differences between the strength of acids and bases (ACSCH102)”. Site L05 and L06. Prerequisites: chem-m6-c05 and chem-m6-p03.

**School method.** Use teacher-prepared 0.100 mol/L HCl, ethanoic acid, NaOH and ammonia. Put 20 mL of each in four coded beakers. Calibrate the pH probe at pH 4.00, 7.00 and 10.00, rinse and blot between measurements, and record three readings per solution at 25 °C. Then test a 0.00100 mol/L HCl aliquot to challenge the mistaken claim that low pH always means a stronger acid. Label the 100-fold dilution clearly and never infer intrinsic strength from pH alone.

**Variables and results.** Independent: acid/base identity for the matched 0.100 mol/L comparisons. Dependent: pH. Control temperature, total formal concentration, probe procedure and sample volume. Expected HCl pH 1.00, ethanoic acid about 2.88, NaOH 13.00 and ammonia about 11.12. The 0.00100 mol/L HCl has pH 3.00, slightly higher than the more concentrated weak ethanoic acid. Thus pH compares strength only when concentration is matched.

**Analysis and working.** For 0.100 mol/L ethanoic acid, x²/(0.100−x) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵; x = 1.33 × 10⁻³ mol/L, pH = 2.88. The same numerical Kb for ammonia gives pOH 2.88 and pH 11.12 at 25 °C. HCl has [H+] = 0.100 mol/L, pH 1.00. A 100-fold dilution gives 0.100/100 = 0.00100 mol/L and pH 3.00. Explain partial versus near-complete ionisation with particle counts, not a claim that weak acid is dilute.

**VRA.** Validity: equal formal molarity in each matched pair isolates strength; the dilute-HCl challenge is a separate comparison. Reliability: replicate readings with fresh aliquots. Accuracy: calibrated probe and temperature control. Check prepared concentrations from stock labels or volumetric preparation records.

| Hazard and classification | Control |
| --- | --- |
| 0.100 mol/L NaOH: irritant to eyes in school guidance | Safety glasses, promptly rinse splashes. [RSC 0.1 M practical](https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/making-plastic-from-potato-starch/1741.article) names this concentration. |
| 0.100 mol/L HCl: supplier SDS identifies it as non-hazardous at this dilution, though eye splashes still need control | Safety glasses and small volumes. [Southern Biological 0.1 M HCl SDS](https://www.southernbiological.com/content/MSDS/HydrochloricAcid0.1M_SDS_2025.pdf). |
| 0.100 mol/L ammonia: RSC describes dilute ammonia of this concentration as irritant | Capped teacher-prepared aliquots and ventilation; do not use concentrated ammonia. [RSC indicator practical](https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/indicators-and-dry-ice-demonstration/703.article). |

**Errors and fixes.** Comparing different concentrations confounds strength: prepare equal formal concentrations. Cross-contamination shifts pH: rinse and blot the electrode. Treating pH 3.00 HCl as weak ignores dilution: show particle ionisation alongside total concentration.

**HSC-style question idea.** A 0.100 mol/L weak acid has pH 2.9 while 0.00100 mol/L HCl has pH 3.0. Evaluate the claim that the first acid is stronger.
