## 2. Site order, ideas, examples, practice and memory aids

**Teaching order.**

**L19:**

1. A glass electrode measures a voltage related to pH; two-point calibration with known buffers corrects offset and slope.
2. A pH probe measures current acidity, while titration measures total titratable acid or base through stoichiometry.
3. Choose direct titration, back titration, conductometry or a probe according to sample solubility, colour, required result and monitoring needs.
4. The page applies these choices to wine, dairy, aspirin, wastewater, vinegar and antacid tablets.
5. For vinegar acidity it recommends NaOH titration; for CaCO₃ antacids it recommends back titration.
6. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander examples include controlled leaching of cycad seeds, adding alkaline plant ash to pituri and empirical testing of materials.

**Worked examples and numbers from the extracts.**

**L19:**

- For 10.00 mL vinegar of density 1.005 g/mL, titration with 0.5000 mol/L NaOH gives titres 18.55, 18.60 and 18.60 mL, averaging 18.58 mL. The page gives 5.55% acetic acid by mass. A probe reading pH 2.40 with Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ gives an estimated 0.884 mol/L or 5.28%.
- An aspirin tablet case compares direct NaOH titration, pH probing and conductometry; the page says hydrolysis into acetic and salicylic acids would make direct titration overestimate intact aspirin.
- A final design task asks students to select methods for three commercial vinegars and describe glass-electrode operation and calibration.

**Practice.**

**L19:** Method comparison, vinegar and aspirin analysis, pH-probe mechanism, multiple choice, short answers and extended method design. Representative question: “A student uses the following procedure to determine the concentration of acetic acid in vinegar. They pipette 10.00 mL of vinegar into a conical flask, add 3 drops of phenolphthalein, and titrate with 0.5000 mol/L NaOH. Their four titres are: 16.40 mL, 16.35 mL, 16.45 mL, 16.90 mL. Calculate the percentage by mass of acetic acid in the vinegar (density = 1.005 g/mL; M = 60.06 g/mol).”

The extracts record no named mnemonic unless shown above. Keep any useful contrast or question from the page; do not manufacture a memory aid.

## 3. Number and science check

Vinegar titres average 18.58 mL. Titration gives about 5.55% acetic acid by mass. At pH 2.40, [H⁺] = 0.003981 mol/L and c ≈ [H⁺]²/Ka = 0.884 mol/L, about 5.28% by mass. The opening 0.44 mol/L and 2.6% is wrong. For weak-acid/strong-base conductometry, read the intersection of trends, not a conductivity minimum. Relevant site-correction register entries: L19. Use the corrected statements in that register. Source for flagged corrections: [Module 6 site corrections](../site-corrections-m6.md).

**Site correction:** Site L19’s opening pH 2.40 calculation gives 0.44 mol/L and 2.6% vinegar; the arithmetic gives about 0.884 mol/L and 5.3%. Its vinegar/NaOH conductometric curve should be read at a trend-line intersection, not a minimum. See [Module 6 site corrections](../site-corrections-m6.md) for the source and replacement statement.

## 4. Exam traps and student mistakes

**L19:**

- A pH probe reading directly gives vinegar's total acetic acid concentration; it measures pH, requiring a model and Ka to estimate concentration.
- A digital instrument needs no calibration; the page requires two reference buffers.
- Titration is always best for a coloured or turbid solution; conductometry avoids a visual endpoint.
- First Nations chemical processing was accidental; the page describes repeatable leaching, alkaline ash use and empirical testing as continuing knowledge.

These traps come from the linked site lessons. They are not attributed to NESA markers without a specific exam report.
