## 2. Site order, ideas, examples, practice and memory aids

**Teaching order.**

**L18:**

1. Scientific capability affects populations only when a technology is adopted broadly and over time.
2. Public trust and community priorities shape social acceptance.
3. Cost, ownership, access and commercial incentives shape who can use a technology.
4. Cultural perspectives, including Indigenous perspectives, and regulation can redirect or restrict use.
5. Lasting genetic change is possible, but the scale depends on biological action and real-world uptake.
**L19:**

1. Mutation creates new alleles; recombination reshuffles existing alleles; gene flow moves alleles between populations.
2. Allele frequency is an allele's share of all copies in the population's gene pool.
3. Selection favours alleles linked to survival or reproduction; drift changes frequencies by chance, especially in small populations.
4. Bottleneck and founder events can reduce variation and change allele frequencies.
5. Individuals do not evolve during one lifetime; population allele frequencies change over generations.

**Worked examples and numbers from the extracts.**

**L18:**

- A guided capstone response weighs scientific capability against social, economic, cultural and regulatory conditions before judging whether DNA manipulation could change populations over time.
- The page cites semi-dwarf wheat released in 1966 and a 2019 report as a long-term comparison of production benefits and environmental costs.
**L19:**

- Four allele A copies among 100 allele copies give frequency 0.04 or 4%.
- A selection scenario begins with 4% carrying a resistant allele and says 90% of susceptible individuals die before reproducing. The resistant allele becomes more common in the next generation.
- A gene-pool simulator compares five replicates of N = 20 and N = 500, beginning at p(A) = 0.50 over 50 generations, then changes genotype fitness to contrast drift and selection.

**Practice.**

**L18:** Context-factor identification, multiple choice, a capstone inquiry evaluation, guided response and three short answers. Representative question: “Explain why a scientifically effective biotechnology may still have limited long-term population impact.”
**L19:** Prediction and reveal, simulator runs, multiple choice, three short answers and guided evaluation. Representative question: “Explain how mutation, recombination and gene flow each contribute to variation in a population.”

The extracts record no named mnemonic unless shown above. Keep any useful contrast or question from the page; do not manufacture a memory aid.

## 4. Exam traps and student mistakes

**L18:**

- A scientifically effective biotechnology will spread everywhere. Cost, law and acceptance can limit uptake.
- Community and Indigenous perspectives are irrelevant once a technique works. The page includes land, food, species and identity concerns.
- Laboratory mechanism alone answers the inquiry question. The page also requires context.
**L19:**

- Every allele-frequency change proves natural selection. Drift, mutation and gene flow can also change frequencies.
- Natural selection creates new alleles. Mutation creates them; selection acts on existing variation.
- An individual evolves after exposure to pressure. Evolution is a population change across generations.

These traps come from the linked site lessons. They are not attributed to NESA markers without a specific exam report.
