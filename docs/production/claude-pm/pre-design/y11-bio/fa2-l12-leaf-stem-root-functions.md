## 2. Site lesson material

Source: `C:/Users/kygs/hscscience/subjects/biology/year11/fa2-cells-to-systems/lesson12.sa.html` and `docs/production/claude-pm/site-extracts/bio-y11-fa2-l12.md`. Site section order: 1. Preparing and Observing Plant Sections; 2. Structure to Function, Leaf, Stem, Root; 3. Reading the Slide as Evidence; 4. Why plant pathologists slice stems too. Relevant ideas:

- Structure to Function, Leaf, Stem, Root: In every plant section, the shape of a structure is a clue to its job: pores for gas swap, hollow tubes for water, living tubes for sugar, and hairs for soaking up.
- Reading the Slide as Evidence: In a good practical write-up, every claim about function is backed by something you observed, not just memorised.
- Why plant pathologists slice stems too: The same sectioning skill is used to diagnose plant disease.

Practice demand: Map stomata, mesophyll, xylem, phloem and root hair cells to locations and jobs.

Site short-answer questions (3): 1. Using observations from microscopic leaf, stem and root structures, explain how a plant carries out gas exchange, water transport and nutrient transport. 2. A celery stalk is left overnight in red dye and then cut across. Describe what a student would observe, and explain how that observation is evidence for the function of a named tissue. 3. Using observations that can be made from microscopic sections of a leaf, a stem and a root, explain how a plant carries out gas exchange, water transport and nutrient transport. For each function, name the structure observed and state what about its appearance explains the function.

Site memory/retrieval cue: You can now prepare and read microscopic sections of leaf, stem and root and explain how their structures carry out gas exchange, water transport and nutrient transport. This completes the plant-transport story; next lesson switches to how animals manage the same needs.

## 4. Mistakes and traps

The site names this misconception: Misconceptions To Fix watch out ✗ Wrong: Plants get all their gases through their roots, like drinking water. ✓ Right: Gas exchange happens mainly through the stomata of the leaf (and lenticels of the stem), where carbon dioxide diffuses in and oxygen out. This is a page-backed teaching trap, not a claim about NESA marking trends.
