## 2. Site order, ideas, examples, practice and memory aids

- **The Nucleotide Building Block:** Phosphate, sugar and base, repeated millions of times DNA is a polymer built from repeating units called nucleotides, each made of three parts: a phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar, and a nitrogenous base. The four bases are adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G) . Many nucleotides link through their sugars and phosphates to form a long sugar–phosphate backbone , with the bases pointing inward. A nucleotide has three parts, a phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar and one of four nitrogenous bases (A, T, C, G). Joined sugars and phosphates form the backbone; the bases store the genetic information.

- **Complementary Base Pairing:** A pairs with T, C pairs with G, every time The two DNA strands are held together by base pairs that follow a strict rule, adenine always pairs with thymine, and cytosine always pairs with guanine. This is complementary base pairing . Because the pairing is fixed, if you know the sequence of one strand you can work out the other exactly, this is what makes DNA copying possible. Complementary base pairing: A always pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds), C always pairs with G (3 hydrogen bonds). One strand's base sequence therefore specifies the other strand's sequence completely.

- **The Double Helix and Hydrogen Bonds:** Why "weak" bonds are exactly what DNA needs The two strands twist into a double helix held by hydrogen bonds, individually weak, but numerous enough to be stable, while still easy to "unzip" when the DNA needs to be read or copied. Watson and Crick's model shows the two sugar–phosphate backbones running in opposite directions (antiparallel) and twisting around each other into a double helix , a spiral ladder, with paired bases as the rungs. Each base pair is held by hydrogen bonds : weak individually, but with millions along the molecule they together hold the strands firmly.

**Page activity:** Apply Band 4 Build the Complementary Strand Pattern B, Worked application In your book, write out this DNA strand: T–A–C–G–G–A–T–C . Underneath it, write the complementary strand using the base-pairing rules. Then label, for any one base pair, how many hydrogen bonds hold it together. Finish with one sentence stating why this pairing matters for copying DNA.

**Practice:** Use the double helix model to describe the structure of DNA. Refer to the composition of a nucleotide, complementary base pairing and hydrogen bonding.

**Memory aid:** No named mnemonic is stated in the selected page cards. Use the page’s comparison or physical model where it helps recall the mechanism.

**Page misconception:** ✗ Wrong: Any base can pair with any other base in DNA. ✓ Right: Pairing is strictly complementary, adenine only pairs with thymine, and cytosine only pairs with guanine. This is because of their shapes and the number of hydrogen bonds they can form (A–T forms 2, C–G forms 3). The rule is what makes accurate copying possible.
