## 2. Site lesson material

Source: `C:/Users/kygs/hscscience/subjects/biology/year11/fa2-cells-to-systems/lesson19.sa.html` and `docs/production/claude-pm/site-extracts/bio-y11-fa2-l19.md`. Site section order: 1. Filtration at the Glomerulus; 2. Reabsorption and Secretion in the Tubules; 3. Why "Filter Then Reclaim" Works; 4. Glucose in urine. Relevant ideas:

- Reabsorption and Secretion in the Tubules: As filtrate flows along the tubule, two opposite jobs happen: useful substances are reclaimed into the blood, and extra wastes are added in.
- Why "Filter Then Reclaim" Works: The "filter everything, then take back what you need" design lets the kidney both remove wastes and precisely adjust the blood's water and salt levels.
- Glucose in urine: a warning sign: Normally all filtered glucose is reabsorbed, so healthy urine contains none.

Practice demand: Use a sieve and beads to model filtration, reclamation and secretion.

Site short-answer questions (3): 1. Explain how glomerular filtration, selective reabsorption and secretion contribute to the removal of waste in a nephron. Refer to the role of the glomerulus, Bowman's capsule and tubules. 2. Glucose is present in the filtrate that enters the nephron tubule, yet it is normally absent from urine. Explain how this happens, and explain what a person's urine testing positive for glucose might indicate about the process. 3. The nephron filters almost everything small out of the blood and then reclaims most of it. Analyse why this apparently wasteful "filter then reclaim" design is more effective than a filter that removed only wastes, and use the named structures of the nephron to explain how the design is implemented.

Site memory/retrieval cue: You can now explain filtration, reabsorption and secretion and the role of each nephron structure. Next lesson compares this natural process with renal dialysis, the technology used when kidneys fail.
