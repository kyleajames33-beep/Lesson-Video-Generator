## 2. Site order, ideas, examples, practice and memory aids

**Teaching order.**

**L05:**

1. Somatic mutations arise in body cells and can affect the individual, while germ-line mutations in gametes or their producing lineage can pass to offspring.
2. Coding-region mutations can change codons, amino acids and protein function, but can also be silent.
3. Non-coding regulatory changes can alter when, where or how strongly genes are expressed.
4. The page combines two dimensions: body location determines inheritance; genome location helps determine function.

**Worked examples and numbers from the extracts.**

**L05:** The page contrasts Angelina Jolie’s inherited BRCA1 mutation, present throughout her body including eggs, with an imagined BRCA1 mutation arising later in one breast cell. It contrasts UV-induced skin-cell mutation with a change in sperm or egg. Its four-cell framework pairs somatic or germ-line location with coding or non-coding DNA, describing potential protein or expression effects in each. An activity contrasts an exon-codon substitution with a promoter-region change.

**Practice.**

**L05:** Somatic versus germ-line classifier, true/false, four-cell classification, coding versus non-coding comparison, multiple choice and short answer. Representative: “A mutation in a lung cell of an adult.” “A substitution in an exon codon. A mutation in a gene’s regulatory (promoter) region.”

The extracts record no named mnemonic unless shown above. Keep any useful contrast or question from the page; do not manufacture a memory aid.

## 4. Exam traps and student mistakes

**L05:**

- Any body-cell mutation passes to offspring. Somatic mutations generally do not.
- Every coding change is severe. Some are silent or have limited effects.
- Non-coding DNA is useless. Promoters, enhancers and other regions can regulate gene expression.

These traps come from the linked site lessons. They are not attributed to NESA markers without a specific exam report.
