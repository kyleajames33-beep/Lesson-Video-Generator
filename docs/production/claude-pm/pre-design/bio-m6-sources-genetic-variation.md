## 2. Site order, ideas, examples, practice and memory aids

**Teaching order.**

**L06:**

1. Mutation changes DNA sequence and can introduce new alleles.
2. Independent assortment and crossing over in meiosis rearrange existing alleles into genetically varied gametes.
3. Random fertilisation combines gametes, creating new allele combinations in offspring.
4. Siblings can differ without any new mutation in the relevant genes.
5. Mutation supplies long-term novelty; meiosis and fertilisation produce the combinations on which selection can act.

**Worked examples and numbers from the extracts.**

**L06:** After the 1977 drought on Daphne Major, surviving *Geospiza fortis* finches had offspring with deeper beaks on average. The page says the shift acted mainly on standing heritable variation rather than requiring a new mutation. Its seven-mark guided response analyses the relative contributions of all three processes to variation.

**Practice.**

**L06:** Process-to-role matching, sibling-difference explanation, checks, multiple choice and three short answers. Representative: “Explain how meiosis contributes to genetic variation.” “Compare the roles of mutation, meiosis and fertilisation in producing genetic variation.”

The extracts record no named mnemonic unless shown above. Keep any useful contrast or question from the page; do not manufacture a memory aid.

## 4. Exam traps and student mistakes

**L06:**

- Mutation is the only source of variation. Meiosis and fertilisation also generate different combinations.
- Meiosis and fertilisation create new alleles. They generally recombine alleles already present.
- Sibling differences require new mutation each generation. Different gametes and their random pairing are sufficient.

These traps come from the linked site lessons. They are not attributed to NESA markers without a specific exam report.
