## 2. Site order, ideas, examples, practice and memory aids

**L23 order:** Connect: predict a polymer's whole lifecycle; Explain: vary chains, branches, cross-links and forces; Represent: compare polymer classes and heating; Respond: choose a polymer for a purpose; Feedback: evaluate persistence, recycling and alternatives; Apply: connect natural polymers, hydrolysis and lifecycle.

**L23 key ideas:** 1. A polymer's structure at the nanoscale is the blueprint for everything you can feel, bend, melt, or break at the macroscale.
2. Can you melt it and reshape it? That single question separates thermoplastics (yes) from thermosets (no, it will char first).
3. Every addition polymer was chosen for a specific job, the monomer's side groups dictate what that job can be.
4. Most synthetic polymer chains combine high molecular mass, hydrophobic surfaces, crystalline regions and poor accessibility with a lack of suitable microbial enzymatic pathways.
5. Carbohydrates: monosaccharides to polysaccharides.
6. Same monomer, different bond geometry: starch vs cellulose.
7. Hydrolysis products of the three biomolecule classes.

## 3. Number and science check

Examples connect chain length, branching/crosslinks and forces to thermoplastic/thermoset behaviour, applications and recycling. Starch/cellulose use glucose with different linkages; protein hydrolysis gives amino acids; polysaccharides give sugars; triglycerides give glycerol and fatty acids. Triglycerides are not polymers. Site-extract phrases “compare synthetic and naturally occurring polymers” and “account for hydrolysis of triglycerides, proteins and carbohydrates to building-block monomers” are not standalone verbatim2017 Module7 content points in the ledger. Treat as context, not exact dotpoints. Softening changes chain movement/interchain interactions rather than breaking backbone covalent bonds. No numerical worked calculation.
