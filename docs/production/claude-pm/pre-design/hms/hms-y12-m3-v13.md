## 2. Site order, ideas, examples and practice

- **Why this is a health issue, not a media-studies issue:** Four consequences, each of which has been observed at population scale. Money. Australians spend a great deal out of pocket on products with little or no evidence, and that spending falls hardest on people with least, who often have the greatest need. Delay. - **How do you know whom to believe?:** Not by memorising a list of good sources, which goes out of date and does not help with a source you have never seen. Use four tests, in this order, they take seconds and they screen out most bad claims. Who benefits if I believe this? The single most efficient question. - **Site intentions:** Six questions. They are the same six whether the decision is about a surgical procedure, a supplement or a wellness app, and a person who can ask them is health literate in the sense this dot point means. What exactly is being claimed, and for whom? A product tested in one group may not transfer to another. “Supports wellbeing” is not a claim; it is marketing that cannot be false. How big is the benefit, in terms I can act on? Insist on the absolute change, not the relative one. A halving of a very small risk is still a very small change. Ten seconds, and it removes most of what you encounter. 2. What is the actual claim? Strip the adjectives. If nothing testable is left, stop. 3. Where would this be if it were true?
