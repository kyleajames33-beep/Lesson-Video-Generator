# Draft for owner review: chem-m5-c07

## 1. Explain (3 marks)

**Question:** Explain why an industrial equilibrium process may operate at a temperature that does not give the maximum equilibrium yield.

**Marking criteria:** 1 mark for yield versus rate distinction; 1 for temperature changing reaction rate; 1 for useful production rate or cost compromise.

**Sample answer:** A temperature that gives maximum equilibrium yield may make the reaction too slow. Industry chooses a temperature that gives enough product per unit time at an acceptable cost.

## 2. Apply (4 marks)

**Question:** For 2A(g) ⇌ B(g) + heat, predict the effect of higher pressure on equilibrium yield and state one practical reason a factory might avoid the highest possible pressure.

**Marking criteria:** 1 mark for two gas particles becoming one; 1 for higher pressure favouring B; 1 for higher yield; 1 for vessel cost, compression energy or safety constraint.

**Sample answer:** High pressure favours B because the product side has fewer gas particles, raising equilibrium yield. Very high pressure requires stronger vessels and more compression energy, increasing cost.

## 3. Evaluate (5 marks)

**Question:** A factory reaction is exothermic and produces fewer gas particles than it consumes. Compare Options A and B: A uses low temperature, high pressure and no catalyst; B uses moderate temperature, moderate pressure and a catalyst. Evaluate which is more likely to deliver a high practical output per hour.

**Marking criteria:** 1 mark for low temperature improving equilibrium yield; 1 for high pressure improving yield; 1 for low temperature slowing rate; 1 for catalyst speeding both directions without changing yield; 1 for justified judgement with cost or throughput.

**Sample answer:** A has a high theoretical yield because low temperature and high pressure favour products, but its reaction may be slow and compression costly. B gives a lower equilibrium yield but reacts faster at moderate temperature, with the catalyst helping it settle sooner. B could produce more per hour at a practical cost; plant data would decide the exact optimum.
