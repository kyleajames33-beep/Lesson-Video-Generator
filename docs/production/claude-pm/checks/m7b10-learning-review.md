Main question traced: *In 1847, washing hands in chlorine cut deaths in a maternity clinic, years before anyone accepted that germs cause disease. How do hygiene, isolation, quarantine and vaccination each break the chain of spread, and how do you judge which one works?* Useful outcome: given a disease and its route, the student can say which link each measure breaks, why hygiene fails for an airborne pathogen, why isolation starts late and quarantine must cover the incubation period, how herd immunity protects the unvaccinated and why measles needs higher coverage, and judge a plan by link, speed, how long it lasts and cost.

## Scope and focus

The video covers four measures plus environmental management, border biosecurity and herd immunity in 9.5 minutes, with seven new spoken terms. It stays one question because every scene answers "which link does this break, and when does it fail?" on one control board, and herd immunity is framed as the last link, not a second topic. The split decision is reasonable. The risk is pace: scenes 7 and 11 are the densest, and the video is at the 570 s cap. Watch both at the voiced preview.

## Strengths to keep

- **The hook is real evidence, read as data.** Paired bars (11.4 against 2.8, then 1.27 against 1.33) let the student see the gap close, and the basin lands between them on the cause. The question "stop a disease nobody could see?" earns the video.
- **One board gains one break per measure.** Students can point at the station each measure acts on and compare it with the earlier breaks.
- **The failure cases teach the limits.** Air dots floating above the sink's break mark (scene 5) and the shaded "spreads before anyone knows" band on the measles timeline (scene 6) show why one measure is not enough, which sets up quarantine and layering.
- **The contact network is the beat video does best**: pulses stopping at teal circles, the never-reached small circle, then two schools side by side with one outbreak. It shows herd immunity and local coverage, rather than describing them.
- **"Fourteen in every fifteen"** gives an intuitive route to 1 − 1/R₀ without a formula on the voice track, and the 93 per cent threshold is kept apart from the 95 per cent target with a reason.
- **Both attempts are genuine transfer** with brief feedback (about 28 s and 27 s) and one crossed mistake each.
