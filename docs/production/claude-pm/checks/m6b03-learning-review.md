Main question traced: *Every one of these changes is a single base, so why can one do nothing, one swap an amino acid, and one stop the protein?* Useful outcome: given a mutated sequence and a codon chart, the student can name the type of point mutation, read the new codon, and explain its effect on the polypeptide, including why a one-base insertion or deletion shifts the frame and a three-base one does not.

## Strengths to keep

- **One gene holds everything still.** Silent, missense and nonsense all sit in beta-globin (codons 6 and 39), so the only thing that changes between cases is the new codon. This is the strongest teaching decision in the design. The beta thalassaemia extra earns its time: it costs one hook sentence and scene 5, and it removes the need for an invented sequence.
- **The cause comes before the name in every case.** Scene 3 shows the chart giving the same amino acid before "silent"; scene 5 shows the ribosome halting before "nonsense"; scene 6 shows the brackets regrouping before "frameshift".
- **The video does something text cannot.** The three-base `FrameWindow` riding under the ribosome, and the brackets regrouping one codon at a time, make "reading frame" visible. A student can predict the regrouping before it happens.
- **The analogy carries its limit.** THE FAT CAT is followed at once by "a shifted frame still makes amino acids, just the wrong ones", which blocks the "gibberish means no protein" reading.
- **The attempts are genuine transfer.** 10a is an HSC-style sequence item with its own chart excerpt and every codon a student needs; 10c (cystic fibrosis against Tay-Sachs) tests the multiple-of-three rule as a cause on real alleles. Both extras earn their time: they are application, not new content, and neither card shows a person or organ.
- **Recall stays recall.** Sickle cell from B7 gets two sentences in scene 4; the stop-codon mnemonic gets one clause.
- **People.** No human figures. Red cells, a ribosome plate and coded blobs only.
