## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "A titration question gives you a pH curve, an indicator, a back titration or a conductivity graph. Where is the equivalence point, does the method find it, and if it misses, which way does the answer move?"

One main question: yes. All four questions ask "where is equivalence, and which way does a miss move the answer?", and the three-row check strip makes that visible. Useful outcome: a student can read equivalence from a pH curve and a conductivity graph, choose an indicator by the steep part, and predict an error's direction by finding which way the titre moves and where it sits in the calculation.

## Findings

No blockers.

### S1. Should fix: Q1's steep part is described differently from C13 and C14, and by its own rule methyl orange also fits

- Exact text, 2c: "The steep part drops from about **seven to three**. Methyl red changes between six point two and four point four, right **inside** that drop. So methyl red is the best choice."
- Student questions: "C13 and C14 said the ammonia jump was about six point six to four. Why seven to three now?" and "Methyl orange is three point one to four point four, which is inside seven to three, and the site said methyl orange. Why is methyl red better?"
- Problem: "seven to three" comes from 23.9 to 24.5 mL, a lopsided window (0.1 mL before, 0.5 mL after). One drop either side of equivalence, the series' usual definition, gives 6.58 to 3.94 (author note a), matching C13 and C14. With that definition methyl red is inside, bromothymol blue mostly above and methyl orange mostly below: the "most suitable" choice then follows from the rule the student already has. The site's methyl orange answer still needs a sentence, or students who studied the site will think they were wrong.
- Replacement, 2c: "Now the indicator. At equivalence, the flask holds ammonium chloride, and ammonium gives protons to water, so the pH there is **acidic**, about five. One drop either side of equivalence, the pH falls from about **six and a half to four**. Methyl red changes between six point two and four point four, right **inside** that drop. Methyl orange changes lower, at the foot of the drop, so it's a little late. Methyl red is the best choice." (424 characters, +111.) Visual: the y-axis bracket spans 23.95 to 24.05 mL (about 6.6 to 3.9), "seven to three" removed; the existing grey tag becomes bright on "foot of the drop": "methyl orange: changes just after the drop, slightly late; also accepted". Answer box (b) and model answer (b): "The steep section runs from about pH 6.6 to 3.9." Key note 1 can stay.
- Understanding enabled: one consistent jump for this system across C13, C14 and G04, and a reason a marker would prefer methyl red while accepting methyl orange.

### S2. Should fix: Q3 compresses the last three calculation stages into one unspoken sentence

- Exact text, 4b: "Halve that for the one to two ratio, turn it into a mass, and divide by the sample: **ninety four point one percent**."
- Student question: "Where did 0.1882 come from? It flashed up while she was still talking about halving."
- Problem: AGENTS.md asks for one reasoning stage at a time, with result lines aligned to spoken cues. The screen writes n(CaCO₃) = 1.880 × 10⁻³ mol and m = 0.1882 g, but nothing spoken lands on them, and their planned cues are not in the narration.
- Replacement, 4b: "Four steps, as in the back titration video. Added: **six point two five zero times ten to the minus three** moles. Left over, equal to the sodium hydroxide: **two point four nine zero times ten to the minus three**. Subtract: the eggshell used **three point seven six zero times ten to the minus three**. Halve it for the one to two ratio: **one point eight eight zero times ten to the minus three** moles of calcium carbonate. That's **zero point one eight eight two** grams, out of zero point two zero zero: **ninety four point one per cent**." (521 characters, +114.) Visual cues: "the equation" underlines on "one to two ratio" with the n(CaCO₃) line on "one point eight eight zero"; "M(CaCO₃) = 100.1" underlines and the mass line writes on "zero point one eight eight two"; "0.200 g" underlines on "zero point two zero zero".
- Understanding enabled: the student can follow and reproduce each line of the most-tested back-titration calculation, including the halving that C15 flagged as the classic slip.

### S3. Should fix: Q2's "instead of leaping" contradicts the curve being drawn

- Exact text, 3b: "After it, the extra ammonia and the ammonium form a **buffer**, so the pH creeps up slowly instead of leaping."
- Student question: "But the line just shot up from three to seven. Is that the leap?"
- Problem: the curve rises from about 3.1 (20.5 mL) through 5.3 at 20.83 mL to about 7.2 (21.0 mL): a real but short leap. What the buffer prevents is a continued leap up to the phenolphthalein range, which is the point Q2 needs.
- Replacement, 3b: "Picture the curve this way round. It starts low, near pH **one**, and the equivalence point is still acidic, about five. With question one's answer, that's at about **twenty point eight** millilitres. There the pH jumps, but only to about seven. Then the extra ammonia and the ammonium form a **buffer**, so the pH creeps up slowly instead of leaping on to ten." (349 characters, +50.) Visual: a short bracket on the jump "about 3 to 7" as "only to about seven" is said; the buffer stretch then draws as designed.
- Understanding enabled: the student sees exactly why phenolphthalein, which needs 8.3, waits: the jump stops below its range and the buffer holds the pH there.

### M2. Minor: key notes 2 and 4

- Note 4 adds "sample not fully reacted" and "water in the conical flask: no effect", which no scene explains. Replace: "4.
