## Facts confirmed (no change)

- **Q1 event order (C, E, G, F, A, D, H, B).** Barrier breach; inflammation within minutes (mast cell mediators widen and leak small vessels: a chemical change causing physical changes, the B7-IQ2-02 link); neutrophils emigrate and phagocytose within hours; dendritic cells take up antigen and migrate to the draining lymph node over hours to about a day, where they activate helper T cells; B cells bind intact antigen arriving in lymph; activated helper T cells give linked help; clonal expansion into plasma cells and memory B cells; antibodies bind. The design's model limit (E, G, F in any order among themselves, all after C and before A) is the right tolerance. B cells can meet lymph-borne antigen early, but A is the first adaptive event on this list, so placing F before A is a fair expectation for an HSC answer.
- **Q1(b), about a week.** Specific antibody to a first exposure is usually measurable about 7 to 10 days after exposure. "About a week" is correct as hedged; the reasons (few fitting cells, selection, help, many divisions; innate defences meanwhile) are correct.
- **Q2 graph values.** Invented, labelled "illustrative values". Log axis gridlines 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10 000. Primary lag about 6 days, peak about 40 at day 14, falling to about 10 by day 28; secondary lag about 2 days, peak about 2000 at day 35 (7 days after dose 2), about 1000 at day 56. 2000 ÷ 40 = 50, inside the commonly cited 10 to 100 times (the site's L13 Interpret model gives the same range). log10(40) = 1.60 and log10(2000) = 3.30, a gap of 1.70 gridlines, so "less than two gridlines above forty" is correct. A secondary peak about a week after a booster, with antibody rising within 2 to 3 days, is realistic. "Many vaccines are given as more than one dose" is correct (primary courses and boosters).
- **Q2 crossed mistake.** Counting gridline gaps on a log axis as linear steps is the classic misreading; "read the values off the axis, then divide" is the correct fix.
- **Q3 case.** Australian bat lyssavirus is found in all four mainland flying fox species and in insectivorous bats (RCH: "found in bats (including flying foxes and microbats)"); the 1998 human case followed a flying fox bite. A grey-headed flying fox is an appropriate animal for the plate. "Some Australian flying foxes carry" is correctly hedged (prevalence in healthy wild flying foxes is low, higher in sick or injured animals).
- **Q3 post-exposure treatment.** For an unvaccinated, immunocompetent person with a category II bat exposure (a scratch), the Handbook recommends HRIG plus rabies vaccine on days 0, 3, 7 and 14 (a fifth dose on day 28 if immunocompromised); this differs from a category II terrestrial animal exposure, where HRIG is not given. HRIG is infiltrated "as much of the dose as possible in and around all wounds" (RCH), with the vaccine in a different limb. Wound washing with soap and water, then flushing for about 15 minutes (RCH). The card ("first dose of rabies vaccine", "Further vaccine doses follow over the next two weeks", "into and around the wound") is correct. (searched) Handbook bat exposure figure and tables (search extract: "Unvaccinated, immunocompetent, Category II bat or any Category III: HRIG plus vaccine doses on days 0, 3, 7 and 14"); RCH guideline (links in findings 2 and 4).
- **Q3 timing.** Rabies vaccine produces neutralising antibody in about 7 to 14 days, so "a week or more" is correct; HRIG (half-life about 3 weeks) acts immediately and fades over weeks, so "fade over weeks and leave no memory" is correct. Incubation: RCH "varies from 5 days to many years"; usually 1 to 3 months for rabies; the 1998 ABLV case had more than two years. "Weeks or months" is correct as hedged.
- **Q3 classification.** HRIG: artificial passive. Vaccine: artificial active. The author note (HRIG can slightly reduce the vaccine response, hence one early dose at a separate site) is correct and properly kept out of learner copy.
- **Memory aids.** "Block, alarm, eat, alert" (b06), "Clonal selection: the name is the process", "Second exposure: faster, bigger, longer" (same antigen, stated on the card), "Active: you do the work. Passive: you're handed the antibodies" are all sound as used.
- **Absolute words.** "innate defences, which are always ready" (true: no prior exposure needed); "Only a few B cells fit" (true); "never reached the protective line" (true of the graph data); "almost always fatal" (hedged, correct); "leave no memory" (true of passive antibodies); "It doesn't design anything" (true). Findings 3 and 6 cover the rest. No others flagged.
- **Doubt (d), the protective line.** A defined protective antibody level is a fair teaching device; real correlates exist (for example anti-HBs of at least 10 mIU/mL for hepatitis B; a rabies neutralising titre of at least 0.5 IU/mL, which the Handbook uses). None needs naming.
