Main question: fur slows heat loss. How can students test that fairly without an animal, and what can the model tell them? The useful outcome is to plan the flask comparison, explain the controls, graph and interpret cooling data, and distinguish evidence about passive insulation from evidence about regulation in an animal.

## Trace from entry knowledge

The lesson earns its question with a familiar claim and a visible contrast. Scene 2 explains the model mapping and the trapped-air cause. Scenes 3 and 4 connect each variable to apparatus. Scene 5 gives the reason for equal starting temperatures before the example depends on it. Scenes 6 to 9 turn readings into curves, rates and a repeated-trial conclusion. Scene 10 explains why the result does not establish temperature regulation. Scene 11 uses S07's questions on a concrete method, and scene 12 links hazards, harms and controls.

The planned graph animation is useful: the student can follow each reading from the table to a plotted point, then watch the temperature difference from the room shrink as the curve flattens. Keep the room line and one current rate calculation legible. The three-zone layout and the long table in scene 9 still need actual-size playback review; the design alone cannot establish fit.

## Double-teaching and practical skills

B02 owns endotherms, metabolic heat, effectors and adaptation trends. P01's brief recalls of those ideas serve the new investigation and do not re-teach B02's kangaroo, countercurrent exchange or flying-fox case. The new material is how to isolate insulation's effect, process readings, and restrict the conclusion. Scene 10's one loop sentence is necessary to explain the model's limit.

The method can be planned and evaluated using S07's language: validity asks whether the comparison tests the intended factor; reliability asks whether repeated results agree; accuracy asks about closeness to the true value. Each has a visible example and a cause-specific fix. The three risk rows use hazard, harm and control. Keep the steel sensing tip immersed and the electrical connections dry, as the science review explains.

Attempt 1 has a calculation and causal explanation; attempt 2 requires identifying a confound and giving a fix. These are appropriate Section II-style tasks with illustrative marks. The 10 seconds is an opportunity to pause, not enough time to complete a four-mark written answer, and the invitation to pause is present. The two-second prediction can remain a quick binary decision with its reason immediately afterwards.
