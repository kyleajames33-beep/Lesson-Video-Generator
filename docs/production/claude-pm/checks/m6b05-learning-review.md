Main question traced: *A mutation's effect depends on where it happens, so where in the body and where in the DNA, and what difference does each make?* Useful outcome: given a mutation in a named cell and a named stretch of DNA, the student can say whether it affects only this organism or can be inherited, and judge its significance by what that stretch does rather than by whether it is coding.

## Strengths to keep

- **One tree carries the whole first half.** The cell lineage tree shows why a somatic mutation stays in a patch (mitosis copies it only into one cell's descendants), why earlier means bigger, and why a germ-line mutation ends up in every cell of the offspring (it is in the zygote). A student can predict which cells will tint before they do. This is the video doing what text cannot.
- **Causes come before names.** The patch forms before "somatic mutation" is said (scene 3); the polymerase slips and the amount bar falls before "regulatory sequences" (scene 7); the intron lifts out as the term lands (scene 9).
- **The hook earns attention and is answered.** One gene, two places, two outcomes; scene 5 returns to it. The figure is her own doctors' estimate, not a general carrier risk, and no person is drawn.
- **The second half blocks the right misconceptions.** "Coding always matters more" and "non-coding is junk" are each met by a concrete case (the silent coding change, the promoter, lactase persistence). Scene 10's "Neither, as a rule" is the right judgement for the "assess" verb.
- **The grid joins the two questions.** The two-by-two grid is a compact, reconstructable anchor and each cell has a real example.
- **The attempts are genuine transfer.** 13a uses a new organism and asks for effects on the organism and its offspring, which b01's lizard item did not. 13c makes the coding change silent, so the "coding matters more" trap is live.
- **Recall stays recall.** b02's UV, B9's one in two, b03's four outcomes and B6b's promoter are each one clause.
