## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "A crushed antacid tablet fizzes away slowly in acid, and a dark red vinegar hides any indicator. When a direct titration can't give a clear endpoint, how do back titrations and conductivity titrations still find the amount, and how do you read their results?"

One main question: yes. Both methods answer "what do you do when a direct titration can't give a clear endpoint?", and the route places both here. Useful outcome: a student can say why a calcium carbonate tablet defeats a direct titration, describe a back titration and the carbon dioxide step, calculate a mass and percentage by subtracting moles and using the ratio, say when a conductivity titration helps, read equivalence where two best-fit lines cross, and say why dilution matters.

## Findings

### S1. Should fix: scene 8's reason for the best-fit lines does not match its own data

- Exact text: "Near the start and near equivalence the points curve a little, so don't hunt for one point." Visual: "the first two points and the 8 to 9 mL points pulse grey (tag 'curved: leave out of the lines')".
- Student question: "The 8 and 9 mL points look straight. Why not just read the corner?"
- Problem: with the dilution correction, the 7 to 10 mL points sit within 0.01 of the two lines (science S1). The real reasons are the curvature at the start and that no reading lands on 8.20 mL.
- Replacement: "The first points curve a little, and equivalence usually falls **between** two readings, so don't hunt for one point." The 0 and 1 mL points pulse grey ("curved at the start: leave out"), and a bracket spans the 8 to 9 mL gap ("no reading lands on equivalence").
- Understanding enabled: the student draws lines because the answer lies between measurements. That reason transfers directly to A2, where the lowest measured point is 12.0 mL and equivalence is 12.5 mL.

### S2. Should fix: scene 9 does not say why adding water reduces the dilution problem

- Exact text: "So the vinegar is topped up with plenty of water, which doesn't change the moles of acid, and the sodium hydroxide is concentrated, so only a small volume goes in."
- Student question: "Adding water dilutes the flask more. How does that fix dilution?"
- Replacement: "So the vinegar is first topped up with plenty of **water**. That doesn't change the moles of acid, but now each portion changes the total volume by only a small fraction. And the sodium hydroxide is **concentrated**, so only a small volume goes in." Add a "water" cue, with the tag "small fraction of the volume" when the full beaker rises only slightly.
- Understanding enabled: the fix is a ratio (added volume ÷ total volume), so the student can explain both remedies with one idea.

### S3. Should fix: scene 10 divides by the vinegar's volume without saying why

- Exact text: "So the acid is zero point zero zero eight two zero moles, and its concentration is zero point eight two zero moles per litre".
- Student question: "Scene 9 said water was added. Do I divide by 10 mL or 100 mL?"
- Replacement: "So the acid is **zero point zero zero eight two zero** moles. Divide by the ten millilitres of **vinegar**, not the diluted volume, because the water added no acid: **zero point eight two zero** moles per litre". Pulse the "10.00 mL" given, with the tag "divide by the vinegar's volume: water adds no acid".
- Understanding enabled: it prevents the classic made-up-volume slip, and it closes the loop with scene 9's "doesn't change the moles".

### M1. Minor: scene 1 end state

- Dim the "HCl", "CaCO₃ tablet" and "slow, at the surface" tags when their sentences end, leaving the heading and the two chips.

### M2. Minor: headroom in scene 16

- S2 and S3 add about 160 characters. Trim "let it react," from scene 16 (it repeats scene 3 and key note 1): "Add an excess, boil off carbon dioxide, titrate the leftover, then subtract moles and use the ratio."
