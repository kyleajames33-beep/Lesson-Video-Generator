## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "A name like 3-methylpent-1-ene looks like a code. How do you read a name like that, write one from a drawing, and draw the molecule back from the name?"

One main question: yes. Reading, writing and drawing back are one skill seen from two directions, and scene 11 closes the loop. Useful outcome: a student can draw a hydrocarbon three ways, restore implied hydrogens, name a branched alkane, alkene or alkyne up to C8 (longest chain, nearer-end numbering, di- and alphabetical order, multiple-bond locant and priority), and check a drawing against its name by counting atoms.

## Trace from the entry knowledge (student starting Year 12, Year 11 bonding only)

Entry: carbon has four valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds; single, double and triple bonds; line drawings of simple molecules; molecular formulas. No Year 12 video assumed. The trace follows the hook's 3-methylpent-1-ene and then A1's alkene.

1. Hook: a real canister, "butane" split into "but" (4 carbons) and "ane" (single bonds), then a harder name with a blank board and "?". The puzzle is set and is paid off in scene 12. Good.
2. Four bonds: the Year 11 callback is one clause and one chip. "The bonds spread out, so the carbons zigzag" is a short cause, and the turning ball-and-stick shows it. Shapes stay in c03. Good.
3. Three drawings: the morph from full to condensed to skeletal keeps one molecule on screen, which is the right use of the medium. The implied-H rule "enough to give each carbon four bonds: three on an end carbon, two on a corner, and one where a branch joins" is correct for single-bonded carbons only. It is the only implied-H rule the video teaches, and A1 depends on a case it does not cover (B1).
4. Rule 1: stem ladder, mnemonic after the rule, shape links. Good.
5. Rule 2: endings with plain meanings of saturated and unsaturated, and the "a, e, y: 1, 2, 3" aid after the meanings. Good.
6. Rule 3: the bent-chain trap ("you might call it two-ethylbutane") traced first in grey, then the five-carbon path. This is the strongest beat: a student predicts four, sees five, and learns why the drawing's row is not the chain. Good.
7. Branch names and rule 4: correct, but dense (two worked cases, branch naming, the locant term and a crossed chip in 480 characters; M2). "so the branch gets the lowest number" states the rule, not why a rule is needed (M3).
8. Rule 5: di-, punctuation, alphabetical order; the "methyl first because 2 < 3?" chip is said positively. Good.
9. Multiple bonds: "The main chain has to contain the double bond" gives no reason. Student question: "Why must it? The longest chain is the longest chain." (S2). Scene 9 is also the natural place for the missing implied-H case (B1).
10. Priority: "The double bond wins" is stated, with a real conflict (4-methylpent-1-ene against 2-methylpent-4-ene), which is a good design change from the site. But the teaching checks claim the cause "the ending names the bond, so its locant comes first", and the narration never says it (S1).
11. Name to structure: a strong reverse case with a count check. "C six H fourteen, which is a different compound" teaches that a molecular formula is a compound, and c02 opens by teaching the opposite (S3).
12. Memory aid: three lines after all rules; the hook's name drops into the builder, answering its "?". Good.
13, 14. A1 and feedback 1: see B1.
15, 16. A2 and feedback 2: good. The student's name is the only given and stays on screen; the feedback draws what the student described, then traces. The "explain why" demand is answered by "six carbons, not five".
17, 18. Handoff with the C₄H₁₀ "?" is a good bridge to isomers. Key notes are useful; note 1 has the same implied-H gap (B1) and omits the zero-hydrogen case scene 11 shows (M1).

## Findings

### B1. Blocker: the attempt asks for an alkene's molecular formula from a skeletal formula, but the only implied-hydrogen rule taught gives the wrong answer

- Exact text (scene 3): "You put them back in your head, enough to give each carbon four bonds: three on an end carbon, two on a corner, and one where a branch joins." Key note 1 repeats "end 3, corner 2, branch point 1". Author note g admits the gap ("A1's skeletal formula needs this, and feedback 1 states the count only as the molecular formula").
- Student question: "I counted 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 16, so C₇H₁₆. Why is it C₇H₁₄?" Applying the taught shortcut to A1's skeleton gives C₇H₁₆; no scene shows a skeletal C=C carbon's hydrogens being counted.
- Repair, scene 9, after "In but-one-ene, the double bond starts at carbon one.": "In a skeletal drawing, a double bond counts as **two** of a carbon's four bonds, so each carbon in it has one fewer hydrogen." Visual: on "two" the implied-H counter runs on but-1-ene: the end C=C carbon gets two grey H ghosts, the next one, then they fade (tag "C=C: one fewer H on each carbon", dims).
- Repair, feedback 1: replace "That's **five-methylhex-two-ene**, C seven H fourteen." with "That's **five-methylhex-two-ene**. Each double-bond carbon has just one hydrogen, so it's **C seven H fourteen**." Visual: on "one hydrogen" the implied-H counter shows one ghost on each C=C carbon, then the C₇H₁₄ chip.
- Repair, key note 1: "...skeletal (zigzag; corners and ends are C; add H so each C has 4 bonds: end 3, corner 2, branch point 1, a carbon joined to four carbons 0; a C=C counts as 2 bonds, so each of its carbons has one fewer H)."
- Understanding enabled: the student counts hydrogens from the four-bond principle rather than a single-bond shortcut, so the count transfers to alkenes, alkynes and A1.

### S1. Should fix: scene 10 says the double bond wins but not why

- Exact text: "What if there's a branch as well? The double bond **wins**."
- Student question: "Why does the double bond get the low number and not the branch? Last scene, the branch did."
- Replacement: "What if there's a branch as well? The double bond **wins**, because it's named in the ending, so it gets first claim on the low numbers."
- Understanding enabled: the priority follows from the structure of the name (the ending describes the main chain), which the teaching checks already claim.

### S2. Should fix: scene 9 says the main chain must contain the double bond without a reason

- Exact text: "The main chain has to **contain** the double bond, and you number from the end that gives it the lowest number."
- Student question: "Why can't the longest chain just be the longest?"
- Replacement: "The main chain has to **contain** the double bond, because the ending, ene, describes the main chain's bonds. Then you number from the end that gives it the lowest number."
- Understanding enabled: the student connects rule 2 (the ending shows the bonds) to the chain choice, which also supports S1. (Science check M2 records that the 2013 IUPAC rules choose length first; every case here agrees with both conventions.)

### S3. Should fix: scene 11 calls a molecular formula "a different compound"

- Exact text: "Count only one of the methyls, and you'd write C six H fourteen, which is a **different** compound."
- Student question (next video): "Last video said a formula is a compound. Now one formula is several compounds?"
- Replacement: "Count only one of the methyls, and you'd write C six H fourteen: one carbon and two hydrogens **short**, so it can't be this molecule." Move the crossed-chip cue from "different" to "short".
- Understanding enabled: the count check is about matching atoms, and it leaves c02 free to show that one formula can describe several compounds.

### M1. Minor: key note 1 omits the zero-hydrogen carbon

- Scene 11 shows "C2: four carbons, no H", but note 1 says "branch point 1". Covered by the B1 key-note replacement.

### M2. Minor: scene 7 is the densest scene

- Two cases, branch names, the word locant and a crossed chip in one scene. Dim the "CH₃ = methyl, C₂H₅ = ethyl" tag before the locant counter runs, and take the author's own fallback (2-methylbutane in its own short scene) if the voiced preview shows the scene rushed.

### M3. Minor: rule 4 has no reason in narration

- Exact text: "Rule four: number the main chain from the end **nearer** the branch, so the branch gets the lowest number."
- Replacement: "Rule four: number the main chain from the end **nearer** the branch, so the branch gets the lowest number, and everyone writes the same name." (The teaching checks already claim this cause.)

### M4. Minor: YouTube title over 100 characters

- Current title is 114 characters. Replacement (95): "IUPAC Naming of Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes: Skeletal Formulas and Locants | Year 12 Chemistry".
