Main question traced: *In 1377, Ragusa made travellers from plague areas wait 30 days before entering the city, with no idea what caused plague. In 1980, smallpox was declared eradicated. How did ideas about controlling disease change between these points, and how do you judge a strategy from the past, or from another culture, fairly?* Useful outcome: given any control strategy, old, culturally diverse or current, the student can say which transmission link it breaks, whether the evidence shows it worked (and what else could explain the change), and who bears the cost, then reach a judgement.

## Scope and focus

One comparison across time, judged by one three-question card. This is a good spine for an "evaluate" dotpoint: it turns a history list into a repeated judgement. The four idea chips give a timeline students can reconstruct. Exclusions are right (Eyam, the cat cull, the 1789 figures, traditional practices without a named source). Double-teaching check: b02's germ theory, b09's vaccine mechanism, b10's quarantine and incubation period, and b10's Semmelweis data are each recalled in a clause or tag. Snow is retold in more detail than b03's hook, but b03 explicitly hands his method and evidence to b14, and scene 5 adds new content (the evidence problem), so this is recall plus extension, not double-teaching. One conflict with b10 was found (finding 2). The video is at 566 s against the 570 s cap; the fixes need trims (finding 10).

## Strengths to keep

- **"Wrong about the mechanism, right about the intervention"** is taught causally: plague usually shows within a week, so 30 days catches infected travellers. Students see why a wrong theory can still give a working measure.
- **The three-question card** applies the same criteria to Ragusa, North Head, Snow, variolation, sewage testing and the APY Lands. This is exactly what "evaluate" needs, and it stops the culturally diverse cases being treated as a separate category.
- **The evidence question is real**: "deaths were already falling" teaches attribution with a case students half-know from b03.
- **Variolation's double edge** (helps the person, risks the town) is set up in scene 6 and tested in 12a.
- **North Head's "who pays"** names race and class plainly, without drawing people.
- **Culturally diverse origins are credited**: variolation from China, India, West Africa and the Ottoman Empire, and Onesimus named as the source of Boston's knowledge.
- **Both attempts are genuine HSC Section II items** (calculate and evaluate; explain), kept on screen through the countdown, with brief feedback (12b about 30 s, 12d about 25 s) and one crossed mistake each. 12c is a strong transfer item: the data rule out the passengers.
