Main question traced: *A chromosomal mutation can change hundreds of genes without spelling a single base wrong, so what exactly changes, and why does that affect a whole organism?* Useful outcome: given an HSC-style chromosome diagram, the student can name each structural mutation gene by gene, say which changes copy number and why, explain nondisjunction and trisomy 21 as extra dosage of normal genes, and compare point and chromosomal mutations on cause, process and effect.

## Strengths to keep

- **The hook is a real surprise with a real object.** Forty-seven chromosomes, and the genes on the extra one are normal. The painted metaphase spread shows what was seen; the coded karyotype carries the exact count (project manager decision (a), accepted).
- **One HSC-style board carries every structural type.** Genes A to H on one bar, normal above and changed below, is how exam papers draw it, and attempt 11a uses the same drawing, so the teaching transfers directly.
- **Causes before names.** The break and the out-of-step swap appear before "deletion" and "duplication"; a pair failing to separate appears before "nondisjunction".
- **Unequal crossing over makes a duplication and a deletion at once.** This is a genuine "aha" that only motion shows well.
- **The analogy carries its limit.** "A gene that's flipped still reads normally" stops students thinking an inversion garbles the genes inside it.
- **The extras earn their time.** Bar eye gives a real organism for gene dosage and links back to unequal crossing over (one sentence, one plate after the trim). The 9;22 leukaemia makes "a break can join two genes" concrete in one sentence. Trisomy 21 survivability tests the scale idea directly. Each is short and supports the main question, provided the fixes below are made.
- **People.** No human figures. The hook uses the microscope and the cell spread, never a child or patient.
