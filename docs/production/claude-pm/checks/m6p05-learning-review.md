## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "Two beakers both read pH 4.68: one is a buffer you've just made, the other is very dilute hydrochloric acid. How do you prepare a buffer, and what tests prove that it behaves like one?"

One main question: yes. Preparation, prediction, the four property tests and the VRA all serve "make it, then prove it by what it does". Useful outcome: a student can choose a pair and amounts for a target pH, prepare it accurately with enough volume, predict its pH, design tests with a water control, the same dose and fresh portions, interpret buffer-against-water data, and show that dilution barely changes the pH but cuts the capacity.

4. Measure: the offset from 4.74 is explained in a plain sentence without the word activity, and the crossed "should equal 4.74 exactly" chip pre-empts a real lab worry. Good.
5. Test design: control, same dose, separate portions, each with its reason ("base added after acid would just undo it"). Good. Screen load is high at "dose" (M1).
6. Results: tiny teal bars against long pale blue bars on a shared axis is the core visual and it works. "resists, not prevents" is placed after the evidence. The student may ask why water rose 5.70 but fell only 3.58 (M2).

9. Capacity: a tenth of the partners, the same dose, ethanoate used up, pH down 1.31: the cause is clear, and the bar beside the original −0.09 bar makes the point visually. Applies C11's capacity idea to a bench test rather than re-teaching it. Good.

12. Risk: real classifications; supplier wording needs the same fix as P04 (science check S2).
13. Memory aid: "Split, dose, compare" is the method in three words, with a condition tag. Good.
14 to 17. Attempts and feedback: see the checklist.

## Findings

No blockers.

### S1. Should fix: scene 7 re-teaches C11's worked example

- Exact text: "Why so little? Each buffer portion holds zero point zero zero one zero zero moles of each partner, and the acid dose is **one tenth** of that. Ethanoate takes up the added hydronium, so the ethanoic acid goes up and the ethanoate goes down. Using hydronium equals K a times acid over base, the **ratio** moves from one to about one point two, and the predicted pH falls from four point seven four to **four point six six**. That's the same small drop you measured."
- Student question: "This is the buffers video's example again, isn't it?"
- Problem: C11 scene 6 used the same dose-to-partner ratio (one tenth), the same ratio 1.2 and the same 4.74 → 4.66. Here it is the longest scene but one (452 characters) and adds only the scale. The practical's new idea is that the prediction matches the measurement, not the calculation itself.
- Replacement: "Why so little? Each buffer portion holds zero point zero zero one zero zero moles of each partner, and the acid dose is only **one tenth** of that. It's the buffers video's example at a tenth of the scale: ethanoate takes up the hydronium, the **ratio** moves from one to about one point two, and the predicted pH falls to **four point six six**. That's the same small drop you measured." (about 340 characters). Visual: keep the task line, the equation strip (grey, "buffers video") and the grouped givens; write the ratio line and the [H₃O⁺] line quickly in grey, then bright "pH = 4.66" and the kept chip. Drop the separate C11 equation write.
- Understanding enabled: the student sees the formula used as an analysis tool for their own data, not re-derived, and the saved time pays for S3.

### S2. Should fix: scene 8 opens with a contrast that is false

- Exact text: "Now a property water can't copy."
- Student question: "If I dilute water with water, doesn't its pH stay the same too?"
- Problem: it does. Water cannot be the comparison for dilution; the scene itself (rightly) compares with P04's diluted HCl, which moved a whole unit. A student who notices the slip may doubt the control logic just taught in scene 5.
- Replacement: "Now a property an unbuffered acid can't copy." Visual unchanged (the P04 chip is the comparison).
- Understanding enabled: the student knows which control fits which test: water for added acid and base, an unbuffered acid for dilution.

### S3. Should fix: the hook's dilute HCl is never paid off in the narration

- Exact text, hook: "The other is very **dilute hydrochloric acid**. Which is which?" Scene 11 holds only a silent chip.
- Student question: "So what would the hydrochloric acid have done?"
- Problem: the question that earns the video's attention is never answered aloud. The answer is the best evidence the design has: an unbuffered solution at the same pH plunges just like water.
- Replacement, scene 11 (after "every test uses a fresh portion."): "An even closer control is that dilute hydrochloric acid from the start: same pH, same dose, and it would plunge to about **two point three**." Visual: the existing grey chip becomes bright on "two point three", with the hook's beaker Y small and a long pale blue bar "4.68 → 2.32".
- Understanding enabled: the hook closes; the student sees that the same starting pH gives completely different behaviour, which is the definition the hook promised.

### M1. Minor: bright-item counts in scenes 5 and 11

- Scene 5, at "dose": four beaker labels, two column headers, the control term and two dose tags. Write the beaker labels and headers, then dim them to grey before the dose tags; dim the control term when "dose" starts.
- Scene 11: card, grey chip, small beaker Y, the two-reading offset chip and the footer. Dim the validity card and its chip once the reliability card writes (as designed), and keep the footer grey.

### M2. Minor: scene 6, water's unequal changes

- Student question: "Why did water rise 5.70 but fall only 3.58?" The narration gives the cause of the 5.93 start but not its effect.
- Replacement, scene 6: "The water started at five point nine three, because of dissolved carbon dioxide, so it had further to rise than to fall. It **plunged** to two point three five with acid, and shot up to eleven point six three with base." (about 40 characters longer; offset by M3 or the author's scene 4 reserve cut).

### M3. Minor: scene 3, "so fifty would run out"

- 50 mL covers the three draws exactly, with nothing for rinsing or a re-test.
- Replacement: "each set of tests uses about seventy millilitres, so fifty would leave nothing for rinsing or a re-test."

### M4. Minor: key note 5 is long

- Split: "5. Validity: water control, same dose and volume, fresh portions. Reliability: fresh batches agree. Accuracy: calibrated meter, pipettes; comparing changes cancels a constant offset." Move risk to note 6: "6. Risk: dilute solutions below limits (glasses still); 0.100 mol/L NaOH can irritate; glacial ethanoic acid flammable and corrosive, teacher only; pipette filler." Six short notes copy more easily than five long ones.

## Retained strengths

"A buffer is what it does" as the frame; the site's 50 mL correction turned into a planning lesson; the crossed "should equal 4.74 exactly" chip; separate portions justified by "base after acid just undoes it"; the shared-axis bars; dilution and capacity tested in two scenes from one diluted flask; "Split, dose, compare" as a method aid distinct from C11's chemistry aid; A1 that tests method rather than arithmetic; brief feedback.
