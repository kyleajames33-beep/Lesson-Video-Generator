Main question traced: *Each new generation is full of variation, so where does it come from, and how do meiosis and fertilisation share the work with mutation?* Useful outcome: given a change between parents and offspring, the student can say whether it needed a new allele (mutation) or only a new combination (crossing over, independent assortment or fertilisation), and can judge "which matters most" by timescale (one generation against the long term).

## Strengths to keep

- **One amber bead carries the middle of the video.** Following one new allele from a sperm, to a carrier, to half its gametes, to new neighbours and finally to a "dd" box makes "making against shuffling" visible on one object. The amber-only-for-new rule (B8, b01) lets a student predict which bead to watch. This is the video doing what text cannot.
- **The overlap with B8 and B5b is handled as recall.** Scene 2 is three sentences; crossing over and assortment are one sentence each on B5b's own board; the deck of cards returns as the aid without its mapping being rebuilt. The new work (one allele's journey, the per-generation count against the long-term source, the bacterial limit) is enough for a full video.
- **The hook is answered.** The drought shows selection acting on variation already present; scene 7 closes the loop ("The finches' beak alleles were made by mutations long before the drought").
- **The comparison in scene 6 is evidence, not assertion.** A measured count of new mutations per child against more than eight million combinations per parent gives students something quotable.
- **The "if mutation stopped" thought experiment** (scene 7) gives the ultimate source a cause a student can reconstruct: a fixed set of alleles, and losses are permanent.
- **The attempts are genuine transfer.** 10a uses the carrier by non-carrier cross, which scene 5 never works out, so the 1/2 must be derived; 10c reverses the site's claim and needs a verdict.
