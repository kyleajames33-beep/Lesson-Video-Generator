## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "Ammonia is clearly a base, but it contains no hydroxide. Why did chemists keep changing what 'acid' and 'base' mean, and how does proton transfer explain what the older models couldn't?"

One main question: yes. Ammonia carries it from the hook (1) through the failure (6), the fix (10), the gas tube (11) and attempt 2. The history (3 to 5) and the Brønsted-Lowry limit (13) both serve the same "what broke each model" thread. Useful outcome: a student can follow the proton to label the acid and the base in an unseen equation, and can assess a model by "what it explained, then what broke it".

## Trace from the entry knowledge (student starting Year 12 Module 6)

Entry: Year 11 formulas, polyatomic ions, balancing, states, litmus and neutralisation; Module 5 double arrow. The trace below follows ammonia.

1. Hook: litmus turns blue, NH₃ has no O. A Year 11 student can see this. Good puzzle; the crossed "OH⁻?" chip is a clear, visible search.
2. Models: "a model is a definition that predicts" is given a plain meaning before use. Good.
3 and 4. Lavoisier and Davy: each idea is shown on the formula tiles (O glows red, H glows teal), and each failure is a visible counterexample (HCl, CH₄). Causes are clear. Name cards only, no portraits. Good.
5. Arrhenius: **this is where a careful student will stop.** The narration defines an Arrhenius base as one that "produces **hydroxide ions** in water". Scene 10 then shows that ammonia solution does contain OH⁻ produced in water. A student holding scene 5's wording will reasonably say "so ammonia is an Arrhenius base". Scene 6 then switches to a different rule ("releases **its own** hydroxide ions") that was never stated as part of the definition. The main question depends on this step. See blocker B1.
6. Ammonia and NH₄OH: clear and visual (split panel: NaOH releasing its own OH⁻ against NH₃ with nothing to release). Good, once scene 5 carries the "its own OH⁻ / dissociates" idea.
7. Carbonates and the gas tube: clear. "A definition that needs a solution in water can't even start" is a good plain cause.
8. Brønsted-Lowry: "Stop asking which ions appear in water. Ask what moves" is a strong conversational turn. The proton is explained (hydrogen atom without its electron) before it is relied on.
9. HCl in water: proton hop shown on screen; hydronium named with meaning. The crossed "HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ (for a Brønsted-Lowry answer)" is a useful contrast with scene 5.
10. Ammonia in water: the hop from water to N and the "left from water" ring answer the hook exactly. The equilibrium callback is one clause. Module 5 is not re-taught.
11. Gas tube and carbonate: same hop reused, no water present. Good transfer.
12. Roles: the two water equations stacked with violet and amber outlines is a genuine video advantage (same molecule, two roles, side by side). Rule card stated after the comparison. Good.
13. Brønsted-Lowry limit: the student is told CaO and SO₃ "look like a base meeting an acid" with no reason why a Year 12 student would think so. See S2.
14. Memory aid: causal and reconstructable ("each model fixed a failure of the one before"), checked against the lesson. Good.
15 to 18. Attempts and feedback: see below.
19, 20. Decision rule and notes: separate from new reasoning; quiet copy hold. Good.

## Findings

### B1. Blocker: scene 5 defines an Arrhenius base in a way ammonia satisfies

- Exact text (scene 5): "He said an acid is a substance that produces **hydrogen ions** when it dissolves in water, and a base produces **hydroxide ions** in water."
- Student question: "Ammonia solution has OH⁻ in it (you show that in scene 10). So why isn't ammonia an Arrhenius base?" As written, scene 6's "its own" reads as a new rule added to make ammonia fail.
- Repair: put the dissociation idea into the definition, so the limitation follows from it.
- Replacement (scene 5, about the same length): "He said an acid is a substance that breaks apart in water to release **hydrogen ions**, and a base breaks apart in water to release its own **hydroxide ions**."
- Carry the same wording to the Arrhenius card (scene 5 visual): replace "acid makes H⁺ in water; base makes OH⁻ in water" with "acid releases H⁺ in water; base releases its own OH⁻". And key note 3: replace "an acid produces H⁺ in water; a base produces OH⁻ in water" with "an acid releases H⁺ in water; a base releases its own OH⁻ in water".
- Understanding enabled: the student sees that Arrhenius required the OH⁻ to come out of the base itself, so ammonia (and carbonates) fail for a stated reason, and scene 10's "the hydroxide comes from water" lands as the decisive contrast.

### S1. Should fix: scene 6 states a reversed piece of history

- Exact text (scene 6): "To make it fit, chemists wrote ammonia solution as "**ammonium hydroxide**", N H four O H."
- Problem: the NH₄OH formula predates Arrhenius; it was written in the 19th century so ammonia would fit "acid + base (hydroxide) → salt + water" (Cosandey, CHIMIA 2021, cited in the science check). Under Arrhenius, chemists kept using it. A student is not harmed by the teaching point, but the sentence implies the formula was invented for Arrhenius.
- Replacement: "To make it fit, chemists kept writing ammonia solution as "**ammonium hydroxide**", N H four O H."
- Understanding kept: the model needed a compound that was never isolated.

### S2. Should fix: scene 13 gives no reason to see CaO and SO₃ as a base and an acid

- Exact text (scene 13): "It looks like a base meeting an acid."
- Student question: "Why? Neither one is on the litmus list." Year 12 students have not been told calcium oxide is a basic oxide or that sulfur trioxide is an acidic oxide.
- Replacement: "In water, these oxides make a base and an acid."
- Understanding enabled: the student accepts the reaction as acid-base behaviour first, so "no hydrogen, so no proton can move" reads as a real limit, not a trick.

### S3. Should fix: attempt 1 feedback uses a name not yet taught

- Exact text (scene 16): "The **hydrogen carbonate ion** becomes carbonic acid, so it **gained** a proton."
- Problem: "carbonic acid" is taught in C02, not C01; the equation on screen shows H₂CO₃. A student who wrote "H₂CO₃" may wonder whether they missed something.
- Replacement: "The **hydrogen carbonate ion** becomes H two C O three, so it **gained** a proton."

### M1. Minor: scene 4 sentence adds little

- Exact text (scene 4): "That fits hydrochloric acid, and the others too."
- The visual (four tiles, every H glowing) already says this. Cut it (49 characters) to pay for B1. Optional replacement if a bridge is wanted: "That fits all four acids."

### M2. Minor: scene 2 "So let's follow the failures." repeats the previous clause

- Exact text (scene 2): "and each new model fixed the failure of the one before. So let's follow the failures."
- Replacement: "and each new model fixed the failure of the one before."

### M3. Minor: scene 5 lens should show OH⁻ entering before "Mix them"

- Visual text (scene 5): "on "hydroxide ions" a second line "NaOH → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)"."
- Replacement: "on "hydroxide ions" a second line "NaOH → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)" and violet OH⁻ tokens enter the lens."

### M4. Minor: crowded frames in scenes 9 and 13

- Scene 9 visual: "beneath it a chip "HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ (for a Brønsted-Lowry answer)" fades in and is crossed". Let the "hydronium ion" label dim before this chip appears so at most the equation plus one chip is bright.
- Scene 13 visual: "As the sentence ends, all four cards show their stamps together." Shrink the CaO equation to the strip first; replacement: "As the sentence ends, the equation shrinks and all four cards show their stamps together."
