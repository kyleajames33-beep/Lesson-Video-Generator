## Key notes (suggested wording)

1. A DNA nucleotide has three parts: a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base (A, T, C or G). Strong covalent bonds join the sugars and phosphates into each backbone, and the bases point inward.
2. In DNA, A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and C pairs with G (three). This is complementary base pairing. The two strands are antiparallel: one runs 5' to 3' and its partner runs 3' to 5'.
3. Replication: helicase separates the strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds. Each original strand is a template. Free nucleotides pair with the exposed bases, and DNA polymerase joins them into a new strand with covalent bonds.
4. Semi-conservative replication: each new DNA molecule has one strand it was copied from and one new strand. The first original strands are never copied twice; they are shared out. To explain accurate copying: weak hydrogen bonds let the strands separate and act as templates, the pairing rule decides each new base, and each daughter keeps an old strand.
5. Accuracy comes from the pairing rule. Proofreading and repair fix most mistakes. A mistake that remains is a mutation. It passes to every cell that divides from that cell, and in an organism that reproduces sexually it reaches offspring only through a cell that makes gametes.
6. To trace a copy: pair A with T and C with G, label old and new, and run the new strand opposite its template. Aids: Apple in the Tree, Car in the Garage (in DNA). Half old, half new (old = the strand it was copied from).

### BLOCKER

1. **6b and key note 5 say a mutation reaches "both new cells" of a splitting bacterium. This contradicts the lesson's own model.** Scene 6a shows a wrong tile added to the new strand of one daughter molecule. By 4b and 5d the student knows the two daughter molecules are different copies, so only one of them carries the error. A student who traces this (the lesson teaches them to) will see the contradiction, and the visual tag "passes to both new cells" teaches the wrong idea.
