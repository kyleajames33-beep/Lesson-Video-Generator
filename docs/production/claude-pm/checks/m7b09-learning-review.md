What changed after the first infection, and how do vaccines and antibody injections give protection?* Useful outcome: the student can explain why the secondary response is faster and bigger, read a primary and secondary graph (including a new antigen), explain how a vaccine and a booster work, and classify and choose between active and passive immunity, natural and artificial.

## Response strip continuity (b06 to b09)

Coherent. The strip appears once, complete (b06's innate rungs, b07's B column, b08's T column), with teal memory rings at the foot of both adaptive columns in scene 3, which matches b07 scene 8 ("memory B cells: stay for later", grey tag "b09") and b08 scene 10 (memory T cells ringed, grey tag "b09"). ## Strengths to keep

- **The hook sets up a real surprise** ("the antibody level has usually fallen ... So what's stopping the virus?") and the "leftover antibodies?" chip is answered within one scene.
- **The graph and the cell field change together.** Each part of the curve has a visible cause: two or three lit cells before, many after; plasma cells fading as the curve falls; a short clone tree against a long one. This is the strongest use of the medium in the video.
- **The different-antigen trap** (scene 5 and attempt 1) tests "same antigen" directly, which is the exam's favourite twist.
- **The two-by-two table builds one quadrant per cue** with a painted thumbnail each, and the two questions ("who made the antibodies?", "how?") generate it rather than asking students to memorise four boxes.
- **Antivenom is a well-chosen Australian case**, and attempt 2 makes students separate "had antibodies" from "has memory".
- **Both memory aids come after their explanations**, and their limits are tested (scene 5 and attempt 1 for "faster, bigger, longer").
- **Herd immunity is moved out cleanly**, keeping one question.
