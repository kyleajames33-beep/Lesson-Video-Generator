## Facts confirmed (no change)

- **Boiling points (CRC and PubChem).** Propane −42.1 °C; trimethylamine 2.9 °C ("about three"); propan-1-amine 47.2 to 48 °C; propan-1-ol 97.2 °C; propanoic acid 141.2 °C; propanamide 213 °C; ethanamide 221 °C (the site's 220 °C is fine); ethanamine 16.6 °C; dimethylamine 6.9 °C (the site's 7.4 °C is high but not used in learner copy); butan-1-amine 77 to 78 °C; N-ethylethanamine 55.5 °C; N,N-dimethylethanamine 36 to 38 °C; N,N-dimethylethanamide 165 °C. The "+72 °C" gap (213 − 141) is right.
- **Melting points.** Ethanamide 79 to 81 °C; propanamide 79 to 81 °C; methanamide about 2.5 °C. The tag "ethanamide and propanamide: solids, melting at about 80 °C" is right, and leaving methanamide out is correct.
- **Molar masses (IUPAC atomic weights, by script).** C₃H₉N 59.11; C₃H₈O 60.10; C₃H₇NO 73.09; C₃H₆O₂ 74.08; C₄H₁₁N 73.14 g/mol. **Electron counts** 34, 34, 34, 40, 40 and 42 are right. In both matched pairs the electron counts are equal, so dispersion cannot explain either gap.
- **Formulas and counts (attempt 1).** CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂, CH₃CH₂NHCH₂CH₃ and CH₃CH₂N(CH₃)₂ are all C₄H₁₁N. N–H counts are 2, 1, 0 and carbon groups on N are 1, 2, 3. The boiling point order 78 > 55 > 37 °C follows the N–H count.
- **Names (IUPAC 2013).** Ethanamine, propan-1-amine, propan-2-amine (primary), N-methylethanamine, N,N-dimethylethanamine, N,N-dimethylmethanamine (trimethylamine), butan-1-amine, N-ethylethanamine, ethanamide, propanamide, N-methylethanamide. All correct. The amide C1 rule ("no number") is right for monoamides. "Ethylammonium", "trimethylammonium" and "methylammonium ethanoate" are the usual HSC names. Their IUPAC forms (ethanaminium, N,N-dimethylmethanaminium, methanaminium ethanoate; author note c) are right.
- **Classification.** Counting carbon groups on N is the correct rule. Propan-2-amine is primary, and the crossed chip "secondary, like propan-2-ol" is a fair, real trap.
- **Equations (every one balanced by atom count and charge).** CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ (C 2/2, H 9/9, N 1/1, O 1/1, charge 0/0). CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻ (H 8/8, Cl 1/1). (CH₃)₃N + H⁺ → (CH₃)₃NH⁺ (charge +1/+1). CH₃COOH + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃NH₃⁺CH₃COO⁻ (C 3/3, H 9/9, N 1/1, O 2/2). CH₃COOH + H₂NCH₃ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + H₂O (C 3/3, H 9/9, N 1/1, O 2/2; finding 1 adds the condition). CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₃⁺ + OH⁻ (H 11/11). Key note 4's general forms balance.
- **Base strength (author note d).** pKa of the conjugate acids: ethylammonium 10.65 to 10.7, methylammonium 10.6, trimethylammonium 9.80, ammonium 9.25 (CRC). Kb for ethanamine is about 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ (from 10.65). At 0.10 mol/L the quadratic gives [OH⁻] = 6.5 × 10⁻³ mol/L, pH 11.8, 6.5% protonated, which is about 1 in 15. The 15-icon lens with 1 ion pair matches. "Equilibrium lies well to the left" and "weak base" are right. Alkylamines are slightly stronger bases than ammonia, and "organic ammonia" does not claim equal strength.
- **Amide basicity and acidity.** Protonated ethanamide pKa is about −0.5 (Kb about 3 × 10⁻¹⁵). The site's "Kb ~10⁻¹⁵" is a fair rounding. Ethanamide N–H pKa is about 15 in water (25.5 in DMSO, Bordwell), so the solution is close to neutral. The "pair share" explanation (lone pair delocalised into the C=O; C–N about 1.33 Å in amides against 1.47 Å in amines) is the standard reason and meets the site's Must Do via the grey tag. "The same C double bond O effect" as the carboxylate is a fair link, because both are delocalisation through a C=O.
- **Intermolecular forces.** N less electronegative than O, so N–H is less polar and amine hydrogen bonds are weaker. Tertiary amines have no N–H donors but accept hydrogen bonds from water. Trimethylamine is very soluble in water (PubChem: about 890 g/L at 19 °C). Primary amide networks against acid dimers is the standard HSC contrast. "Longer chain, more dispersion" is right within each series.
- **Fish and lemon.** Trimethylamine forms by bacterial reduction of trimethylamine N-oxide in spoiling marine fish, and it is a major contributor to the smell. "Much of that smell" is correctly hedged. Citric acid (pKa₁ 3.13) is far stronger than trimethylammonium (pKa 9.8), so proton transfer is favoured. "Turning much of it into a salt" is hedged correctly.
- **Acid against base (scene 10).** Ethanoic acid (pKa 4.76) and methylammonium (pKa 10.6) give K about 10⁶ for proton transfer, so the salt forms essentially completely at room temperature with no water. A strong heat is needed to dehydrate the salt to an amide. Correct.
- **Litmus.** Propanoic acid turns blue litmus red; propan-1-amine turns red litmus blue; propanamide changes neither. Correct (finding 3 adjusts the cause wording).
- **Memory aids.** "Amines are organic ammonia" and "Amine: free pair, a base. Amide: shared pair, barely a base." are both true as worded. Finding 6 qualifies the boiling point line.
- **Absolute words.** "only one carbon group" (correct); "no N H at all" (correct for trimethylamine); "can't hydrogen bond to each other" (correct for tertiary amines; they can still accept from water, which the next sentence says); "in every series, longer chains add dispersion" (correct); "C has none" (correct); "No water forms" (correct at room temperature); "can't take a proton" (finding 4).
- **Ethanamide with HCl (requested judgement).** At room temperature, aqueous HCl protonates only a small fraction of ethanamide. With pKa −0.5 the fraction is about 3% in 0.1 mol/L HCl and about 25% in 1 mol/L, ignoring activity effects. No salt crystallises from water. Heated with aqueous acid, the amide hydrolyses: CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O + HCl → CH₃COOH + NH₄Cl (C 2/2, H 8/8, N 1/1, O 2/2, Cl 1/1). With dry HCl in a non-aqueous solvent, acetamide does form an isolable hydrochloride (for example (CH₃CONH₂)₂·HCl), so "no stable salt" must not be stated absolutely (site error 8 below). The design leaves hydrolysis out, so no learner copy changes.
