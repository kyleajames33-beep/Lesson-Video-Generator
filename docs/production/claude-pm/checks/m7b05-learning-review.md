Main question traced: *Plants can't send immune cells to an infection. So how does a banksia defend its roots against Phytophthora, and why does one species hold out while another dies?* Useful outcome: for a named plant and pathogen, the student can say how the pathogen gets in, sort each defence as always there or switched on, explain the hypersensitive response as a firebreak, read inoculation evidence for timing and name its limits.

## Strengths to keep

- **The defence ladder and the aligned root-cell pair are the lesson's best visual idea.** Each induced defence is revealed in place on the same tissue (callose under the hypha tip, thicker neighbouring walls, deposits, then the dead ring), so the student sees what changed and where.
- **Scene 2 corrects "it's a fungus" with a cause** (cellulose walls, a different group) and traces symptoms back to roots ("less water reaches the leaves").
- **The weak point is explained, not asserted:** the young zone behind the tip is least protected, and that is where the spores settle.
- **The firebreak is earned before it is named** (scene 6), and the memory line adds the condition that matters ("speed decides whether the break holds").
- **A real secondary-sourced investigation.** The race strip shows published observations at the ticks the abstract reports, with no invented values between ticks, then the limits. This is the strongest p03 fold-in so far.
- **Control matches the route** (soil movement; phosphite does not clean the soil).
- **Both attempts are genuine transfer.** 12a reads unseen data; 12c evaluates a claim, uses a fungal pathogen (one of the syllabus's listed examples) and tests the HR misconception.
