## Facts confirmed (no change)

- **Florida panther numbers.** Johnson et al. (Science 2010;329:1641, "Genetic restoration of the Florida panther") describe a population isolated in southern Florida that had fallen to fewer than 30 adults by the early 1990s, with low genetic variation and inbreeding correlates. "About twenty to thirty ... by the early 1990s": correct (the University of Florida release gives about 25 at the time of the introduction; another review gives "< 30 adults"). "Cut off in the swamps of southern Florida" (Big Cypress and the Everglades): correct. Sources: https://archive.news.ufl.edu/articles/2010/09/study-florida-panther-population-in-better-shape-than-before-still-a-long-way.html, https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/UW402, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6829145.
- **The 1995 release.** Eight female pumas from Texas were released in south Florida in 1995; five bred, producing at least 20 F1 kittens (12 litters). "Eight female Texas pumas": correct. Same sources; Seal and Lacy 1994 planned the introduction.
- **Population afterwards.** The Johnson abstract: panther numbers increased threefold, genetic heterozygosity doubled, survival and fitness measures improved, and inbreeding correlates declined significantly. Population estimates reached about 100 to 120 by 2007 to 2008 (McBride et al. 2008, cited by UF/IFAS), so "Within about a decade, the panther population had roughly tripled" is correct. The design's later screen tag "variety restored: about double" follows the abstract (author note: a UF/IFAS summary gives average heterozygosity rising from 16 to 24 per cent, which is a 1.5-fold rise in that measure; the Codex check should confirm which measure "doubled" refers to before any number is put on screen). A 2024 follow-up (Scientific Reports) reports the benefits persisting after five generations of admixture. Source: https://nsuworks.nova.edu/cnso_bio_facarticles/455 (abstract record).
- **Inbreeding traits.** Kinked tail tips, cowlicks, cryptorchidism, sperm abnormalities and atrial septal defects were documented in pre-1995 panthers (Roelke et al. 1993; Johnson et al. 2010). After admixture these became less frequent (atrial septal defects 7 per cent and cryptorchidism 3 per cent in necropsies from 2013 to 2018), and admixed kittens survived better (Hostetler et al., Biological Conservation 2010; USGS record https://www.usgs.gov/index.php/publications/genetic-introgression-and-survival-florida-panther-kittens). "Kinked tails became less common, the kittens survived better, and the population grew": correct.
- **Isolation and species.** Pumas were eliminated from most of the eastern and southern United States by the early twentieth century, leaving the Florida population isolated from western populations; both are *Puma concolor* (since the 2017 revision all North American pumas are one subspecies, *P. c. couguar*, which supports "same species"). "The pumas that once lived between Florida and Texas had been wiped out": correct. The genetic basis of each defect is not established and is not claimed (the design's author note is right).
- **Scene 2 count.** 25 × 2 = 50 copies; 10 / 50 = 0.2. Illustrative (spoken as "If ten of those copies"). Correct.
- **Coin statistics.** P(exactly 7 heads in 10 fair tosses) = 120/1024 = 0.117; P(7 or more) = 176/1024 = 0.172 (recomputed). "Seven heads isn't unusual": correct, and the site's "commonly give seventy per cent heads" is rightly corrected (site correction 8). 1,000 tosses: standard deviation √250 = 15.8 heads; P(470 to 530 heads) = 0.946, so "you'll land close to half" is correct. The screen's seeded run landing within 0.49 to 0.51 has a probability of about 0.49 for a random seed, so a chosen seed is fine and is honest as one run.
- **Drift and population size.** The per-generation sampling variance of an allele frequency is p(1 − p) / 2N, so drift is larger in small populations; alleles can be lost or fixed; changes accumulate because each generation's frequency is the next one's starting point. Correct (OpenStax 19.2). The seeded Wright-Fisher lines at N = 20 and N = 500 over 50 generations match the site's simulator settings.
- **Bottleneck and founder effect.** Both are drift from a small sample of the original gene pool. Correct (OpenStax 19.2). "Harmful alleles became common just by chance" and "With so many relatives breeding, more kittens got two copies of a harmful allele, so its effect showed": correct as the general mechanism of inbreeding depression (Frankham et al., ch. 12), consistent with the panther literature.
- **Mutation.** A new mutation enters as one copy among 2N: 1/50 = 0.02 and 1/2,000,000 = 5 × 10⁻⁷ (recomputed). Per-gene mutation rates are roughly 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁶ per generation. A new neutral allele's chance of eventual fixation is 1/2N, so in 25 animals about 49 in 50 are eventually lost, and under Poisson offspring numbers about 37 per cent are lost in the very first generation. "Mutations at any one gene are rare", "drift loses most new alleles before they spread": correct.
- **Gene flow.** Migrants that breed move alleles between populations, can restore alleles a population lacked, reduce differences between populations and create no new alleles. Correct (OpenStax 19.2).
- **Which matters most.** Drift dominates short-term change in small isolated populations; gene flow can dominate where migration is high; only mutation makes new alleles and matters over long times; selection is non-random with respect to fitness. Correct. Site correction 3 (the ranking does not simply reverse in a large connected population) is right.
- **Attempt 1 (9a, 9b).** 10 × 2 = 20 copies; 4 / 20 = 0.2, double 0.1. If the ten survivors were a random sample, the expected count is 2 copies; P(4 or more | n = 20, p = 0.1) = 0.133 (recomputed), so 4 copies is plausible by chance and drift (a bottleneck) is the justified answer given "survival was unrelated to this gene". "Founder effect" is correctly not accepted (no new population was founded elsewhere).
- **Attempt 2 (9c, 9d).** A hypothetical population; one new copy in 60 for 30 possums; gene flow as genetic rescue is supported by meta-analysis (Frankham, Molecular Ecology 2015;24:2610) and by an Australian case (mountain pygmy possum at Mt Buller, Weeks et al., Nature Communications 2017;8:1071). Outbreeding depression as a possible limit (newcomers adapted to a different environment) is rightly accepted in the model answer and not spoken. "The added alleles still face drift": correct.
- **Memory aids.** "Drift is luck, and luck matters more in small groups": correct (variance falls with 2N); its limit (luck has no direction) is spoken in scene 3. "Bottleneck vs founder effect: few individuals, so the gene pool is a random sample": correct. The gene-flow aid: see finding 5.
- **Site corrections 1 to 10** in the design are scientifically right (the Lake Victoria cichlid opsin divergence is the standard case of divergent natural and sexual selection, Seehausen et al., Nature 2008;455:620; drift acts in every finite population; the ranking does not reverse; the typhoon case is a bottleneck and 1 copy in 40 is 0.025; the bank item needs the "survival unrelated" condition; the 2002 Grant paper did not measure a mutation rate; seven or more heads is about one time in six; resistance raises survival rather than guaranteeing it; the five simulator values average 0.406).
