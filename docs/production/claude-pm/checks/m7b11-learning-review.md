Main question traced: *Antibiotics do nothing for the flu, but a flu antiviral can shorten it, if it's taken early. Why do antibiotics and antivirals work so differently, and how effective is each?* Useful outcome: given a drug and a pathogen, the student can say what the drug hits that the host lacks, explain why penicillin bursts growing bacteria and spares our cells, why an antibiotic has nothing to hit in a virus (and still selects resistant bacteria), why an antiviral must block a virus-only step and so works best early, why resistance is selected rather than created and spreads on plasmids, why three drugs at once stop HIV resistance, and judge each class by selectivity, breadth and how long it keeps working.

## Scope and focus

One question, answered in two halves (how each class works; how effective each is). The split decision is sound: resistance and combination therapy are the "assess" half, not a second topic, and the design keeps resistance mechanisms, the S. aureus table and the pipeline out. The handoff to p05 is clean, and the video does not teach disc diffusion. The densest beats are scene 3 (two targets plus kill against stop-growth) and scene 10 (the assess table); both have dimming rules. At 564 s the video has about 6 s of headroom.

## Strengths to keep

- **The hook is a genuine puzzle with evidence.** "One drug misses completely; the other only helps if you take it early" sets up both halves of the video, and the day strip with its 0 to 2 bracket returns in scene 6 to pay it off.
- **Effects are shown as changes in place.** The same bacterium bursts (scene 3) and the same airway cell keeps its new viruses stuck (scene 5). Students can point at the thing that changed and compare it with its earlier state.
- **Scene 4 answers the "so what?" of antibiotics for a cold.** The population of throat and gut bacteria with two ringed glyphs that take over turns a "no effect" statement into a cause of resistance.
- **"Viruses bring a few tools of their own"** resolves the contradiction a student would otherwise feel after scene 4 ("it uses your cell's ribosomes"), and gives the reason antivirals are possible at all.
- **Scene 6 derives "early, narrow, few" from the mechanism** rather than listing it: they stop new copies, so early; each blocks one virus's enzyme, so narrow; viruses rely on our cells, so safe targets are rare.
- **Attempt 2 is the best beat in the video.** The student calculates the size of the resistant subpopulation and the triple-resistance rate, and the feedback converts 10⁻⁵ a day into "fewer than one a century". It explains combination therapy with numbers, which is the "assess" verb.
- **Recall is disciplined.** b01's rule is one grey tag and one sentence; bio-m6-b01's selection is two sentences on its own board; plasmids get a five-word meaning.
