## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "Stomachs, soils, chimneys and mine creeks can all suffer from too much acid. How do you choose a base to neutralise each one, and what does the reaction actually do?"

One main question: yes. The four-plate hook sets one problem (too much acid) and one decision (which base). The choice card (safe, cheap, gentle, what it leaves behind) is introduced in scene 2 and lit in each setting, and the decision rule (problem, treatment, equation, effect) answers the dotpoint's "investigate applications" in an exam-usable form. Useful outcome: for an unseen application a student can name the problem and its cause, justify a base, write the equation and say what happens to the pH and what is left behind.

## Trace from the entry knowledge (student starting Year 12 Module 6)

Entry: C02 acid + hydroxide and acid + carbonate patterns and salt formulas; C01 base as a proton acceptor and carbonate as a proton acceptor; pH as "lower is more acidic, seven neutral"; Fe(OH)₃ as a brown precipitate (chem-m5-c13).

1. Hook: four real problems, NaOH crossed on each. Good puzzle; "you can't just pour sodium hydroxide into a stomach" earns the choice question.
2. Neutralising and the four questions: neutralise gets a plain meaning; the pH marker overshooting to 12 makes "gently" concrete. Good.
3. Stomach: "caustic" gets a meaning; "barely dissolve, so they react mainly with the acid they meet" is a plain cause for gentleness. The CO₂ burp is memorable. **"Magnesium hydroxide, from last video"**: in playlist order the previous video is not C02 (C04 is order 5, after C03 and P02). A student watching in order will look for magnesium hydroxide in the calorimetry video (S1). Scene 4 has the same phrase.
4. Paddock: the two causes of acidity and the aluminium mechanism are explained in plain words. Good.
5. Lime: the carbonate hop and split reuse C01 and C02 motion; "barely dissolves unless there's acid" explains why lime does not overshoot. Screen load is high: the term card, the lens, the marble plate with its own tag, the strip, the pH marker, a tag and three lit choice words (S3).
6. Chimney: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ is taught clearly in a droplet. Good.
7. Scrubber: **this is where a careful student will stop.** Scene 6 has just shown that sulfur dioxide in water makes sulfurous acid. Scene 7 then sprays SO₂ into a calcium hydroxide *slurry*, "a solid base stirred through water", and says "Sulfur dioxide has no hydrogen to donate, so this isn't a proton transfer." The student will ask: "There's water in the slurry, and you just told me SO₂ plus water makes an acid. So isn't that acid giving its hydrogen ions to the hydroxide?" They are right (science check S1). As written, the scene teaches a wrong idea and contradicts the previous scene. See B1. The screen also plans about seven text items (S3).
8. Mine drainage: cause and harm explained in plain words. Good.
9. Hydrated lime: neutralise, then precipitate as the pH rises, with the C13 precipitate motion. Good mechanism. But the main question is "how do you choose", and scene 9 never says why the plant uses hydrated lime when scene 5 just praised limestone for being gentle. A student will ask "why not limestone again?" (S2).
10. Memory aid: "Every fix leaves something behind" connects to the "leaves behind" choice criterion (burp gas, collectable solid, sludge). It earns its place (M3). The screen plans four tags, the aid, the frame and a condition tag (S4).
11 to 14. Attempts and feedback: see below.
15, 16. Decision rule and notes: separate from new reasoning; quiet copy hold. Key note 4 repeats the scene 7 error ("no proton transfer").

## Findings

### B1. Blocker: scene 7 says the scrubber reaction is not a proton transfer, right after scene 6 showed SO₂ making an acid in water

- Exact text (scene 7): "Sulfur dioxide has no hydrogen to donate, so this isn't a proton transfer."
- Student question: "The slurry is wet, and SO₂ in water makes sulfurous acid (scene 6). So the hydroxide is neutralising an acid's hydrogen ions, isn't it?"
- Repair: keep the true point (SO₂ itself has no hydrogen, so it is not a Brønsted-Lowry acid on its own; it is an acidic oxide) and connect it to scene 6.
- Replacement (scene 7, about 70 characters longer): "Sulfur dioxide has no hydrogen of its own, so it can't donate a proton. It's an **acidic oxide**: a non-metal oxide that makes an acid in water and reacts with bases to make a salt. In the slurry's water it first makes **sulfurous acid**, and the hydroxide neutralises that."
- Visual: replace the "no proton moves" tag, the crossed "SO₂ accepts a proton" chip and the C01 "like CaO + SO₃" callback (a dry reaction) with a droplet lens: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃, then its teal tokens hop to OH⁻ and become water.
- Carry the change to key note 4 ("SO₂ is an acidic oxide (no H of its own; in water it makes H₂SO₃)"), "Stops after", the cause chain, teaching checks, site correction 9's note and the YouTube description ("without any proton transfer").
- Understanding enabled: the student sees one consistent story from the chimney to the scrubber, and can still say why SO₂ is called an acidic oxide rather than an acid.

### S1. Should fix: "from last video" is wrong in playlist order

- Exact text: scene 3 "**Magnesium hydroxide**, from last video, gives a salt and water."; scene 4 "like the ammonium sulfate from last video".
- Replacement: "from the acid reactions video" in both places.

### S2. Should fix: the choice frame is not applied to mine water

- Exact text (scene 9): "Treatment plants add **hydrated lime**, calcium hydroxide, to the drainage water."
- Student question: "Scene 5 said limestone is cheap, safe and gentle. Why switch to hydrated lime here?"
- Replacement (add after "removes the metals"): "Why not limestone, like the paddock? It works slowly and stops near neutral, but some metals only drop out at a higher pH. Hydrated lime dissolves more and acts faster, so the plant **controls the dose**."
- Visual: CaCO₃ and Ca(OH)₂ tiles on the pH bar, limestone stopping near 7 to 8, hydrated lime reaching higher (positions only); the choice card lights "gently" with a dial icon.
- Understanding enabled: the student sees that the same four questions give a different answer when the job is different, which is the main question.

### S3. Should fix: crowded frames in scenes 5 and 7

- Scene 5 visual: the term card "= crushed limestone, CaCO₃", the lens, `m6-marble-chips-acid-v1.png` "with a tag "same reaction as marble in acid"", the strip, the pH marker and the tag "no acid left: limestone stops reacting". Repair: dim the term before the lens opens; keep the marble plate but drop its tag (the fizzing carries the link).
- Scene 7 visual: slurry tag, strip, "no proton moves" tag, crossed chip, the acidic-oxide meaning, the C01 callback chip, the gypsum tag and the choice card. Repair: as B1, and let each tag replace the one before (slurry tag, then the acidic-oxide meaning, then the sulfurous-acid tag, then the gypsum tag), so the strip plus one tag are bright.

### S4. Should fix: scene 10 plans seven text items

- Visual text (scene 10): the aid, four product tags, the four-step frame and "Condition tag: "base chosen to be safe, cheap and gentle"".
- Repair: each product tag dims as the next writes; the tags fade before the frame writes; drop the condition tag (the corner choice card already says it).

### M1. Minor: entry knowledge for pH

- Exact text: "Year 11: pH scale as "lower is more acidic, seven neutral" (Module 3 and Stage 5)" and "(Year 11)" in the Entry knowledge line.
- Replacement: "Stage 5 (Years 9 and 10): pH scale as ...". The Year 11 syllabus does not teach pH as content; Stage 5 does.

### M2. Minor (no change): attempt 1 is close to the liming case

- A1 moves the same CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ reaction from soil to a lake. The transfer is small, but the student must produce the net equation and the cause of the pH rise unprompted, and the crossed Ca²⁺ mistake is a real exam trap. Keep.

### M3. Minor (no change): the memory aid earns its place

- "Every fix leaves something behind" is true of every reaction, but here it points to a choice criterion the lesson uses three times (CO₂ in the stomach, a collectable solid in the scrubber, sludge from mine water). Keep; record the review decision under Open decisions.
