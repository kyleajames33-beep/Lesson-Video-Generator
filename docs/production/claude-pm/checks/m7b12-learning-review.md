Main question traced: *In the 1950s, spraying DDT on mosquitoes bred resistance. In 2014, Townsville released more mosquitoes, and for years no local dengue outbreak was confirmed there. How can controlling a vector reduce infection, and how do you judge which method to use?* Useful outcome: given a vector-borne disease, the student can say why transmission needs a bite, survival and a second bite; follow the *Aedes* life cycle and place each method at the stage it attacks; explain why removing water, insecticides, sterile males, self-limiting males and Wolbachia work and where each fails (missed containers, resistance and harm, releases that must continue, spread by itself); and judge methods by transmission cut, duration, cost and harm, justifying a combination.

## Scope and focus

One question: every method is placed on the same life cycle wheel and answers "which link does it attack, and how long does it last?" The split decision is sound, and the exclusions (screwworm, Bt crops, GM livestock, gene drive detail, incidence data for b13) are right. The video is at the 570 s cap, and scenes 5 and 9 are the densest. The b13 overlap note and the prerequisite flag (add bio-m6-b11) are both correct; I agree with adding bio-m6-b11 as a prerequisite, and the two-sentence gene drive recall is enough for a student who has watched it (Doubt c: no extra sentence needed).

## Strengths to keep

- **The hook is a real contrast.** "Spray them" against "add more of them" is surprising, and "why did adding mosquitoes help?" is answered in scene 9.
- **Scene 2 makes the causal chain explicit** before any method: bite an infected person, survive while the virus multiplies, bite again. The three ways to cut infection then follow from it rather than being listed.
- **The bucket pair is the right painted case.** Eggs above the waterline, flooding, larvae and pupae in the same container make the life cycle and environmental management visible on one object, and "one missed container keeps breeding" shows the method's limit.
- **Biting time decides the method** (scene 5). The night and day split explains why nets suit *Anopheles*, which is a reason, not a fact to memorise.
- **The wheel gains one break mark per method**, so each method's stage can be pointed at and compared.
- **Sterile males and Wolbachia are contrasted on persistence**, which is exactly the "trade-off" the route stop asks for, and attempt 2 tests it.
- **Both attempts are genuine transfer** with brief feedback (about 28 s each) and one crossed mistake each.
