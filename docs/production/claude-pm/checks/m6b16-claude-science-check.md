## Facts confirmed (no change)

- **Dolly's birth and breeds.** Dolly was born on 5 July 1996 at the Roslin Institute, Scotland. The nucleus donor was a Finn Dorset (white-faced). The egg donor and the surrogate were Scottish Blackface ewes. Dolly was a Finn Dorset-type lamb carried by a Blackface ewe. "A white-faced lamb was born in Scotland to a black-faced ewe" is correct. (Searched: https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/dolly-at-20-the-inside-story-on-the-world-s-most-famous-sheep ; https://digit.fyi/a-clone-is-born-remembering-dolly-the-sheep/)
- **Whole-cell fusion.** The whole mammary cell was placed against the enucleated egg (metaphase II oocyte with its chromosomes removed), and electric pulses fused the two and activated the egg. "An electric pulse fused the udder cell with the empty egg and started it dividing" is correct (site correction 6 confirmed). "Sucked out its nucleus" is the standard simplification: the oocyte's metaphase chromosomes are aspirated. "So the egg had no chromosomes of its own" is exact.
- **277 / 29 / 1.** There were 277 fused couplets from the mammary cell line. Of these, 29 reached the morula or blastocyst stage after about six days and were transferred to 13 recipient ewes. There was one pregnancy and one live lamb. "Fused 277 udder cells with eggs. Only 29 grew into embryos healthy enough to transfer, and just one became a live lamb" is correct. The 277 include the success (site correction 1 confirmed). "After about a week" is correct. (Searched: Scientific American and Petrosains summaries above.)
- **Whom the clone matches.** Nuclear DNA comes from the donor cell. Face colour follows the nucleus donor. "Three mothers" is the standard framing. This is correct.
- **Phenotype differences.** Size, markings and health can differ because of the uterine environment, development and later environment. This is correct (finding 9).
- **Why cloning fails.** Incomplete epigenetic reprogramming is the main explanation. High pregnancy loss, abnormal placentas, large offspring syndrome and deaths around birth are documented in cattle and sheep. Live-birth efficiency stays low (single-digit to low double-digit per cent of transferred embryos). "So many clones die before or soon after birth, and some are born unusually large. Rates have improved, but most attempts still fail" is correct. (Keefer 2015 PNAS review; Tian et al. 2003 Reprod Biol Endocrinol, for Codex to open.)
- **Dolly's health and death.** Arthritis was reported in 2002. She was euthanised on 14 February 2003 (aged 6 years 7 months) with ovine pulmonary adenocarcinoma, caused by the jaagsiekte sheep retrovirus. Other sheep housed with her had the same disease, and Roslin did not link it to cloning. "At six she was put down because of a lung disease caused by a virus that also infected other sheep in her flock. That alone can't show cloning aged her early" is correct. "Put down" is a plain Australian term and fine. (Searched: https://www.avma.org/javma-news/2003-04-15/goodbye-dolly-first-cloned-sheep-dies-six-years-old ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolly_(sheep))
- **Telomeres.** Shiels et al. 1999 (Nature 399:316) reported shorter telomeres in Dolly. Later cattle clones had normal or longer telomeres (Lanza et al. 2000, Science 288:665). Sinclair 2016 did not measure telomeres. The narration rightly leaves telomeres out, and site correction 3 is supported.
- **Site correction 4.** "Premature disease" in surviving clones is not established, and Sinclair 2016 contradicts it. Confirmed.
- **Gene cloning outline.** A gene in a plasmid vector is taken up by a bacterial host and replicated at each division. Only a small fraction of cells take up a plasmid, and one transformed cell is enough. Some plasmids lack the insert, so colonies are screened. Sequencing checks the copies. "The copies let scientists study a gene, make its protein, or put it into another organism" is correct.
- **Attempt 1 data.** The data are hypothetical and labelled so. Four live calves at one year from 100 transferred embryos is within reported cattle SCNT efficiencies. Mitochondria from the egg donor appear in the model answer. Correct.
- **Attempt 2.** The scenario is hypothetical and labelled so. The northern white rhino is functionally extinct (two females). Cells from dead males are stored, for example as fibroblasts in the San Diego Frozen Zoo. A related rhinoceros would probably be the surrogate. Correct, and consistent with b12, where the last male died in 2018.
- **Absolute words.** "Only 29" and "just one" are true by count. "You need just one" is true because it multiplies. "Most attempts still fail" is true. "Can't show cloning aged her early" is correct as an inference. "So both new cells get the gene" is covered by finding 8. "Very, for its purpose" is a qualified judgement. None are problems.
- **Memory aids.** "The clone matches the nucleus donor" is correct for nuclear DNA, with the mitochondrial and phenotype limits stated in scene 4. "Gene cloning copies a gene; organism cloning copies an individual" is correct, with the nuclear-DNA limit in the design's notes.
- **Exam style.** Both items are HSC Section II style, use NESA verbs (assess, distinguish) with mark values and are tagged "HSC-style question, written for HSCScience". A web search found no NESA or other past-paper question with this wording or these scenarios (searched). They are original.
- **YouTube metadata.** The dotpoint is quoted verbatim. "See why 277 attempts gave one lamb" is correct (277 fused cells). The prerequisites and next link match the design.
