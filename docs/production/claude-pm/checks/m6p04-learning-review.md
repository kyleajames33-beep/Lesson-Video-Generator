## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "Two bottles, both labelled 0.100 mol/L: one hydrochloric acid, one ethanoic acid. How can a pH meter show which acid is strong, what makes the comparison fair, and how could you still tell them apart if you couldn't trust the label?"

One main question: yes. Both tests answer "what pH evidence separates strong from weak?", and the variables, VRA and risk scenes serve the same investigation. Useful outcome: a student can design a matched-concentration comparison, explain the pH gap by extent of ionisation, design an exact tenfold dilution, use the size of the pH change to classify an acid or base, and evaluate a conclusion drawn from unmatched solutions.

## Findings

No blockers.

### S1. Should fix: the video ignores that P03 already ran the matched comparison, and gives a different factor (70 against P03's 23) without saying why

- Exact text, scene 2: "The first test is the one from the strong and weak video. Compare the acids at the **same concentration**." Scene 5: "from these readings it has about **seventy times** as much hydronium."
- Student questions: "We matched them last practical too. What's new?" and "P03 said twenty three times for the same two acids. Is it 23 or 70?"
- Problem: P03 scene 7 compared HCl and acetic acid at the same 0.010 mol/L and said "about twenty three times ... the next practical tests it properly". Without a link, P04 test 1 looks like a repeat, and the two factors look contradictory. They are both right: the gap shrinks as both acids are diluted, which is exactly what test 2 shows (1.86 units at 0.100 mol/L, 1.35 at 0.0100 mol/L). This is a payoff the design already has the data for.
- Replacement, scene 2 (replaces the first sentence and the last sentence): "You met the first test in the strong and weak video, and the last practical used it at zero point zero one zero: compare at the **same concentration**. Why does that matter? Because a concentrated weak acid can read a lower pH than a dilute strong one. So if the concentrations differ, the pH mixes up two things. Match them, and any pH difference has to come from **strength**. This time, all four are at zero point one zero zero."
- Replacement, scene 10 (replaces "Two sessions, same results."): "And look at the gap between the two acids: almost two units before dilution, about one point four after. That's why the last practical found about twenty three times, not seventy. The more dilute the acids, the smaller the gap." Visual: on "about one point four" a bracket from 2.04 to 3.39 reads "1.35 units", beside the scene 5 bracket "1.86 units" (grey); tag "P03: about 23 ×".
- Understanding enabled: the student sees one consistent picture: the strong-weak gap depends on concentration, which is why strength must be compared at matched concentration and why dilution reveals strength.

### S2. Should fix: scene 9 relies on "a bigger fraction ionises on dilution" without a cause

- Exact text: "Remember from the strong and weak video: when you dilute a weak acid, a **bigger fraction** of its molecules ionise. So as the solution spreads out, extra molecules give up their protons and partly **make up** for the dilution."
- Student question: "Why would more molecules ionise just because I added water?"
- Problem: C05 scene 7 stated this without a reason, so "remember" points to a fact, not an explanation. It is the one step the dilution test rests on. A Module 5 cause is available without Ka: the reverse reaction needs a hydronium ion and an ethanoate ion to meet, and dilution makes those meetings rarer, so the balance settles further forward.
- Replacement: "Ethanoic acid is different. When you dilute it, the hydronium and ethanoate ions are spread out, so they meet less often, and fewer join back into whole molecules. The balance settles with a **bigger fraction** ionised. Those extra ions partly **make up** for the dilution. So the hydronium per litre falls by much **less than ten times**, and for ethanoic acid the pH rises only about half a unit." Visual: on "meet less often" two highlighted ion pairs in the widening lens drift apart instead of rejoining (one amber molecule re-forming in the narrow lens, none in the wide one); then as designed.
- Understanding enabled: the student can explain the half-unit result from equilibrium reasoning they already have, and can answer A2's "justify" with a cause, not a remembered rule.

### S3. Should fix: "up a whole one unit" and "strong moves one" overstate what a class will measure

- Exact text, scene 7: "Hydrochloric acid went from one point zero four to two point zero four, up a whole **one** unit." Scene 8: "Why a whole unit?" Memory aid: "strong moves one".
- Student question (in the lab): "We got 0.95. Did we do it wrong?"
- Problem: real meters respond to ion activity, so a tenfold dilution of 0.100 mol/L HCl reads about 0.94 to 0.97 units, not 1.00 (see the science check, S1). Teaching "a whole unit" from idealised data sets students up to doubt a correct result.
- Replacement: use the science check's adjusted data (HCl mean 1.08, NaOH mean 12.91), or at minimum change the wording. Scene 7: "Hydrochloric acid went from one point zero eight to two point zero four, up almost exactly **one** unit." Scene 8: "Why about a whole unit?" Memory aid: "Dilute ten times: strong moves about one, weak moves about half." Decision rule line 2 and key note 3 to match.
- Understanding enabled: the student expects "about one against about half", which survives real data and still separates strong from weak clearly.

### M1. Minor: bright-item count in scene 5

Dim the bracket and its chips to grey as soon as the lenses open; keep the lens counter grey once the equations write. Bright at the end, as designed: the two equations and the violet bracket.

### M2. Minor: scene 5 asks for 10^1.86 without a route

### M3. Minor: key notes 1 and 3 are long for a copy hold

- Note 1: drop the four means (they are this class's data, not a fact to learn): "1. Test 1, matched concentration: 0.100 mol/L of each, same calibrated meter, routine and temperature. Same concentration, so the gap is strength: HCl ionises completely, ethanoic acid only partly."
- Note 3: "3. Test 2, exact tenfold dilution (10.00 mL pipette to a 100.0 mL flask). Strong: pH moves about 1 (a tenth of the H₃O⁺). Weak: about 0.5 for ethanoic acid (a bigger fraction ionises on dilution)."

But the conclusions rest on **big gaps**, so they still hold." (about 70 characters shorter). Recount by script before recording; aim for 6,350 or fewer.
