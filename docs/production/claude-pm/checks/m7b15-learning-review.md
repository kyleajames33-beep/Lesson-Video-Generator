Main question traced: *In 1993 the US government applied to patent a compound from smoke bush, and Aboriginal people of the region received no payment or credit. Ten years later, a Kimberley Aboriginal corporation was named as an owner on a patent. What changed, and why do Aboriginal protocols matter?* Useful outcome: the student can name a knowledge-holding community and a medicine example, explain why a lab result is not a medicine, describe the smoke bush case and why the law allowed it, state and explain the five protocols, apply them to a new project, and give reasons why protecting Indigenous cultural and intellectual property matters.

## Scope and focus

One question, one comparison (taken against agreed) and one test (the five protocols) applied to every case. Both syllabus examples are used. The exclusions are sound: Tu Youyou, the unsourced site plant list, Nagoya's articles and the Kakadu plum dispute are kept out, and the reasons are recorded. Double-teaching check: b11's drug action is one grey tag (but see finding 3, where the narration contradicts b11); b14's APY Lands is one grey tag; bio-m6-b19's "Who gets it?" is one grey tag; b03, b10, b12 and b13 do not overlap. No re-teaching found. The video sits at the 570 s cap, so every addition below is paid for by a cut (finding 12).

## Strengths to keep

- **The hook is a real contrast**: two patents on Australian plants with opposite processes. The student wants to know what made the difference.
- **One test for every case.** The protocol card ticks, crosses or says "not recorded", so smoke bush, mudjala and spinifex are judged in the same way. Leaving mudjala's consent rows "not recorded" instead of ticking them is honest.
- **The development lane** separates "lab test" from "approved medicine" and keeps both markers stopped at "lab test". This blocks the commonest wrong idea in this topic.
- **"Consent to one use isn't consent to another"** and **"A payment offered afterwards isn't an agreement"** are reconstructable causal rules, and attempt 1 tests both.
- **The communities act.** They hold, share, agree, own and veto. No preparation, ceremony, story or restricted content appears, and no people are drawn.
- **The sourcing is specific.** Communities, corporations, a patent number and an Indigenous-authored main source are named. The design also states plainly where the record is thin (smoke bush's traditional use) instead of repeating the site's unsourced nation and uses.
- **Both attempts are genuine HSC Section II items** (assess, 4 marks; explain, 3 marks), with invented, unnamed communities, so no real nation is attached to an invented wrong. Each card stays up through the 10 s countdown. Feedback takes about 27 s and 28 s, with one crossed mistake each.
