## Facts confirmed (no change)

- **The 1977 drought.** Boag and Grant (Science 1981;214:82, "Intense natural selection in a population of Darwin's finches (Geospizinae) in the Galápagos") report non-random survival through the 1977 drought on Daphne Major, with selection favouring large body and beak size, among the strongest selection intensities then recorded for a vertebrate. The *G. fortis* population fell by about 85 per cent (commonly given as about 1,200 to about 180 birds; some accounts give about 1,400 to a few hundred). Small, soft seeds were depleted first, leaving large, hard seeds such as *Tribulus cistoides* that deep-beaked birds crack more easily. "A drought hit Daphne Major", "The small, soft seeds ran out", "most of the medium ground finches died", "The survivors tended to have deeper beaks, strong enough to crack the big, hard seeds": correct. Secondary: https://www.doi.org/10.1038/309787a0 (Price et al., Nature 1984, on the same Boag and Grant selection), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beak_of_the_Finch.
- **Heritability and the chicks.** Boag (Evolution 1983;37:877, "The heritability of external morphology in Darwin's ground finches (Geospiza) on Isla Daphne Major, Galápagos", doi 10.1111/j.1558-5646.1983.tb05618.x) estimated beak-depth heritability from offspring on mid-parent regression at about 0.7 to 0.8 (a teaching handout citing the paper gives a slope of 0.78 for 1976; the site's 0.79 is consistent). The 1978 offspring had deeper beaks on average than the pre-drought population (about 4 to 5 per cent, secondary sources). Revision 2's "Beak depth is largely inherited, so their chicks hatched with deeper beaks on average, too" is correct ("largely" fits a heritability near 0.8). The study began in 1973 (not said, correctly).
- **"No new mutation was needed."** The response used existing heritable variation in one generation. Beak-size variation in Darwin's finches includes an *HMGA2* haplotype that predates the drought (Lamichhaney et al., Science 2016;352:470), and hybridisation with *G. scandens* adds variation (Grant and Grant, Science 2002;296:707). Correct; scene 7's "made by mutations long before the drought" is correct.
- **Segregation of a new allele.** A heterozygote passes each allele to half its gametes; Nn × NN gives 1/2 Nn and 1/2 NN (no nn); Nn × Nn gives 1/4 nn (recomputed). Scene 3's "about half", scene 5's "one in four", 10b's "half the offspring carry one copy, and none has two" and "about one offspring in four": correct. A zygote's mutation is in every cell because every cell descends from it by mitosis (Revision 2 wording).
- **Crossing over and independent assortment.** Crossing over between non-sister chromatids recombines alleles of linked genes; independent assortment recombines whole chromosomes; neither makes a new allele. Correct (B5b).
- **New mutations per child.** Kong et al. (Nature 2012;488:471; 78 Icelandic trios) found a de novo point-mutation rate of about 1.20 × 10⁻⁸ per base per generation at an average paternal age of 29.7, about 60 per child (Kondrashov's commentary) or about 63 per trio, rising by about 2 per year of paternal age. Jónsson et al. (Nature 2017;549:519) found 108,778 de novo mutations in 1,548 probands (about 70 each), with 1.51 more per year of paternal age and 0.37 per year of maternal age. **"About sixty to seventy" is correct**; the project manager's fallback ("a few dozen") is not needed. "Roughly six billion bases" for the two genome copies a child carries (about 6.2 billion base pairs, diploid): correct. Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28959963/, https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=110295, https://thetransmitter.org/spectrum/fathers-age-dictates-rate-of-new-mutations.
- **"Millions of places" (Revision 2).** Two copies of a human genome differ at about one base in a thousand, so a person is heterozygous at about 3 to 4 million sites (1000 Genomes Project Consortium, Nature 2015;526:68). Correct.
- **"More than 8 million combinations".** 2^23 = 8,388,608 combinations of whole chromosomes among one person's gametes, from independent assortment alone; crossing over adds vastly more. The design rightly corrects the site's "per human gamete" (site correction 6). "Fertilisation multiplies the two parents' choices together": correct (2^46, about 70 trillion, B8; Revision 2 drops the number from the screen).
- **Siblings.** Full siblings share about half their DNA identical by descent; their differences come mainly from parental heterozygous sites recombined and assorted differently, plus tens of new mutations each. "Almost all of those differences were already in their parents, reshuffled": correct.
- **Ultimate and immediate source.** Every allele originated as a mutation; recombination and fertilisation generate each generation's combinations. Standard framing (site Analyse model; Campbell ch. 23). "If mutation stopped ... Any allele the population lost, by chance or selection, would never be made again": correct (gene flow could only bring back an allele that still exists elsewhere; author note in the design).
- **Bacteria.** Binary fission gives genetically identical daughters apart from new mutations; large populations and short generation times mean many new mutations arise each day; transformation, conjugation and transduction move genes, including on plasmids. Correct (OpenStax Microbiology 11.6). With finding 1 the learner claim is limited to bacteria.
- **Recessive hidden in carriers.** A fully recessive allele's phenotype shows only in homozygotes. Correct; the narration says "if the new allele is recessive".
- **Attempt 1 (10a, 10b).** Stem: a new recessive autosomal allele in one sperm; the animal has one copy in every cell (so no germ-line mosaicism arises); it mates with a non-carrier. (a) 1/2 carry *n*; (b) two carriers mate, each passes *n* in half its gametes, about 1 in 4 offspring *nn*. Correct.
- **Attempt 2 (10c, 10d).** The claim's premise is correct per generation; its conclusion fails because every allele first arose by mutation and asexual organisms depend on it. "It matters even more in asexual organisms" (10d) is correct generally (no meiosis or fertilisation to reshuffle).
- **Memory aid (B8's deck, extended).** "In one generation, almost all the variety comes from the dealing. But over a long time, every card was once printed new." Correct as the per-generation against long-term distinction. Its limits (a hand always has one card of each kind; new cards are printed only in the germ line) were spoken in B8; the author note is right.
- **Site corrections 1 to 8** in the design are scientifically right (the study began in 1973; the 17 per cent figure is not supported and the offspring change was about 4 to 5 per cent; the finch study did not measure mutation or crossing-over rates; gene transfer also varies asexual populations; adaptation can run on standing variation; 2^23 is the number of combinations among a person's gametes, not per gamete; independent assortment is the independent orientation of each pair; the drought-era alleles came from past mutation and some hybridisation).
- **Em dashes:** none in the design.
