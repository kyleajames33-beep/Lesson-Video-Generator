## Biology B07

## Biology B08

1. **Scene 6 screen tag and author note, wrong population-wide doubling.** The tag says "variety restored: about double" and the author note says genetic heterozygosity "roughly doubled". Johnson et al. report mean yearly adult multilocus heterozygosity rising from **18.4% in 1993 to 25%**, about a 36% relative increase. Their table shows a different comparison that approximately doubled: first-generation Texas cross offspring **0.336** against purebred Florida panthers **0.167**. The abstract's unqualified "doubled" must not be presented as a doubling of the entire Florida population's measured variation. Correct tag: "genetic variation increased". Correct author note: "Mean adult heterozygosity rose from 18.4% to 25%; first-generation Texas cross offspring had about twice the heterozygosity of purebred Florida panthers." Source: [Johnson et al. 2010, Fig. 3B and Table 1](https://faculty.lsu.edu/kharms/files/johnson_etal_2010.pdf).

2. **Scenes 4 and 6, specific defect mechanism treated as established.** Scene 4 says harmful alleles became common by drift and shows a kitten's tail kinking when it receives two copies of the drawn allele. Scene 6 says the Texas kittens had "fewer copies of the harmful alleles" and shows that allele's frequency falling. The study documents inbreeding signs and their decline with admixture, but does not identify a recessive allele for the kinked tail or measure its frequency before and after rescue. Correct narration: "Inbreeding made harmful inherited traits more likely to show. After Texas pumas bred with Florida panthers, their kittens had more genetic variation, and kinked tails became less common." Remove the specific two-copy tail animation and the measured-looking harmful-allele frequency change; show the broader reduction in inbreeding traits. Source: [Johnson et al. 2010, Table 1 and discussion](https://faculty.lsu.edu/kharms/files/johnson_etal_2010.pdf).

3. **Scene 8 narration, screen aid and key-note memory line, selection is overgeneralised.** "Gene flow makes populations more alike, while drift and selection pull them apart" implies selection always drives populations apart. Selection can instead make two populations more alike when the same allele is favoured in both. Correct text: "Gene flow tends to make populations more alike. Drift can pull them apart; selection can pull them apart when their environments favour different alleles." Source: [OpenStax Biology 2e, population genetics](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/19-2-population-genetics).
