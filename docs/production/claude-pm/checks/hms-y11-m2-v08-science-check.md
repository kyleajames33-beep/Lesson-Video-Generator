## Findings
1. **Should fix (scene 9 narration and on-screen "never just tiredness"; flagged).** "A collapse at the end of hard effort is never just tiredness: follow DRSABCD and get help." As a factual claim this is wrong: most collapses just after finishing an endurance event are exercise-associated collapse, usually benign postural low blood pressure once the athlete stops (Asplund, O'Connor and Noakes, *Br J Sports Med* 2011, 45:1157 to 1162). Collapse before the finish or during exercise is the more sinister sign. As a first aid rule, treating every collapse as serious is right. Corrected: "Never put a collapse after hard effort down to tiredness: follow DRSABCD and get help." On screen: "collapse: never assume it's just tiredness". The next sentence ("Chest pain or collapse during exercise means an ambulance") stays.
2. **Should fix (scene 8 narration).** "But confusion in a hot athlete means heat stroke." Confusion can also come from low blood sodium or low blood glucose, but field guidance is to treat it as heat stroke until proven otherwise. Corrected: "But treat confusion in a hot athlete as heat stroke. That's an emergency." The crossed chip "confusion = discipline problem" stays.
3. **Note (scene 8).** Muscle cramps are a contested sign of dehydration (exercise-associated cramps are now thought mainly neuromuscular). Acceptable in a list of warning signs; "cramps" could be dropped if a line needs cutting.
4. **Note (11b).** Low blood glucose and dehydration also impair passing accuracy late in a match. The model's "nervous system, from central fatigue" is the best fit to "still running well", but an answer naming fuel for the brain with that reasoning should not be marked wrong; only "heart and lungs" is crossed, which is fine.
5. **Note (scene 7, author).** Current guidance (PEACE and LOVE, Dubois and Esculier, *Br J Sports Med* 2020) questions prolonged icing; RICER and No HARM remain Sports Medicine Australia's published first aid advice. No change.
