Main question traced: *A cotton plant that poisons caterpillars and a bull edited so his calves grow no horns: both work, but how should we judge plant and animal biotechnology, and what does an animal case add?* Useful outcome: given an unseen plant or animal biotechnology, the student can name a concrete benefit and who gets it, a concrete harm or risk and who carries it (and, for an animal, its welfare), say which kind of question each objection is, and end with a verdict backed by criteria and evidence (the route stop: "judge a concrete benefit, harm and affected group").

## Strengths to keep

- **"It works" is separated from "it is justified" in the first 20 seconds**, and the board's lines make distribution visible: one benefit chip with four lines to four groups is a picture of "who gets which".
- **Bt cotton is a real, measured, Australian case** with a real control (refuges) that turns a "risk" into a managed condition, so the verdict can carry a condition naturally.
- **The plasmid twist is the strongest teaching moment.** An intended, well-motivated edit had an unintended outcome found only by checking, which teaches "judge the outcome, not the intention" better than any rule. The project manager's decision to keep it is sound.
- **The three bins (consequences, distribution, principle)** sort earlier chips the student has already seen, so the classification is earned, and 11a(b) tests it directly.
- **The evaluate item fits its marks.** 11c (5 marks) supplies the benefit, the containment conditions and the groups, and 11d models judgement, criteria and evidence with a conditional verdict.
