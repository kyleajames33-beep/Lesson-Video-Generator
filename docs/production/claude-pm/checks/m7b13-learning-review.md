Main question traced: *A randomised trial in Yogyakarta found about 77 per cent fewer dengue cases where Wolbachia mosquitoes were released, yet 2024 was the worst dengue year ever recorded. What limits dengue control, and how do incidence and prevalence data show it?* Useful outcome: given data on a vector-borne disease, the student can say whether a figure is incidence or prevalence, compare places by rate per 100,000, read a seasonal curve with its lag, explain why a mostly immune city can still have an outbreak, judge a randomised trial against a before-and-after comparison, and combine biological and social causes to explain why control is limited.

## Strengths to keep

- **The hook is a real puzzle** with a dated, sourced answer later. "If one method works that well, what limits dengue control?" is the question every later scene answers.
- **The tank model** makes incidence (inflow) and prevalence (level) visibly different, and scene 4's fast inflow with a wide drain shows why dengue's current prevalence stays low in an outbreak. This is something video does better than text.
- **Rates against counts** is shown by bars that swap from count to rate and change order. A student sees the town overtake the city.
- **"Why the delay?"** in scene 5 asks a real question and answers it with causes (breeding, then the virus's time in the mosquito), with b12's wheel as a recall, not a re-teach.
- **The serotype dot grid** (scene 6) shows DENV-3 moving through amber-ringed dots "as if they were empty", which is the exact misconception attempt 2 tests.
- **Scene 9 explains why randomisation matters** in a plain causal sentence ("weather and serotypes affected both groups alike"). This is the evaluative skill the route needs.
- **Both attempts are genuine HSC Section II items** with tables, marks and a demand verb, kept on screen through the countdown, with brief feedback (12b about 26 s, 12d about 28 s) and one crossed mistake each.
- **No people anywhere**; travel is an arc, the clinic an outline.
