## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "A freshly calibrated pH meter reads distilled water as 5.8. Is the meter wrong, the method wrong, or the water? How do you measure the pH of a range of acids and bases so the readings can be trusted, and what does each reading tell you about the ions in the sample?"

One main question: yes ("how do you get a reading you can trust?"), with reading the results as ions as its natural end. Useful outcome: a student can explain why a meter is calibrated and with which buffers, justify each step of the routine, read a result as more hydronium or more hydroxide and compare two readings by factors of ten, explain why open distilled water reads below 7, and separate reliability from accuracy.

## Findings

### B1. Blocker: the calibration rule "readings fall within the range you calibrated" is broken by four of the five results

- Exact text, scene 4: "Start with the pH seven buffer, then pH four, and add pH ten because you'll measure bases too. That way your readings fall **within** the range you calibrated." Repeated in scene 11 ("calibrating with buffers that cover your readings"), scene 18 ("calibrate with buffers that cover your readings"), key note 2 ("covering the readings") and key note 5 ("calibration covering the readings").
- Student question: "The band is 4 to 10, but the acids are at 2 and 3.4 and the bases at 10.6 and 12. So are these readings not accurate? Why did we use these buffers?"
- Problem: the lesson's main claim (this is how you get a reading you can trust) is visibly contradicted on screen by its own data, and the decision rule tells the student to do something the method does not do. Standard school practice (pH 4, 7, 10) is sound for readings from about 2 to 12 because the meter fits a straight line through the buffer points; that is the idea to teach, and it follows directly from scene 3's "voltage → pH".
- Replacement, scene 4: "That's **calibration**. You dip the probe into **buffer solutions**, which hold a known, steady pH. How they do that is a later video. Start with the pH seven buffer, then pH four, and add pH ten because you'll measure bases too. The meter fits a **straight line** through those known points and uses it for every later reading. Buffers on **both sides** of seven check that line for acids and for bases. Calibrate at the same temperature as your samples, on the day you measure."
- Visual, scene 4: replace the green 4 to 10 band and the outside-the-band HCl tag with a small coded graph beside the bottles: x axis "pH", y axis "meter signal". On each buffer's tick a point lights (7, then 4, then 10); on "straight line" a teal line draws through the three points, with short dashed ends running to 2 and 12; the five sample positions appear as empty ticks on the line, grey tag "readings near 2 and 12 lie on the same line". On "both sides" the acid and base halves of the line tint teal and violet.
- Scene 11: "That depends on calibrating with buffers on both sides of seven, at the same temperature as your samples." Accuracy card: "Calibrate with buffers either side of 7; same temperature."
- Scene 18: "calibrate with buffers on both sides of seven". Key note 2: "Calibrate the meter with buffers on both sides of 7 (pH 7.00, then 4.00 and 10.00), at the samples' temperature." Key note 5: "Accuracy: calibration with buffers either side of 7, same temperature." Update the teaching checks ("Why the buffers must cover the readings") and close open decision 2.
- Understanding enabled: the student can explain what calibration does (sets the line from voltage to pH), why one buffer is not enough, and why acid and base readings both need checking. The science check should confirm the wording against meter practice.

### S1. Should fix: scene 5 gives no reason for waiting or for fresh portions

- Exact text: "Lower the bulb fully into the sample, swirl gently, and **wait** until the reading stops changing before you record it. Take **three readings**, each in a fresh portion."
- Student question: "Why does the number keep changing? And why can't I just dip three times into the same beaker?"
- Replacement: "Lower the bulb fully into the sample, swirl gently, and **wait** until the reading stops changing, because the glass needs time to respond. Take **three readings**, each in a fresh portion, so one spoiled dip can't spoil all three."
- Understanding enabled: each routine step has a reason the student can write in a "justify" answer; "fresh portions" also prepares A1's crossed mistake (repeats that copy the contamination).

### S2. Should fix: scene 7 jumps from 1.4 units to 23 times with no route the student can follow

- Exact text: "Hydrochloric acid reads about one point four lower than acetic acid, so it has about **twenty three times** as many hydronium ions, at the same concentration."
- Student question: "One unit is ten times. How is 1.4 units twenty three?"
- Replacement: "Hydrochloric acid reads about one point four lower than acetic acid. One step would be ten times, two steps a hundred, so this is in between: about **twenty three times** as many hydronium ions, at the same concentration." Visual: the bracket from 2.03 to 3.40 shows faint grey markers "1 unit: × 10" and "2 units: × 100" before "1.37 units: about 23 ×" writes.
- Understanding enabled: the student can estimate any reading gap without logarithms (C07), and the number is bounded by facts they already have.

### S3. Should fix: scene 8's boiled-water sentence is an unexplained claim

- Exact text: "Freshly boiled and cooled water reads closer to seven."
- Student question: "Why would boiling change it?"
- Recommended repair (also saves time): cut the sentence, and change the grey tag to "boiled to drive out CO₂, then cooled: closer to 7". If the author keeps it spoken, use: "Boiling drives the carbon dioxide out, so freshly boiled and cooled water reads closer to seven."
- Understanding enabled: the boiled-water case becomes evidence for the carbon dioxide cause rather than a separate fact to memorise.

### S4. Should fix: attempt 2 retests a C05 fact and leaves the lesson's core idea untested

- Exact text: A2, "Solution X has a pH of 3.4 and solution Y has a pH of 5.4 ... about twice the concentration of hydronium ions". C05 scene 3 already says "Two steps would be ten times ten, a hundred times".
- Problem: the main question is about trusting a reading, but no attempt asks about calibration or separates reliability from accuracy. The factor-of-ten reading stays taught in scene 7, so the route stop ("relate readings to ions") is still met.
- Replacement A2 (narration): "One more. A student calibrates a pH meter with a pH seven buffer only, then measures zero point zero one zero mole per litre hydrochloric acid three times: two point three one, two point three zero and two point three two. Another group, which also used a pH four buffer, gets two point zero three. Evaluate the reliability and accuracy of the first student's results. Pause here to answer." Card: "A student calibrates a pH meter with a pH 7.00 buffer only. She then measures the pH of 0.010 mol/L hydrochloric acid three times, using a fresh portion each time, and obtains 2.31, 2.30 and 2.32. Another group calibrated with pH 7.00 and pH 4.00 buffers and obtained 2.03 for the same solution. Evaluate the reliability and accuracy of the first student's results."
- Replacement feedback 2: "Her results are **reliable**: the three readings agree within zero point zero two. But they aren't **accurate**. One buffer gives only one point, and the meter needs at least two to set its straight line, so every reading shares the same error. Calibrate with pH four as well, then repeat. Saying 'they agree, so they're accurate' is the **classic slip**." (about 330 characters, about 25 s). Underline "pH 7.00 buffer only", "2.31, 2.30 and 2.32" and "2.03" as used; crossed chip "they agree, so they're accurate".
- Understanding enabled: the student applies scene 4 (after B1) and scene 11 to a new case. Science check to confirm that 2.31 is plausible for a single-point calibration with an aged probe (about 94 percent slope). If the author keeps the current A2, record why in Resolution.

## Retained strengths

The "broken meter" hook held open until scene 8; the voltage-to-pH reason for calibration; the clinging drop for carry-over; measured-style readings rather than forced ideal values; the water's spread used as the unreliable case; carbon dioxide and carbonic acid as the cause; "decimals only mean something if the meter is calibrated"; A1's carry-over into water, which transfers the routine to a new case with a strong crossed mistake; strength named once and handed to P04 without a pre-emptive conclusion; honest hazard classifications for dilute solutions.
