## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "Purple juice from a red cabbage turns red in acid, green in base, and red again. How do you prepare it as an indicator, give each colour a meaning, and show that its colour change is a reversible reaction?"

One main question: yes. Preparation, calibration, the control, the cycle and the evaluation all serve "how do you make and test a natural indicator?". Useful outcome: a student can describe and justify the preparation, calibrate the extract with equal drops against known pH, explain the water control, design the one-sample reversibility test and explain it with the indicator equilibrium, estimate a pH as a range, and say why the extract is a poor titration indicator.

## Findings

No blockers.

### S1. Should fix: scene 9's "strong base turns it yellow" meets scene 6's well of 0.10 mol/L sodium hydroxide without a link

- Exact text, scene 6: "Now test the extract in three wells: dilute **hydrochloric acid**, distilled water, and dilute **sodium hydroxide**." Scene 9: "If a green sample sits in extra strong base for a long time, it can turn **yellow** ..."
- Student question: "Well C was full of sodium hydroxide. Why was it green, and will mine go yellow?"
- Problem: well C (about pH 13) is a stronger base than the cycle's "just green" sample (about pH 11.5, author note d). Published charts put pH 13 to 14 at greenish-yellow to yellow (science check M1), so a class may see well C drift. Without a link, scene 9 seems to contradict scene 6, and a student who sees yellow in well C may think the practical failed.
- Repair, scene 6 visual: add a grey tag on well C as it fills, "read it straight away". Scene 9 narration, replace the second sentence: "If a green sample sits in strong base, like well C, for a long time, it can turn **yellow**, and acid may not bring the red back." (adds about 12 characters).
- Understanding enabled: the student connects the warning to their own wells, reads colours promptly, and treats a drifting well as evidence of a second reaction, not a mistake.

### S2. Should fix: scene 2 gives "more than one proton" as the only reason for many colours

- Exact text: "But they can lose **more than one proton**, so the extract passes through several colours, not just two."
- Student question: "Two proton losses make three forms. Why does the chart have seven colours that change gradually?"
- Problem: the gradual, many-step colour series comes from several proton-transfer steps and from the extract being a mixture of related anthocyanins whose forms blend (author note b). The teaching check names the mixture, but the learner copy doesn't. Scene 5 ("several gradual changes") and scene 10 ("changes gradually, in steps") then rest on an unstated cause.
- Replacement, scene 2: "The purple comes from pigments called **anthocyanins**, stored inside the cabbage's cells, and they dissolve in water. Each one behaves like H In from the indicator video: a weak acid whose forms have different colours. But each can lose **more than one proton**, and the extract is a mix of several of them, so it passes through several colours, blending gradually, not just two." (372 characters, +56). Visual: on "a mix of several of them" two more faint token rows slide in under the first, slightly offset in hue; tag "a mixture: colours blend".
- Understanding enabled: the student can explain why the chart has many gradual steps and why that makes the extract a poor endpoint indicator (scene 10), from one cause.

### M1. Minor: bright-item load in scenes 4 and 10

- Scene 4 at "three drops": seven well labels, the reference tag, the meter and its readout, the drop counter, then the side pair and its tag. Dim the reference tag and the meter as soon as the extract dropper starts; the drop counter is the only bright chip during the drops.
- Scene 10 at "pH meter": the chart strip, two sample chips, two meter readouts and a grey tag. Show each readout beside its chip and dim the vinegar pair when the cleaner pair lands.

### M2. Minor: scene 11 and A2 treat the meter's display as its accuracy

- Exact text: card "Accuracy: close to the true pH? Chart: about ±1 unit. Calibrated meter: 0.01."; A2 answer box "meter reads to 0.01".
- Student question: "Is a meter accurate to 0.01, or does it just show two decimal places?"
- Replacement, card: "Accuracy: close to the true pH? Chart: about ±1 unit. Calibrated meter: within a few hundredths." A2 answer box (b): "less accurate: steps 2 units wide, colour judged by eye; a calibrated meter is within a few hundredths". Narration can stay.

### M3. Minor: scene 3, "the thin layer around each cell"

- A student without Year 11 Biology pictures the cell wall, which is not the barrier.
- Replacement: "The heat damages the cells' thin outer membranes, which hold the pigment in, so the pigment **leaks out** faster." (+27 characters). Lens tag: "heat damages the membranes: pigment leaks out".

### M4. Minor: key notes 4 and 5 are dense for a copy hold

- Note 4 (replace): "4. Water control: dilution alone only pales the colour. Reversibility: one sample, base until green, acid until red, three cycles; the same colours return (equilibrium shifts with H⁺). Strong base left standing can destroy the pigment."
- Note 5 (split): "5. Validity: one batch, equal drops, water control. Reliability: repeats give the same colours. Accuracy: chart about ±1 pH unit; a calibrated meter is far better. Poor for titrations: gradual changes, no single sharp one." and "6. Risk: hot water and knife (teacher); eye protection for 0.10 mol/L HCl and NaOH; extract stains."

## Retained strengths

A hook that pays off C13's handoff in the first ten seconds; every preparation step justified; the equal-drops side pair; the water control introduced as a question; the one-sample cycle with dilution shown as lighter, same-hue swatches; the colour that doesn't come back as an honest limit; two unseen attempts that each target the practical's central misconception; brief feedback with one crossed mistake each; strict avoidance of C13 re-teaching.
