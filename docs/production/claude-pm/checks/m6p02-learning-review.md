## Main question and the useful outcome

Main question (as stated): "The thermometer never catches the full temperature rise, because heat escapes from the moment it is made. So how do you set up the enthalpy of neutralisation experiment, and how do you recover the temperature you never see?"

One main question: yes. Everything serves "set it up so little heat escapes, then recover what did escape". Useful outcome: a student can justify each part of the set-up, explain why both starting temperatures are measured, read and extrapolate a temperature-time graph, calculate ΔH from the extrapolated rise, trace an error through ΔT, q and ΔH, and say why repeats improve reliability but not accuracy.

## Findings

### S1. Should fix: scene 6 says where to extend the cooling line but not why the moment of mixing

- Exact text: "So draw a straight line through the cooling points, and extend it back to the moment of **mixing**. Here it meets **twenty eight point one** degrees. That's our best estimate of the temperature with no heat lost."
- Student question: "Why time zero? Why not stop at one minute, where the peak was?"
- Problem: this is the step the main question is about. Without the reason, the student can follow the procedure but cannot explain it, and will not know what to do with a graph whose peak arrives later.
- Replacement: "So draw a straight line through the cooling points, and extend it back to the moment of **mixing**. Why there? Neutralisation is almost instant, so if no heat escaped, the temperature would jump to its full value the moment you mix. Here the line meets **twenty eight point one** degrees, our best estimate of the temperature with no heat lost." Visual: on "almost instant" a faint dashed step draws from 21.5 °C straight up at t = 0 to the extrapolated dot, tag "no heat lost: instant jump" (grey, then dims).
- Understanding enabled: the student can say what the extrapolated value represents (the instant, loss-free rise), which also explains why the line must go back to t = 0 in A1.

### S2. Should fix: scene 9's "can go either way" example has no cause the student can follow

- Exact text: "Most errors here push the same way, but **not every one**: treating the mixture as pure water is a small error that can go either way."
- Student question: "How can it go either way? Which way does it go?"
- Problem: the point (not every error lowers |ΔH|) is needed to correct the site, but the chosen example is an unexplained claim, and author note (d) says it actually has one direction. Scene 3 already gave a clear error with the opposite direction.
- Replacement: "Most errors here push the same way, but **not every one**. If the base was warmer than the acid and you used only the acid's temperature, delta T comes out too **big**, so delta H comes out too negative." Visual: the third row becomes "base warmer, only acid's temperature used" with up arrows "ΔT ↑ → q ↑ → ΔH too negative" (crossfade from scene 3's "21.6 °C" chip, small). Key note 4: "Not every error does: using only a cooler acid's starting temperature makes ΔH too negative." Keep the pure-water assumption in author notes.
- Understanding enabled: the student traces a second error through the same chain in the opposite direction, so "always less negative" visibly fails and the chain becomes a tool rather than a rule.

### S3. Should fix: scene 10 says repeats cannot fix accuracy without saying why

- Exact text: "Repeats can't fix that. Cutting **heat loss** can."
- Student question: "If I repeat it lots of times, won't the average get closer to −57?"
- Replacement: "Repeats can't fix that, because heat escapes in **every** run, so every run comes out low. Cutting **heat loss** can." Visual: on "every" the three run icons each get a small orange arrow leaving the lid, before the crossed "more repeats make it more accurate" chip.
- Understanding enabled: the student separates a systematic error (same direction every run) from random scatter, which is the exact distinction HSC "assess accuracy" questions test.

### S4. Should fix: scenes 10 and 17 say "equal" or "matching" starting temperatures, but the method measures and averages two different ones

- Exact text, scene 10: "equal starting temperatures, an insulated lidded cup, and extrapolation". Scene 17: "start both solutions at a measured, matching temperature". Scene 10 visual: "(same start temperature, lid, foam, extrapolation)".
- Student question: "Scene 3 had 21.4 and 21.6. Do they have to be equal, or do I average?"
- Replacement, scene 10: "Here, the rise must come from the reaction, with the heat kept in the solution: both starting temperatures measured and averaged, an insulated lidded cup, and extrapolation." Scene 17: "measure both starting temperatures and average them". Validity card: "(both start temperatures measured, lid, foam, extrapolation)". Also in scene 10: "so the mean, minus fifty five, is reliable" becomes "so the results are reliable" (reliability describes the agreement of the runs).
- Understanding enabled: one consistent instruction the student can write in a method.

### M1. Minor: bright-item counts in scenes 2, 7 and 8

- Scene 2, at "measuring cylinders": dim the two cylinder labels to grey once the "50.0 mL" underlines start, leaving the underlines and the two-line tag.
- Scene 7: keep the task line, equation strip, givens and the graph's two values grey while the working writes; bright = the current line, the answer chip, then the comparison chip.
- Scene 8: drop the unspoken grey "comparing acids" card, or leave it for the key notes. It is text with no narration during a new idea.

### M2. Minor: A1's point values must be readable when paused

- The 0.2 °C grid over 20 to 28 °C gives 40 lines. On a phone the student may not resolve 27.1 against 27.0. Label the y axis every 0.2 °C from 26.0 to 27.6 (the region that matters), or put a small grey readings strip under the graph ("t (min): 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0 / T (°C): 20.9, 25.6, 27.1, 27.1, 27.0, 26.8, 26.6, 26.4"). Neither is answer-bearing.

### M3. Minor: A2 feedback uses an untaught comparison

- Exact text: "Glass conducts heat well and the top is open". Scene 2 taught why foam insulates, not that glass conducts.
- Replacement: "Glass lets heat through far more easily than foam, and the top is open".

### M4. Minor: length headroom

- S1 to S3 add about 230 characters (about 17 s), taking the estimate to about 9.8 minutes. Apply both of the author's reserve cuts (scene 9 cup-and-probe sentence, about 60; scene 2 "with holes for the temperature probe and a stirrer", about 50), and trim scene 17 to "So: insulate and cover the cup, measure and average both starting temperatures, log past the peak, and extrapolate the cooling line back to the moment of mixing. Then trace each error through delta T, q and delta H." Recount by script before recording.

## Retained strengths

The hook's "temperature you never see" and the ghost readout that fills in at 28.1; the payoff of "the readings after the peak turn out to be the most useful part"; the "heat already lost" bracket; the C03 calculation run once with the −53, −55, −57 number line; "measuring one value, three times" for the variables of a repeated measurement; the four-box error chain with arrows that flip; "never write human error" reinforced in A2; real hazard classifications; feedback that stays brief; a memory aid that maps onto the three moves.
