## UNVERIFIED (not passed, not counted)

- U1, scene 3c and claim 15: "In 1998 a new strain of the stem rust fungus appeared in Uganda and overcame a resistance gene that many wheat varieties carried." Sources differ: a Ugandan researcher noticed it in 1998 ([Nature blog](https://blogs.nature.com/blog/fight_against_stem_rust_receiv/)); susceptible Sr31 lines were reported in a Ugandan nursery in 1998 to 1999 ([USDA-ARS](https://ars.usda.gov/midwest-area/stpaul/cereal-disease-lab/docs/emerging-virulent-stem-rust-race-ug99), [Njau et al. 2010](https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50620500/Publications/YJ/Njau2010PlantDis.pdf)); and the race is named Ug99 for its 1999 identification. "Appeared in Uganda" also implies it arose there; it was found there and its origin is not established. The gene is Sr31 (on the 1B.1R rye translocation, widely deployed in CIMMYT-derived and other wheats, so "many varieties carried" is supported: [Sr31 background](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5085093)). Suggested wording if wanted: "Between 1998 and 1999 a new strain of stem rust was found in Uganda that overcame ...". The chip would read "1998 to 1999, Uganda: new stem rust strain".
- U2, claim 4 and scenes 2c/6a: "a proven bull, one whose daughters give a lot of milk." The classic progeny test fits, but many AI bulls are now chosen by genomic (DNA-based) prediction before daughters are recorded. Not wrong for the idea taught; I did not find a source on current Australian share of genomic young sires. Optional: "a bull with high genetic merit for milk".
- U3, scene 2d / claim 10: "A cow that struggles to get pregnant has a harder life on the farm." Supported indirectly: infertility is a leading reason for involuntary culling (about 23% of culls in a US dataset, [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/cull-rates-how-is-your-farm-doing); poor fertility the top culling reason for Nordic Holsteins, [VikingGenetics](https://vikinggenetics.com/news/daughter-fertility-as-the-top-culling-reason)). The "harder life" welfare wording itself is a judgement; a sourced alternative is "is more likely to be culled".
- U4, claims 2 and 19: insemination "inside the cow's uterus" past the cervix, and frozen semen from older bulls staying usable. Ohio State extension describes depositing semen in the uterine body ([OSU](https://dairy.osu.edu/newsletter/buckeye-dairy-news/volume-12-issue-3/effect-ai-technique-and-semen-handling-dairy-cattle)), and Merino ram semen frozen in 1968 produced a 61% pregnancy rate ([report](https://www.bovinevetonline.com/news/veterinary-research/researchers-stretch-timeline-semen-viability)), so both are supported, but I found no cattle gene bank source with an Australian storage period, so "can protect variety" for cattle rests on the sheep result and general practice. Not an error.
- U5, scene 5 and notes: NESA glossary. Verified on the [NSW NESA glossary page](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/hsc/student-guide/glossary): Evaluate is "Make a judgement based on criteria. Determine the value of." The lesson's "make a judgement using criteria" is a fair paraphrase; "determine the value of" is not spoken (acceptable). "Criteria: the things you judge it by" is the lesson's own gloss, not NESA wording. The exact NESA 2017 Module 5 dot point text was not retrieved (site extract quotes it).
- U6, claim 12 and scene 3a visuals: wheat emasculation. Confirmed: spikes are emasculated before they shed pollen (anthers pulled out with forceps), the donor pollen is applied one to three days later, and the ear is bagged ([TNAU](https://agritech.tnau.ac.in/crop_improvement/crop_imprv_emasculation_cereals.html), wheat variety patents). Two small mismatches I could not source and did not count: practice usually applies pollen by placing or shaking a shedding donor head, not literally "brushing"; and the bee doodle in 3a suggests insects, though wheat is wind-pollinated and almost completely self-pollinating. Neither makes narration false.
