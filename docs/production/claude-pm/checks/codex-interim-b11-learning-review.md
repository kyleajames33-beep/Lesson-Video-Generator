## Entry point and learner trace

The stated entry knowledge supplies bacterial and viral structure, host-cell dependence, mutation and selection, and the earlier immunity lessons. It does not supply selective toxicity, the named drug mechanisms or criteria for assessing treatment effectiveness.

Main question: why do antibiotics and antivirals work differently, and how effective is each? The useful outcome is to connect a drug's target to its effect and host selectivity, then make a qualified judgement about effectiveness.

The flu case is followable through the planned sequence: the antibiotic lacks a viral target; neuraminidase normally helps new flu particles leave the cell; blocking it limits onward infection; the immune system clears existing particles. ## Additional remaining findings

### 1. Necessary misconception repair: keep the early-treatment explanation attached to the flu case

- **Location:** scene 6, lines 97 to 100; scene 10, lines 118 to 120; key note 3, line 157; author teaching checks, line 290.
- **Exact source:** scene 6 says, "So they work best early"; scene 10 says, "Antivirals are narrower and must be taken early". The day strip relights the hook's two-day bracket while the narration discusses antivirals generally.
- **Likely learner question:** "Does an antiviral stop helping once an infection is established? How can the same lesson say HIV treatment controls infection for decades?"
- **Repair:** explicitly return to flu when explaining the early-treatment advantage and label that day strip as the flu example. Remove the class-wide "must" from the assessment and notes. Keep the HIV example as evidence that antiviral treatment can also control an established infection. This is a distinction between the two cases already taught, not a request for clinical instructions or an extra list of exceptions.
- **Understanding enabled:** students can explain why limiting further spread helps in the flu example without inventing one treatment window for every antiviral. The integration owner's external cross-check is consistent with this distinction: [NIH HIV treatment basics](https://hivinfo.nih.gov/understanding-hiv/fact-sheets/hiv-treatment-basics).
- **Prior coverage:** not recorded as a finding in the three earlier B11 reports. The earlier science report explicitly accepted the class-wide timing wording.

### 2. Necessary assessment repair: answer the safety criterion with selectivity evidence

- **Location:** scene 10, lines 118 to 120.
- **Exact source:** the first criterion is "how safely they hit the pathogen", but the antibiotic column fills "many targets" and the antiviral column fills "virus-only steps: fewer targets".
- **Likely learner question:** "Does having more targets make a drug safer? Does having fewer targets make it less safe?"
- **Repair:** use the first row to recall why the examples can affect the pathogen more than the host. The earlier wall and ribosome comparison already supplies the antibiotic evidence. Use the selected antiviral example's explained selectivity for its cell. Keep the range of infections treated in the separate breadth row. Incorporate the existing Codex target-rule correction once, rather than retaining an absolute virus-only rule in this table.
- **Understanding enabled:** students distinguish selectivity from breadth and can support a safety judgement with a relevant biological reason. Target count alone does not answer the criterion the lesson asks them to judge.
- **Prior coverage:** the absolute target rule is already in `codex-check-105.md`; the mismatch between the table's safety heading and its entries is additional.

### 3. Necessary evidence repair: a one-year burden figure cannot demonstrate a trend

- **Location:** scene 10, lines 118 to 120.
- **Exact source:** "But resistance is rising. In 2021, resistant bacteria directly caused about one point one million deaths worldwide." The table writes "falling as resistance rises" beside a single dated death total.
- **Likely learner question:** "What earlier value am I comparing with to decide that resistance is rising or effectiveness is falling?"
- **Repair:** retain the dated total as evidence that resistance has a serious burden. Do not present it as evidence of a change over time. Either make the durability judgement causal and conditional, based on the selection explanation already taught, or supply genuinely comparable trend evidence if a trend claim is essential. No new dataset is necessary for the causal version.
- **Understanding enabled:** students separate evidence of a problem's size from evidence that it is increasing, which matters to this lesson's stated assessment outcome.
- **Prior coverage:** neither earlier learning/science report nor `codex-check-105.md` records this inference issue. This finding does not dispute the numerical total itself.
