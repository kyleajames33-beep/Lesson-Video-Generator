Main question traced: *How does a pathogen get out of one host and into the next, how is it built for each step, and how does knowing its route tell us where to stop it?* Useful outcome: given a disease, the student can name its mode of transmission with a reason, tell a vector from a fomite, sort a named feature as an entry or a transmission adaptation and explain how it works, compare two pathogens using "both" and "whereas", and pick the control that breaks the link the route depends on.

## Strengths to keep

- **One board carries the whole lesson.** The leave, travel and enter strip with a swappable lane makes the three modes, the two kinds of adaptation (teal at "enter", amber at "leave") and the controls (break marks across lanes) one visual system. Students can point at the link being discussed at every moment.
- **The droplet against drifting particle contrast is a real video beat.** Droplets falling inside a short bracket, then particles hanging in the lane after the host circle fades, shows why one is grouped with direct contact and the other with indirect contact. A textbook can only state this.
- **The fomite against vector correction (scene 5) answers a named site misconception** with a short causal rule ("A vector is alive") and a visible pair (handle beside mosquito).
- **The spike fitting one receptor in a row of non-matching receptors** explains host and tissue specificity without a new term load.
- **Scene 9 teaches the exam move explicitly** (feature, what it does, link to its job) and crosses a plausible wrong chip ("hooks help the tapeworm spread").
- **Scene 10 ends on a causal warning** ("scrubbing surfaces does little against tuberculosis in the air"), which is the route's stop in miniature.
- **Both attempts are genuine transfer.** Ross River virus (an Australian vector-borne case not taught in the lesson) and cholera against influenza (a structured compare with given information).
