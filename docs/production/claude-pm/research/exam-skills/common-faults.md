# Common answer-writing faults in HSC Chemistry and Biology

## Audit and counting rule

I read the official NESA HSC marking feedback for both subjects in **2020 to 2025**: 12 subject-year reports. Each count below is the number of these 12 reports with at least one question-specific comment supporting the fault. A report counts once even if several questions show the same fault. These are **recurrences in published feedback**, not estimates of the proportion of students who made the error. NESA does not give question-specific feedback for every question. The fault descriptions paraphrase NESA's “Areas for students to improve” comments and, where indicated, its descriptions of better responses.

## Build an argument with enough precision

| Fault, in plain terms | Recurrence | Question-specific support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Use broad or imprecise scientific language.** Generic words such as “pathogen”, “impact”, “equipment” or “move” obscure the exact organism, ion, structure, process or observation needed to support the answer. Incorrect paired terms also change the meaning. | **12/12**: Biology 6/6, Chemistry 6/6 | B20 Q32(b)(i); B21 Q28(a); B22 Q31(b); B23 Q31; B24 Q30(b); B25 Q30(a). C20 Q32; C21 Q30; C22 Q23; C23 Q25(b); C24 Q33; C25 Q30(a). |
| **List data or describe a broad trend without using values to support the inference.** Students need to read the axes, identify a meaningful pattern or comparison, then connect relevant values to the conclusion. | **9/12**: Biology 6/6, Chemistry 3/6 | B20 Q25(a); B21 Q33(b); B22 Q24(b); B23 Q25(c); B24 Q27; B25 Q28(b). C20 Q34; C23 Q35(b); C25 Q26(b). |
| **Compare as two separate descriptions.** A comparison must line up the same feature for both cases and state the similarity or difference. An unpaired list makes the relationship hard to see. | **8/12**: Biology 5/6, Chemistry 3/6 | B20 Q31(b); B22 Q30; B23 Q28, Q33; B24 Q30(a); B25 Q23. C21 Q34; C22 Q36; C23 Q35(b). |

## Use in the exam skills videos

The strongest cross-subject training targets are: mark every part of the demand, use the stimulus as evidence, and make the cause-and-effect link visible. For a comparison, align the same feature across both cases. For a judgement, state the decision and use relevant evidence to support it. Calculation and graph lessons should show how a reader can trace the working, quantities, units, axes and plotted evidence. These are teaching inferences from the recurrence audit, not NESA marking rules.
