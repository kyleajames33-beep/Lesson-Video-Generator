# HSC Chemistry equilibrium question types, 2019 to 2025

Prepared 14 October 2026 for the Claude-directed Module 5 run. Research only: no lesson, script, audio or render was changed.

## Scope, method and limits

- **Papers read:** all seven NESA HSC Chemistry papers set on the 2017 syllabus (2019 to 2025), their marking guidelines (MC keys, criteria, sample answers, mapping grids) and the NESA "HSC marking feedback" for each year. Every question was read in full and classified by hand, not by keyword.
- **In scope:** static and dynamic equilibrium, open and closed systems, non-equilibrium systems (enthalpy, entropy, Gibbs), collision theory and rates, Le Chatelier's principle (concentration, temperature, volume and pressure, catalysts), activation energy and heat of reaction, Keq expressions and calculations, Q, temperature and K, industrial equilibrium.
- **Excluded but counted:** Ksp and solubility; acid dissociation (Ka, Kb, pKa, Kw). Counts are in the table in section 2.
- **Owner steer applied:** in Chemistry a correct answer with clear working and no classic mistakes earns the marks, so this report does not catalogue mark allocation. Marking guidelines and marker feedback are used only as evidence of the correct method and of the mistakes students commonly make.
- **Copyright:** no question is reproduced. Each is described by type, structure and stimulus, with year and question number as a reference. Section 6 examples are **authored for HSCScience**, not NESA questions.
- **Difficulty evidence:** NESA exam packs do not publish per-question means. "Hardest" in section 3 is judged from (a) how often and how heavily markers reported errors, (b) the structure of the task, and (c) one independent paper analysis (ACE, 2023). Treat it as a reasoned ranking, not a statistic.

## 1. Headline findings

1. Equilibrium (excluding Ksp and Ka) is worth **15 to 27 marks of 100 every year**: about 4 multiple choice plus 3 or 4 short answers of 2 to 7 marks. 2023 was the heaviest (27 marks).
2. The **most frequent written task** is "explain the change with collision theory (rates), and say what happens to the named species". It appeared in 5 of 7 years, always 3 to 5 marks, and the same errors recur every year.
3. **Graph tasks** (sketch or read concentration, amount, rate or Q against time) appear in 6 of 7 years, as both MC and short answer.
4. The **hardest calculation type** is "how much was added to reach this new equilibrium" (2021, 2023, 2024). Our C6 and C8 videos do not teach it.
5. **No-shift traps** (catalyst, added solid, inert gas at fixed volume) appear in 5 of 7 years, mostly as MC, and as a 4-mark written question in 2025.
6. There was **no extended "Haber compromise" essay** in 2019 to 2025. Industrial equilibrium appears as MC yield questions and short flow-chart explanations.
7. Ksp/solubility appears every year (23 items in total); acid dissociation appears every year (about 18 items in total).

## 3. The hardest types and why

1. **T15, amount added to reach a new equilibrium (2021 Q31, 2023 Q37(b), 2024 Q30).** ACE rated 2023 Q37 "Hard" and listed Q37(b) among the most novel questions of that paper. It is hard because the unknown sits in the initial row, the change row must come from the one new equilibrium value, the added substance may play two roles (2023: CO2 both raised its own concentration and was consumed to make CO), and moles must be converted to concentration in a vessel that may not be 1 L. Markers reported mole-ratio errors, moles used as concentrations and students assuming K changed.
2. **T3, collision-theory explanations (5 of 7 years).** The same faults recur every year: Le Chatelier's principle written as the cause, "forward reaction" written where "forward rate" is meant, collision frequency not linked to rate, the named species not mentioned, and (2025) Ea believed to change with temperature, or the energy profile read as an equilibrium graph. Students know the conclusion but do not give the causal chain.
3. **T4, graphs with a disturbance, especially amount (mol) plots (2023 Q33(b)).** ACE named "the y-axis plots moles, not concentration" as the main challenge, and NESA feedback says the same. A volume change makes concentrations jump but not amounts, so students who memorised "volume change gives a jump" ruled out the right answer. Sketches lose credibility through jumps in the wrong species and curves not in stoichiometric proportion (2019 Q25(a), 2025 Q27(c)).
4. **T14, approximations with extreme K (2019 Q31, 2023 MC20).** A very large K with a products-only start needs the reverse ICE and a "small x" argument from the other side. ACE called 2023 MC20 challenging. Markers in 2019 criticised unexplained or unjustified approximations.
5. **T6, no-shift traps written up as explanations (2025 Q29).** Argon at fixed volume: students applied LCP to a change that is not a disturbance, or argued pressure rises so temperature rises. The fix is a Q = K (or "concentrations unchanged, rates still equal") argument.
6. **T9, ΔG reasoning.** Sign handling of TΔS (2024 Q37), extrapolating a graph beyond its range (2021 Q33), and confusing "how far" with "how fast".

Easiest by comparison: T1 definitions, T10 expressions and T11 direct K (the errors there are arithmetic and solids in expressions).

## 4. How to recognise each type, the method, and the classic mistakes

Each entry gives: how to recognise it, the method with the working to show, the classic mistakes and how to avoid each, and the hard variants seen in 2019 to 2025.

### T1 Define or identify equilibrium
- **Recognise:** "which must be equal", "which is NOT a feature", a plateau on a graph, an isotope or labelled-particle experiment.
- **Method:** (1) state that forward and reverse rates are equal and non-zero; (2) so the amount of each substance stays constant; (3) in a closed system; (4) point to the evidence (flat lines from time X, labelled atoms appearing in both phases).
- **Mistakes:** equal concentrations (fix: "equal rates, not equal amounts"); "the reaction has stopped" (fix: both rates continue, the isotope test proves exchange).
- **Hard variant:** labelled solid added to a saturated solution: label ends up in both solid and solution because dissolving and crystallising continue (2019 MC11).

### T2 Predict and justify a shift
- **Recognise:** "justify TWO ways", "identify what happens to the amount of", "what happens to the colour".
- **Method:** (1) name the change precisely; (2) state which reaction is favoured and why (ΔH sign for temperature; count of gas particles for volume; which species was added or removed); (3) state the effect on the named species or colour; (4) for a "justify" verb, add the rate reason (T3) in one sentence.
- **Mistakes:** a general LCP sentence with no system detail; applying pressure to aqueous systems; forgetting water is a liquid; "shifts right" without saying what that does to the asked species.
- **Hard variants:** compression changes concentrations before the shift, so a statement about ratios is what survives (2022 MC14); a method that removes a species from a complex equilibrium drags the equilibrium and spoils the measurement (2022 Q31(a)).

### T3 Collision-theory explanation (the structure to drill)
- **Recognise:** "using collision theory, explain", "explain the rate", an energy profile diagram supplied, or "how do collision theory and Le Chatelier's principle account for".
- **Method (concentration change):** (1) state the change and the species; (2) fewer (or more) particles of that species, so fewer (or more) collisions per second in the reaction that uses it, so that rate falls (rises); (3) the other rate is unchanged at that instant, so it is now larger; (4) net reaction in that direction, so the named species changes (give the direction); (5) as concentrations change, the faster rate falls and the slower rises until they are equal again: new equilibrium.
- **Method (temperature change):** (1) read ΔH and the two activation energies from the equation or diagram (higher Ea belongs to the endothermic direction); (2) heating raises the average kinetic energy, so a larger fraction of collisions exceed Ea in both directions; both rates rise; (3) the direction with the higher Ea gains proportionally more; (4) net reaction that way, named species and colour change; (5) rates converge to equal at a new, higher level; (6) if asked, K changes (rises if products increased). Cooling is the mirror: both rates fall, the higher-Ea direction falls more.
- **Mistakes and fixes:** "LCP makes it shift" (fix: LCP describes the outcome; the cause is the rate change); "the forward reaction increases" (fix: say "the forward rate increases"); no link to the named species (fix: finish with "so [X] increases"); "Ea decreases when heated" (fix: Ea is fixed; the fraction of particles above it changes); reading the energy profile as a concentration graph.
- **Hard variants:** both models required plus the energy profile (2025 Q35); a temperature change asked as an effect on K (2020 Q26(b)); rate during the approach to equilibrium rather than after a disturbance (2024 Q26(b)).

### T4 Concentration-time and amount-time graphs
- **Recognise:** partly drawn axes and "sketch", or "what disturbance could cause".
- **Method (sketch):** (1) mark the disturbance time; (2) only the added or removed species jumps vertically (a volume change makes every gas concentration jump by the same factor; a temperature change makes nothing jump); (3) curves start steepest and flatten; (4) changes in the mole ratio of the equation (for N2 + 3H2: N2 falls 1 part, H2 falls 3, NH3 rises 2); (5) new ruled flat lines; an added species settles above its old level but below its jump.
- **Method (interpret):** read the axis label first (concentration or amount). List the candidate disturbances and rule each in or out by its fingerprint: addition (one jump), volume (all concentrations jump; amounts do not), temperature (no jump, gradual change), catalyst (no change at equilibrium).
- **Mistakes:** jumps in species that were not added; curves not proportional; equilibrium lines drawn curved or sloping; mol axis treated as concentration; starting a curve at zero against the data.
- **Hard variants:** amount-time plot with a volume change (2023 Q33(b)); approach-to-equilibrium sketch from data, not a disturbance (2024 Q26(a), 2025 MC5).

### T5 Rate-time and Q-time graphs
- **Recognise:** two lines labelled forward and reverse rate; or Q on the vertical axis.
- **Method:** fingerprints. Temperature: both rates step the same way at once, unequally, then converge (heating: endothermic rate steps up more). Concentration added: one rate steps up, the other starts at the old level. Catalyst: both step up equally and stay equal. Volume decrease (gas): both step up, the side with more gas particles more. Q: moves monotonically toward K and levels; if the forward rate is larger, Q rises.
- **Mistakes:** choosing a single-rate step for temperature; forgetting that rates converge to a new common level, not the old one.
- **Hard variant:** read ΔH from the graph (2023 MC18); Q-time instead of rate-time (2024 MC18).

### T6 No-shift traps
- **Recognise:** catalyst, "solid X is added", inert or noble gas, "fixed volume", equal gas counts.
- **Method:** test with Q. Does the change alter any concentration in the K expression? If not, Q = K and nothing shifts. Catalyst: lowers Ea for both directions, both rates rise by the same factor, equilibrium reached sooner, same mixture.
- **Mistakes:** catalyst helping one direction; solid in the K expression; argon "raises pressure so shifts"; argon raises temperature.
- **Hard variants:** argon asked as an effect on temperature (2025 Q29); two containers with different amounts of solid giving the same gas concentration (2024 MC15).

### T7 Temperature and K
- **Recognise:** K values at two temperatures, colour change with heating, "effect on the value of Keq".
- **Method:** (1) from the observation, decide which direction heating favoured; (2) that direction is endothermic; (3) K = products over reactants, so if products increased, K increased; (4) only temperature changes K. For data tables, calculate K for each experiment first, then compare.
- **Mistakes:** confusing equilibrium position with K; "K changes" for a concentration change; "shifts right" instead of naming species.
- **Hard variant:** two experiments approached from opposite starts with only equilibrium values to compare (2025 MC14).

### T8 Industrial equilibrium
- **Recognise:** named process, flow chart, "maximise yield", "excess", "justify the conditions".
- **Method:** for each condition, one cause-effect sentence: condition, effect on position (yield) or rate, and the cost or safety reason. Excess cheap reactant raises the yield of product from the expensive one. Catalyst lowers Ea, so an acceptable rate at a lower temperature, saving time and energy (not "just speeds up"). Removing product and recycling unreacted gases. For an exothermic forward reaction, low temperature favours yield but slows rate: compromise.
- **Mistakes:** not using the flow chart; listing conditions without effects; "catalyst increases yield".
- **Hard variants:** deduce ΔH sign and gas count from a yield-temperature-pressure graph (2020 MC16); a downstream reactor consuming one species from an upstream equilibrium (2023 Q26(a)).

### T9 Enthalpy, entropy, ΔG; open vs closed
- **Recognise:** ΔH, ΔS, TΔS or ΔG data; "proceeds to completion rather than equilibrium"; beaker vs sealed bottle.
- **Method:** (1) state sign of ΔH (heat released favours) and of ΔS (more spread favours); (2) ΔG = ΔH - TΔS, with consistent kJ units; (3) negative ΔG favoured; zero means equilibrium; large negative means effectively complete; (4) open system: a product escapes, Q stays below K, the change continues to completion; closed: Q reaches K.
- **Mistakes:** sign slips with -TΔS; quoting numbers without interpreting; extrapolating graphs; "fast" for "favoured".
- **Hard variants:** K vs ΔG graph (2024 Q37); deductions at three temperatures from ΔH and TΔS lines (2021 Q33).

### T10 and T11 Expressions and direct K
- **Method:** write the expression from the given equation first; leave out (s) and (l); convert moles to concentration (divide by the vessel volume); substitute; check exponents.
- **Mistakes:** solids or water included; mol used directly in a vessel that is not 1 L; substituting before writing the expression.

### T12 ICE from initial amounts plus one equilibrium value
- **Recognise:** starting amounts plus one measured value at equilibrium (an amount, a concentration, an absorbance).
- **Method:** (1) concentrations in the I row; (2) the one known change, with its sign; (3) every other change from the mole ratio; (4) E = I + C; (5) K from E values only; (6) check no E value is negative. If the measurement is an absorbance, read the concentration off a well-scaled calibration line (ruled line of best fit) first.
- **Mistakes:** using the equilibrium value as the change; mole ratio not applied; building an ICE table when equilibrium values are given.
- **Hard variant:** relationships supplied in place of a balanced ICE (2020 Q35); calibration graph to draw first (2023 Q31).

### T13 Q vs K
- **Method:** write Q in exactly the K form; substitute the current concentrations; compare. Q < K: net forward. Q > K: net reverse. Q = K: at equilibrium, no net change.
- **Mistakes:** solid included; using moles in a vessel that is not 1 L; comparing with a K for a different temperature.

### T14 Concentrations from K
- **Method:** (1) I row; (2) x from the coefficients (2x for a coefficient of 2); (3) E row; (4) choose: perfect square (take the square root), small K (x small compared with starting values: say so, then check), very large K (start from the product side so x is small), or a quadratic (reject the root that gives a negative concentration); (5) back-substitute to check K.
- **Mistakes:** approximation used without stating it; approximation used when x is not small; wrong root.

### T15 Amount added to reach a new equilibrium
- **Recognise:** "how many moles must be added", "an unknown amount was added", a new equilibrium value given afterwards.
- **Method:** (1) decide the direction of the shift from LCP (it tells you the signs); (2) I row: the old equilibrium values, with "+ y" on the added species; (3) C row from the one species whose new equilibrium value is given, then the mole ratio; (4) E row in terms of y; (5) K is unchanged at fixed temperature; substitute and solve for y; (6) convert to moles if the vessel is not 1 L; (7) check by substituting back.
- **Mistakes:** forgetting the added species is also consumed or produced in the shift; mole ratios; moles in K; assuming K changes; putting y in the change row.
- **Hard variant:** heterogeneous system where the added gas must supply two increases (2023 Q37(b)).

#### T3 Collision-theory explanation

**A3.1 (authored).** A rigid sealed container holds H2(g) + CO2(g) ⇌ H2O(g) + CO(g) at equilibrium. A drying agent is added that removes some H2O(g) without changing the temperature. Using collision theory, explain the effect on the concentration of CO(g). (3 marks style)
*Answer:* Removing H2O lowers [H2O], so fewer H2O and CO molecules collide per second, and the reverse rate falls. The forward rate is unchanged at that instant, so it is now faster than the reverse rate. Net forward reaction produces CO, so [CO] increases. As [H2] and [CO2] fall and [CO] rises, the forward rate falls and the reverse rate rises until they are equal again at a new equilibrium.
*Classic slips:* "LCP shifts it right" as the only reason; never naming [CO].

**A3.2 (authored, energy profile).** N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g). An energy profile shows Ea(forward) = 120 kJ mol⁻¹ and Ea(reverse) = 63 kJ mol⁻¹. The sealed mixture is heated. Using collision theory and the diagram, explain why the mixture becomes darker brown, and state the effect on K. (5 marks style)
*Answer:* ΔH = 120 - 63 = +57 kJ mol⁻¹, so the forward reaction is endothermic and has the higher Ea. Heating raises the average kinetic energy, so a larger fraction of collisions have at least Ea in both directions, and both rates increase. The forward reaction, with the higher Ea, gains proportionally more, so the forward rate exceeds the reverse rate. Net forward reaction raises [NO2] (brown). The rates converge until equal at a new equilibrium. Products increased, so K = [NO2]²/[N2O4] increases. Ea itself does not change.

**A3.3 (authored, catalyst).** For 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g), explain why adding V2O5 catalyst to an equilibrium mixture does not change the amount of SO3, but is still used industrially. (3 marks style)
*Answer:* The catalyst provides a pathway with lower Ea for both directions, so both rates increase by the same factor and stay equal; the composition is unchanged. In a plant that does not reach equilibrium, the faster rate gives more SO3 per hour and allows a lower temperature, which saves energy and favours the exothermic forward reaction.

#### T4 Concentration-time and amount-time graphs

**A4.1 (authored).** N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g) is at equilibrium in a rigid container. At time t1 some N2 is injected. Sketch [N2], [H2] and [NH3] from t1 until a new equilibrium.
*Answer features:* only [N2] jumps vertically at t1, then curves down and levels above its old value but below the jump; [H2] has no jump and curves down, falling three times as much as N2 falls after the jump; [NH3] curves up, rising twice as much as N2 falls; all curves are steepest at t1 and level off together on ruled flat lines.

**A4.2 (authored, mol vs concentration; reuses the checked compression numbers in the harder-question companions).** 2NO2(g) ⇌ N2O4(g), 1.00 L, 300 K: 0.200 mol NO2 and 0.300 mol N2O4 at equilibrium (Kc = 7.50). The volume is halved at constant temperature. (a) Describe the concentration-time graph. (b) Describe the amount-time graph. 
*Answer:* (a) Both concentrations jump at once to 0.400 and 0.600 mol L⁻¹; then [NO2] curves down to 0.295 and [N2O4] curves up to 0.653 mol L⁻¹ (NO2 falls twice as much as N2O4 rises). (b) No jumps: n(NO2) curves down from 0.200 to 0.147 mol and n(N2O4) curves up from 0.300 to 0.326 mol. The vertical jump in (a) is the moment of the change, not the reaction.

**A4.3 (authored, interpret).** An amount-time graph for 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g), ΔH = -198 kJ mol⁻¹, shows that at time t no amount jumps, and afterwards SO3 decreases while SO2 and O2 increase. Explain two different changes that could cause this.
*Answer:* (1) Heating: no amount changes instantly; the reverse (endothermic) rate increases more, K falls, Q > K, so SO3 decomposes. (2) Increasing the volume: amounts do not jump on a mol plot (concentrations would), every gas is less crowded, the forward reaction (3 gas particles to 2) loses more rate than the reverse, so net reverse. Adding a species is ruled out (it would jump); a catalyst is ruled out (no change).

#### T5 Rate-time graphs

**A5.1 (authored).** At time t, for an exothermic forward reaction at equilibrium, both rates jump upward, the reverse rate more, then the reverse falls and the forward rises until they meet above the original level. Identify the change. *Answer:* temperature increase.

**A5.2 (authored).** At time t the forward rate jumps up, the reverse rate starts from its old level, and both meet at a level higher than before. Identify the change. *Answer:* a reactant was added (concentration increase). A catalyst would raise both rates equally at once.

#### T6 No-shift traps

**A6.1 (authored).** Argon is added to the NO2/N2O4 mixture in a rigid container at constant temperature. What happens to the colour and to the temperature? *Answer:* nothing. Concentrations of NO2 and N2O4 are unchanged, so Q = K and the rates stay equal; there is no net reaction, so no heat is absorbed or released.

**A6.2 (authored).** CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g) is at equilibrium. Two identical sealed containers at the same temperature hold 10 g and 20 g of CaCO3 with excess CaO. Compare [CO2]. *Answer:* equal (ratio 1 : 1), because K = [CO2] only.

**A6.3 (authored).** For H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), the container volume is halved. The colour darkens. Has the equilibrium shifted? *Answer:* no. Gas particles are equal on both sides, so both rates rise by the same factor; the darker colour is the higher I2 concentration from compression, not a shift.

#### T7 Temperature and K

**A7.1 (authored).** For A(g) ⇌ 2B(g), K = 2.5 × 10³ at 400 K and K = 3.1 at 600 K. Is the forward reaction endothermic or exothermic? *Answer:* K fell on heating, so heating favours the reverse reaction; the reverse is endothermic and the forward reaction is exothermic.

**A7.2 (authored, two experiments).** N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g), ΔH = +57 kJ mol⁻¹. Experiment 1 at equilibrium: [N2O4] = 0.0400, [NO2] = 0.0200 mol L⁻¹. Experiment 2 at equilibrium: [N2O4] = 0.0300, [NO2] = 0.0600 mol L⁻¹. Which experiment was at the higher temperature? *Answer:* K1 = 0.0200²/0.0400 = 0.0100; K2 = 0.0600²/0.0300 = 0.120. K is larger in experiment 2 and the forward reaction is endothermic, so experiment 2 was hotter.

#### T8 Industrial equilibrium

**A8.1 (authored).** 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g), ΔH = -198 kJ mol⁻¹. A plant uses about 430 °C, a V2O5 catalyst, about 1 to 2 atm and excess air. Justify each condition. *Answer:* lower temperature favours the exothermic forward reaction, but too low makes the rate too slow, so a moderate temperature is a compromise; the catalyst lowers Ea so the rate is acceptable at that temperature; the yield is already high, so the cost of high pressure is not justified even though fewer gas particles are formed; excess air (cheap O2) shifts the equilibrium toward SO3 and uses more of the SO2.

**A8.2 (authored, graph).** A graph shows the equilibrium yield of product Z rising with temperature, and at any temperature the yield is higher at 20 MPa than at 5 MPa. What can you deduce about the reaction? *Answer:* the forward reaction is endothermic (yield rises on heating) and produces fewer gas particles than it uses (yield rises with pressure).

#### T9 Enthalpy, entropy and ΔG; open vs closed

**A9.1 (authored).** Calcium carbonate is heated strongly in an open crucible and, separately, in a sealed steel vessel at the same temperature. Explain why decomposition is complete in one and not the other. *Answer:* in the open crucible CO2 escapes, so [CO2] stays below K and the decomposition continues until no CaCO3 remains; in the sealed vessel [CO2] rises until Q = K, and an equilibrium with CaCO3 remaining is set up.

**A9.2 (authored).** N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g): ΔH = -92 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS = -199 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. (a) Calculate ΔG at 298 K. (b) Above what temperature is ΔG positive? *Answer:* (a) TΔS = 298 × (-0.199) = -59.3 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔG = -92 - (-59.3) = -32.7 kJ mol⁻¹. (b) ΔG = 0 at T = 92 / 0.199 = 462 K; above about 462 K, ΔG is positive (assuming ΔH and ΔS stay constant).

#### T10 and T11 Expression and direct K

**B11.1 (authored).** 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g). A 2.00 L vessel at equilibrium contains 0.300 mol SO2, 0.150 mol O2 and 0.600 mol SO3. Calculate K. *Answer:* concentrations 0.150, 0.0750, 0.300 mol L⁻¹; K = 0.300²/(0.150² × 0.0750) = 53.3. Using moles directly gives 26.7 (wrong).

**B11.2 (authored).** PCl5(g) ⇌ PCl3(g) + Cl2(g). A 0.500 L vessel at equilibrium holds 0.0400 mol PCl5, 0.0300 mol PCl3 and 0.0300 mol Cl2. Calculate K. *Answer:* 0.0800, 0.0600, 0.0600 mol L⁻¹; K = 0.0600²/0.0800 = 0.0450 (moles directly give 0.0225, wrong).

**B10.1 (authored).** Write K for Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) ⇌ Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), and for CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g). *Answer:* K = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺]; K = [CO2].

#### T12 ICE from start plus one value

**B12.1 (authored).** 0.800 mol N2O4 is placed in an empty 2.00 L flask: N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g). At equilibrium [NO2] = 0.120 mol L⁻¹. Calculate K. *Answer:* I: 0.400, 0; C: -0.0600, +0.120; E: 0.340, 0.120; K = 0.120²/0.340 = 0.0424. Slip: subtracting 0.120 from N2O4.

**B12.2 (authored).** 0.400 mol SO2 and 0.300 mol O2 are placed in a 2.00 L vessel. At equilibrium [SO3] = 0.160 mol L⁻¹. Calculate K for 2SO2 + O2 ⇌ 2SO3. *Answer:* I: 0.200, 0.150, 0; C: -0.160, -0.0800, +0.160; E: 0.0400, 0.0700, 0.160; K = 0.160²/(0.0400² × 0.0700) = 229.

**B12.3 (authored, calibration variant).** 10.0 mL of 4.00 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ Fe³⁺ is mixed with 10.0 mL of 3.00 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ SCN⁻: Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ ⇌ FeSCN²⁺. The calibration line is absorbance = 4250 × [FeSCN²⁺], and the mixture reads 1.70. Calculate K. *Answer:* after mixing, initial [Fe³⁺] = 2.00 × 10⁻³ and [SCN⁻] = 1.50 × 10⁻³ (halved); [FeSCN²⁺] = 1.70/4250 = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴; E: 1.60 × 10⁻³, 1.10 × 10⁻³, 4.00 × 10⁻⁴; K = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ / (1.60 × 10⁻³ × 1.10 × 10⁻³) = 227. Slip: forgetting the mixing dilution.

#### T13 Q vs K

**B13.1 (authored).** N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, K = 0.105 at this temperature. A mixture has [N2] = 0.500, [H2] = 0.400, [NH3] = 0.100 mol L⁻¹. Which way will it go? *Answer:* Q = 0.100²/(0.500 × 0.400³) = 0.313 > K, so net reverse: NH3 decomposes and [N2] rises.

**B13.2 (authored, heterogeneous).** C(s) + CO2(g) ⇌ 2CO(g), K = 1.90. [CO2] = 0.500, [CO] = 0.800 mol L⁻¹, excess carbon. *Answer:* Q = 0.800²/0.500 = 1.28 < K, net forward. Carbon is not in Q.

**B13.3 (authored).** H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI, K = 49.0. [H2] = 0.200, [I2] = 0.0500, [HI] = 0.700 mol L⁻¹. *Answer:* Q = 0.700²/(0.200 × 0.0500) = 49.0 = K: at equilibrium, even though the amounts are unequal.

#### T14 Concentrations from K

**B14.1 (authored, quadratic with root rejection).** PCl5(g) ⇌ PCl3(g) + Cl2(g), K = 0.0450. [PCl5] starts at 0.200 mol L⁻¹ with no products. Find the equilibrium concentrations. *Answer:* x²/(0.200 - x) = 0.0450, so x² + 0.0450x - 0.00900 = 0; roots x = 0.0750 or -0.120. Reject -0.120 (negative concentration). [PCl5] = 0.125, [PCl3] = [Cl2] = 0.0750 mol L⁻¹. Check: 0.0750²/0.125 = 0.0450.

**B14.2 (authored, perfect square and small x).** N2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2NO(g), K = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴. [N2] = [O2] = 0.500 mol L⁻¹ initially. Find [NO]. *Answer:* (2x)²/(0.500 - x)² = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴, square root: 2x/(0.500 - x) = 0.020, x = 0.00495, [NO] = 9.90 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹. Small-x shortcut (0.500 - x ≈ 0.500) gives 1.0 × 10⁻² mol L⁻¹, about 1% high: acceptable to 2 significant figures, and the student must state the assumption.

**B14.3 (authored, very large K, start from products).** 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g), K = 4.0 × 10¹⁰. 0.400 mol SO3 only is placed in a 1.00 L vessel. Find [O2] at equilibrium. *Answer:* K is huge, so very little SO3 decomposes: let [O2] = x, [SO2] = 2x, [SO3] = 0.400 - 2x ≈ 0.400. 0.400²/((2x)² x) = 4.0 × 10¹⁰ gives x³ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹², x = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹; [SO2] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴. Check: 0.400 - 0.0002 ≈ 0.400, and K evaluates to 4.0 × 10¹⁰ to 2 significant figures.

#### T15 Amount added to reach a new equilibrium

**B15.1 (authored, full worked example).** CO(g) + H2O(g) ⇌ CO2(g) + H2(g), K = 4.00, in a 1.00 L vessel at equilibrium: [CO] = [H2O] = 0.200, [CO2] = [H2] = 0.400 mol L⁻¹. Steam is added at constant temperature until the new equilibrium [CO] = 0.100 mol L⁻¹. How many moles of steam were added? *Answer:* adding steam shifts forward. I: 0.200, 0.200 + y, 0.400, 0.400; C: -0.100, -0.100, +0.100, +0.100; E: 0.100, 0.100 + y, 0.500, 0.500. K unchanged: 0.500²/(0.100 × (0.100 + y)) = 4.00, so 0.100 + y = 0.625, y = 0.525 mol. Check: 0.250/0.0625 = 4.00.

**B15.2 (authored, non-1 L vessel and a cube).** N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g). A 2.00 L vessel at equilibrium holds 1.000 mol N2, 0.800 mol H2 and 0.400 mol NH3. Hydrogen is added at constant temperature until the vessel holds 0.600 mol NH3 at the new equilibrium. How many moles of H2 were added? *Answer:* K = 0.200²/(0.500 × 0.400³) = 1.25. NH3 rose by 0.200 mol, so N2 fell 0.100 mol (0.900 mol) and H2 fell 0.300 mol. New concentrations: [NH3] = 0.300, [N2] = 0.450, [H2] = (0.500 + y)/2.00. 0.300²/(0.450 × [H2]³) = 1.25 gives [H2]³ = 0.160, [H2] = 0.543 mol L⁻¹, so 0.500 + y = 1.086 and y = 0.586 mol. Slips: using moles in K (gives 0.184 mol), forgetting the cube, or forgetting the 0.300 mol of H2 consumed.

**B15.3 (authored, heterogeneous attempt).** C(s) + CO2(g) ⇌ 2CO(g), 1.00 L, excess carbon, at equilibrium [CO2] = 0.400 and [CO] = 0.400 mol L⁻¹. CO2 is added at constant temperature until the new equilibrium [CO] = 0.600 mol L⁻¹. How many moles of CO2 were added? *Answer:* K = 0.400²/0.400 = 0.400. CO rose 0.200, so 0.100 mol CO2 was consumed. New [CO2] = 0.600²/0.400 = 0.900 = 0.400 + y - 0.100, so y = 0.600 mol. Slip: answering 0.500 mol by forgetting that some added CO2 was used to make CO.
