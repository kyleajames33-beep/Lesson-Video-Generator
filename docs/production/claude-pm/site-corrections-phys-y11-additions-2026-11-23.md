### FA2 L11 (`fa2-waves/lesson11.sa.html`)

Live re-check: Live 23 Nov: "ionise atoms" (UV, with 300 nm in the design rows), "these waves pass through the body harmlessly" (radio, microwave, infrared) and "$E = hf$ The energy of an EM wave (per photon)" and "find the energy of an EM wave" all found. Contradicts fa2-v14.

From `lessons/phys-y11-fa2-v14.md`:

- **Site corrections (quoted from site lesson 11, read 2 Nov; for `../site-corrections-phys-y11.md`; none of these rows is logged yet):**
  1. Key terms: "$E = hf$ The energy of an EM wave (per photon)"; learning intention "Use $E = hf$ to find the energy of an EM wave"; misconception 1 "Right: Energy depends on frequency ($E = hf$). Longer wavelength means lower frequency, so less energy". E = hf is the energy of one photon; the energy a wave or beam delivers also depends on the number of photons (its intensity), so a bright radio beam can deliver more energy than a faint X-ray beam. Suggest: "E = hf gives the energy of one photon of the wave"; "Longer wavelength means lower frequency, so less energy per photon".
  2. Card 3: "At the high-frequency end (UV, X-rays, gamma rays), the photon energy $E = hf$ is large enough to ionise atoms" and short-answer Q1 model answer for 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ m ultraviolet: "This greater energy is enough to ionise atoms and break chemical bonds in living tissue (damaging DNA)". A 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ m photon carries 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J (about 4.1 eV), well below the roughly 10 eV or more needed to ionise the atoms in living tissue; ultraviolet of this wavelength damages DNA by breaking and re-forming chemical bonds when its photons are absorbed, not by ionising. Only the shortest-wavelength ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays ionise. Suggest: "UV photons carry enough energy to break chemical bonds in DNA, which infrared photons cannot; the shortest UV, X-rays and gamma rays can also ionise atoms."
  3. Card 3: "At the low-frequency end (radio, microwave, infrared), the energy per photon is far too small to ionise, so these waves pass through the body harmlessly (though intense microwaves can still heat tissue)." Infrared does not pass through the body: it is absorbed in the outer layers of skin (that is how it warms us), and microwaves are absorbed within a few centimetres. Suggest: "the energy per photon is far too small to ionise, so these waves do not damage DNA directly; radio waves largely pass through the body, while microwaves and infrared are absorbed and can heat tissue."
  - Checked and correct: card 1 (order; higher frequency, shorter wavelength); card 2 (both equations; h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s); `we-1` (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m; 1.989 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ J); quick check (A, 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J); activity 1 values (3.0 m and 6.6 × 10⁻²⁶ J; 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz and 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J); two truths and a lie (C); match-up; Q2 and Q3 model answers.

  1. Hook: "A long thin wire glows and gets hot, while a short fat wire of the same metal barely warms up." This is true only if the two carry the same current (for example, one after the other in the same circuit). Connected separately across the same supply, the short fat wire carries the larger current and heats more. Suggest adding "when the same current passes through both".
  2. Card 2 worked example step 3: "same material and temperature means the collision rate per electron is unchanged. A thicker wire simply offers more paths". The two disagree. The first is right: the collision rate per electron depends on the material and temperature, not the thickness. The collision rate per electron is the same; each electron makes more collisions only because it has further to go.
