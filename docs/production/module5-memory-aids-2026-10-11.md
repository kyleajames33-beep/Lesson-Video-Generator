## How to use these in videos

- Teach the idea first, then give the aid as a hook to keep it. A mnemonic never replaces the explanation (see AGENTS.md: explain each cause before relying on it).
- Say why the aid works whenever that is short. "Write heat into the equation" is memorable because the student can see *why* heating shifts an exothermic reaction left.
- One or two aids per video is usually right. Put the strongest one in the "Key notes: what to write in your book" hold.
- Each entry is tagged: **Mnemonic** (a phrase to recall a list or rule), **Shortcut** (a faster method), **Insight** (a way of seeing it that makes the rule obvious) or **Trap** (a specific mistake it prevents). **Extension** marks content beyond the core syllabus dot points.

## Chemistry Module 5

### Static and dynamic equilibrium

**"Equal rates, not equal amounts."** *Insight / Trap*
At dynamic equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal. Concentrations stay constant but need not equal each other. Picture two cities with equal traffic flowing each way: the populations stay steady, but one city can still be much bigger.

**Equilibrium needs a closed system.** *Insight*
In a sealed soft drink, carbon dioxide keeps leaving and re-entering the liquid. Open the bottle and gas escapes into the room, so less returns and the drink goes flat. Being sealed is necessary, but sealing alone doesn't prove equilibrium has been reached.

### Non-equilibrium systems, enthalpy and entropy

**Gas moles are the biggest entropy clue.** *Shortcut*
More moles of gas on one side means that side has higher entropy. After that: solid < liquid < gas, and dissolving a solid usually increases entropy (dissolving a gas decreases it).

**"Enthalpy wins cold, entropy wins hot."** *Insight*
In ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, the entropy term is multiplied by T. So when ΔH and ΔS have the same sign, temperature decides:

| ΔH | ΔS | Spontaneous? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| − | + | Always |
| + | − | Never |
| − | − | At low temperature (enthalpy wins cold) |
| + | + | At high temperature (entropy wins hot) |

Students only need to remember the bottom two rows. The top two are common sense.

**Units in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS.** *Trap* (very common lost mark)
T must be in kelvin. ΔS is usually given in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ while ΔH is in kJ mol⁻¹, so divide ΔS by 1000 before combining them.

### Le Chatelier's principle

**"Write heat into the equation."** *Shortcut / Insight* (highest value in the module)
For an exothermic reaction, write heat on the product side: A + B ⇌ C + heat. For an endothermic reaction, put it on the reactant side. Now heating is like adding heat, so the equilibrium shifts away from the heat side. This is a bookkeeping aid for predicting direction: heat is not a concentration and never goes into the K expression.

**"Change T, change K. Change anything else, K stays."** *Mnemonic / Trap*
Only temperature changes the value of K. Concentration and volume changes shift the position of equilibrium, but K stays the same at a fixed temperature. A catalyst doesn't shift equilibrium at all. For an exothermic forward reaction, heating favours the reverse direction and K decreases; for an endothermic forward reaction, heating increases K.

**"It partly undoes what you did, never fully."** *Trap*
Add more A and the system uses some of it up, but [A] at the new equilibrium is still higher than before you added it. Students who say "returns to the original concentration" lose the mark.

**"Squeeze it and it makes fewer gas moles."** *Mnemonic*
At constant temperature, decreasing volume (increasing pressure) shifts towards the side with fewer moles of gas. Count only gases; solids and liquids don't count. Equal gas moles on both sides means no shift.

**"Inert gas at constant volume does nothing."** *Trap*
Adding argon at constant volume raises total pressure but doesn't change any reacting gas's concentration (same moles, same volume), so there's no shift. Rule of thumb: if no reacting gas was added or removed and the volume didn't change, no reacting concentration changed. If an inert gas is added at constant pressure instead, the volume grows and the system shifts towards more gas moles.

**"A catalyst changes the speed, not the destination."** *Insight*
Like a faster route to the same destination. A catalyst lowers the activation energy of both directions equally, so it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions by the same factor. Equilibrium is reached sooner, but the equilibrium amounts and K don't change. In a limited time, a catalyst can give more product; at equilibrium, it gives the same amount.

**Reading concentration-time graphs.** *Shortcut* (frequent HSC question)
These patterns point to the likely disturbance; use the question's information to confirm it.
- **One line jumps vertically, the others don't:** a concentration change (that substance was added or removed).
- **All reacting gas concentrations jump together at fixed temperature:** a volume change (all up means compressed).
- **No jump, then the lines curve to a new composition:** a temperature change.
- **Nothing changes in a mixture already at equilibrium:** consistent with adding a catalyst.

**Reading rate-time graphs.** *Shortcut*
- Concentration change: only one rate jumps.
- Temperature rise: both rates jump, and the endothermic direction jumps more.
- Catalyst: both rates jump by the same amount and stay equal.
- Volume decrease: both rates jump, and the direction with more gas moles reacting jumps more.

### Industrial equilibrium: the Haber process

**"Haber is a compromise."** *Insight*
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ is exothermic with fewer gas moles on the right.
- Yield wants low temperature, but low temperature is too slow.
- So use a moderate temperature (roughly 400 to 500 °C) plus an iron catalyst to make up the speed.
- High pressure (typically about 15 to 35 MPa) helps both yield and rate, but is limited by equipment cost and safety.
- Yield per pass is only about 15%, so unreacted gases are recycled.

Every "justify the conditions" question is a rate vs yield vs cost trade-off.

### Colour-change equilibria (practicals)

**"NO₂: hot is brown."** *Mnemonic*
2NO₂ (brown) ⇌ N₂O₄ (colourless), forward exothermic. Heating shifts left, so the mixture goes browner. An ice bath makes it paler.

**"Cobalt: hot is blue."** *Mnemonic*
[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (pink) + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ (blue) + 6H₂O, forward endothermic. Heating shifts right, so it turns blue; cooling turns it pink. This is the opposite of the everyday "blue = cold" instinct, which is exactly why it gets examined.

**Colourimetry: measure the colour it absorbs, not the colour you see.** *Insight*
FeSCN²⁺ looks red because it absorbs blue-green light, so set the colourimeter to blue-green, the opposite colour on the colour wheel. Within the range of the standards, absorbance is proportional to concentration, so a calibration curve converts absorbance into [FeSCN²⁺].

### Equilibrium expressions and K

**"Products over reactants, coefficients become powers, pure solids and liquids drop out."** *Mnemonic*
For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, K = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Solids, pure liquids and water as the solvent don't appear, because their "concentration" doesn't change.

**"Flip the equation, flip K. Multiply the equation, power K."** *Shortcut*
Reverse reaction: K becomes 1/K. Double all coefficients: K becomes K². Halve them: K becomes √K.

**"Big K, products favoured."** *Insight*
Large K (much greater than 1): the equilibrium lies to the right and products are favoured. Small K (much less than 1): it lies to the left. K says nothing about how fast equilibrium is reached.

### ICE tables

**"Only the C row follows the coefficients."** *Insight / Trap* (prevents many calculation errors)
The Initial and Equilibrium rows can be any numbers. Only the Change row must be in the mole ratio of the equation (−x, −3x, +2x for the Haber reaction). Students who put the I or E rows in ratio get the whole question wrong.

**Work in concentrations, not moles.** *Trap*
Divide moles by volume before substituting into K. If the volume is 2.0 L, every mole value must be halved.

### Reaction quotient Q

**"Q chases K."** *Insight*
Q is calculated exactly like K, but with the current concentrations. The reaction always moves so that Q moves towards K.
- Q < K: too few products, so the reaction goes forward (right).
- Q > K: too many products, so it goes in reverse (left).
- Q = K: at equilibrium.

### Dissolution and the cycad example (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge)

**"Running water keeps the toxin dissolving."** *Insight*
Cycad seeds contain water-soluble toxins such as cycasin. Crushed seeds soaked in running water keep losing dissolved toxin, because the flowing water keeps the toxin concentration around them low. Dissolving never reaches equilibrium, so more toxin keeps leaving the seed. This connects solubility and equilibrium to a long-practised processing method.

### Solubility rules

**NAGSAG: usually soluble, then check exceptions.** *Mnemonic* (matches the catalogue categories)
**N**itrates, **A**mmonium, **G**roup 1, **S**ulfates, **A**cetates, **G**roup 17 (chloride, bromide, iodide).
Exceptions worth knowing:
- Halides: silver, lead(II) and mercury(I) halides are insoluble.
- Sulfates: barium, lead(II), calcium and strontium sulfates are insoluble or only slightly soluble. Silver sulfate is slightly soluble.

**Mostly insoluble:** carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides and sulfides, *unless* paired with Group 1 or ammonium. (Hydroxides of barium, and to a lesser extent strontium and calcium, are partly soluble.)

**Spectator ions: split only (aq), cancel what's identical.** *Shortcut*
To write a net ionic equation, split every aqueous ionic compound into ions, leave the precipitate whole, and cancel ions that appear unchanged on both sides.

### Ksp calculations

**The s², 4s³, 27s⁴, 108s⁵ pattern.** *Shortcut* (very high value)
If molar solubility is s, each ion's concentration is its subscript times s. Then raise each to its subscript:

| Formula type | Example | Ksp |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AB | AgCl | s × s = s² |
| AB₂ or A₂B | PbI₂, Ag₂CrO₄ | s × (2s)² = 4s³ |
| AB₃ or A₃B | Fe(OH)₃ | s × (3s)³ = 27s⁴ |
| A₃B₂ or A₂B₃ | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ | (3s)³ × (2s)² = 108s⁵ |

The rule behind it: "the subscript does double duty: it multiplies s, then it's the power."

**Only compare Ksp values directly for the same formula type.** *Trap*
AgCl (AB) vs AgBr (AB): the smaller Ksp is less soluble. But AgCl (AB) vs Ag₂CrO₄ (A₂B): you must calculate s for each first, because the formulas follow different patterns.

**Convert grams to moles before anything else.** *Trap*
Ksp uses mol L⁻¹. If solubility is given in g L⁻¹, divide by molar mass first. Convert back at the end if asked.

### Precipitation and Qsp

**"Over the limit, it falls out."** *Mnemonic*
Calculate Qsp from the ion concentrations after mixing. Qsp > Ksp: a precipitate forms. Qsp < Ksp: no precipitate. Qsp = Ksp: saturated.

**Mixing dilutes both solutions.** *Trap* (very common lost mark)
When 50 mL of one solution is mixed with 50 mL of another, the total volume is 100 mL, so both concentrations halve before you calculate Qsp.

### Common ion effect

**"Already have some of the ion, less room for more."** *Insight*
Adding an ion already in the solubility equilibrium shifts it left, so less solid dissolves. When the added concentration is much larger than s, ignore the tiny extra from dissolving: for AgCl in 0.10 mol L⁻¹ NaCl, Ksp ≈ s × 0.10.

## Biology Module 5

### Reproduction

**"Asexual copies; sexual combines."** *Insight*
Asexual reproduction copies one parent's alleles, so it's fast and needs no mate, but the offspring share the same weaknesses. Sexual reproduction combines alleles from two parents, so the offspring vary. Variation gives a population a better chance that some individuals survive a change, but it guarantees nothing for any one individual.

**The banana story.** *Insight / real example*
The Gros Michel banana, grown as clones, was wiped out commercially by Panama disease in the mid-1900s. Its replacement, the Cavendish, is also clonal and is now threatened by a new strain (TR4). One example makes the risk of low genetic diversity unforgettable, and it works in reproduction, agriculture and population genetics answers.

**"Shelled eggs mean internal fertilisation."** *Insight*
Sperm must reach the egg before the shell forms, so birds and reptiles fertilise internally even though the egg develops outside the body. Fertilisation type is about where the gametes meet, not where the egg ends up.

**"External: water and numbers. Internal: a mate and protection."** *Mnemonic*
External fertilisation needs water and huge gamete numbers, because most gametes are lost. Internal fertilisation needs mating, but protects the gametes from drying out and wastes far fewer.

**"STAMEN has MEN in it."** *Mnemonic*
The stamen (anther plus filament) is the male part that makes pollen. The carpel (stigma, style, ovary) is female.

**Pollination is not fertilisation.** *Trap*
Pollination: pollen lands on the stigma. Fertilisation happens later, when the pollen tube has grown down the style and a sperm nucleus joins the egg inside the ovule.

**"Binary fission makes more bacteria. Conjugation makes different bacteria."** *Trap*
Conjugation transfers DNA (often a plasmid) between bacteria but doesn't make a new cell, so it isn't reproduction. It's a source of variation, and it's how antibiotic resistance genes can spread (Module 7).

### Mammalian reproduction and hormones

**The names tell you the job.** *Mnemonic / insight*
- **FSH:** follicle-stimulating hormone. It grows the follicle.
- **Oestrogen:** made by the growing follicle. It thickens the uterus lining.
- **LH:** luteinising hormone. A surge triggers ovulation.
- **Pro-gest-erone:** "pro-gestation". It maintains the uterus lining and the pregnancy.
- **hCG:** made by the outer cells of the early embryo (which become part of the placenta). It keeps the corpus luteum producing progesterone early in pregnancy, and it's what a pregnancy test detects.
- **Oxytocin:** Greek for "swift birth". It drives uterine contractions.
- **Prolactin:** "pro-lactation". It drives milk production.

**Birth is positive feedback.** *Insight*
Contractions push the baby against the cervix, which triggers more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions. The loop only ends when the baby is born. It's one of very few positive feedback examples in the course, so it's a favourite question.

### Cell replication

**IPMAT for mitosis.** *Mnemonic*
**I**nterphase (DNA copied), **P**rophase (chromosomes **P**repare and condense), **M**etaphase (line up in the **M**iddle), **A**naphase (pulled **A**part), **T**elophase (**T**wo nuclei form). Interphase isn't strictly part of mitosis, but it's where DNA replication happens. Cytokinesis (splitting the cytoplasm) follows telophase as a separate process.

**"Meiosis I splits the pairs. Meiosis II splits the copies."** *Insight* (clears up most meiosis confusion)
Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes, which halves the chromosome number (diploid to haploid). Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, just like mitosis but in a haploid cell.

**Sister chromatids vs homologous chromosomes.** *Insight*
Sister chromatids begin as identical copies of one chromosome, joined at a centromere (crossing over can later make them differ). Homologous chromosomes are two editions of the same book, one from each parent: the same genes, possibly different alleles.

**"Count the centromeres, not the arms."** *Shortcut*
To count chromosomes in a diagram, count centromeres. An X-shaped duplicated chromosome is still one chromosome.

**"Mitosis keeps the number. Meiosis halves it."** *Insight*
Mitosis gives two nuclei with the same chromosome number as the parent cell, for growth and repair. Meiosis halves the number (diploid to haploid) to make gametes. In human males, meiosis gives four sperm. In females, it gives one egg and small polar bodies that break down.

### DNA structure and replication

**"Apple in the Tree, Car in the Garage."** *Mnemonic*
A pairs with T, C pairs with G. In RNA, uracil replaces thymine, so A pairs with U.

**Enzyme names tell you the job.** *Mnemonic*
- **Helicase:** unwinds the **helix** and unzips the strands.
- **DNA polymerase:** builds the new **polymer** strand.
- **Ligase:** **ligates**, gluing DNA fragments together (like a ligament ties things).

**"Half old, half new."** *Insight*
Replication is semi-conservative: each new DNA molecule keeps one original strand and gets one newly built strand. Think of a zip where each side finds a new partner.

**"Polymerase only builds 5′ to 3′."** *Insight* (Extension)
DNA polymerase only adds nucleotides in the 5′ to 3′ direction. On one strand (leading) that direction follows the opening fork, so it's built continuously. On the other (lagging) it points away from the fork, so it's built in short pieces (Okazaki fragments) that ligase joins.

### DNA in prokaryotes and eukaryotes

**"Bacterial DNA has no nucleus around it."** *Insight*
Bacterial DNA is usually one circular chromosome lying in the nucleoid (no nuclear membrane), often with small extra circles called plasmids. Eukaryotic DNA is linear, wound around histone proteins, and enclosed in a nucleus. (Archaea, the other prokaryote group, can have histones.)

**Mitochondrial DNA looks bacterial for a reason.** *Insight*
Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own small, circular DNA because they descended from bacteria (endosymbiosis). This explains a fact that otherwise seems random.

### Polypeptide synthesis

**"Transcription keeps the language, translation changes it."** *Insight*
Transcription copies DNA into mRNA: nucleic acid to nucleic acid, like transcribing notes in the same language. Translation converts the nucleotide code into amino acids: a different language. Order: trans**C**ription comes before trans**L**ation, just as C comes before L.

**"mRNA is a copy of the coding strand, built on the template strand."** *Trap*
mRNA is complementary to the template strand. It therefore has the same sequence as the coding strand, with U in place of T. Exam questions deliberately give one strand and ask for the mRNA.

**Stop codons: "U Are Away, U Are Gone, U Go Away."** *Mnemonic*
UAA, UAG, UGA. The start codon is AUG (methionine).

**"THE FAT CAT ATE THE RAT."** *Insight*
The code is read in triplets with no gaps. Delete one letter (THE ATC ATA TET HER AT) and every word after it becomes nonsense. That's a frameshift. A substitution changes at most one codon. It may change nothing (several codons code for the same amino acid) or one amino acid, unless it creates a stop codon, which cuts the protein short. This is why an insertion or deletion is usually more damaging than a substitution.

**Protein structure: 1 order, 2 local coils, 3 whole fold, 4 more than one chain.** *Mnemonic*
Primary: the amino acid sequence. Secondary: local coils and sheets (alpha helix, beta sheet). Tertiary: the whole 3D fold of one chain. Quaternary: several chains together (haemoglobin has four). The insight: change the primary sequence and the fold, and therefore the function, can change. Sickle cell is one substituted amino acid.

### Genetic variation and inheritance

**Book analogy for chromosome, gene and allele.** *Insight*
A chromosome is a book. A gene is one recipe in it. An allele is a version of that recipe (chocolate cake with or without nuts).

**Three sources of variation: Cross, Independently, Randomly.** *Mnemonic*
**C**rossing over (prophase I), **I**ndependent assortment (metaphase I), **R**andom fertilisation. Independent assortment alone gives 2²³, about 8.4 million, chromosome combinations per human gamete, before crossing over adds more. Mutation is the only source of genuinely new alleles.

**"Dominant doesn't mean common."** *Trap*
Dominant only means it's expressed in a heterozygote. Huntington's disease is dominant and rare. Students often assume the dominant allele must be the most frequent.

**Test cross: one recessive offspring gives it away.** *Shortcut*
To find whether a dominant-looking individual is AA or Aa, cross it with aa. If even one offspring shows the recessive trait, the parent must be Aa. If every offspring shows the dominant trait, AA is likely but not proven; more offspring make the conclusion stronger.

**FOIL for dihybrid gametes.** *Shortcut* (Extension)
To find the gametes from AaBb, use the algebra FOIL rule: **F**irst (AB), **O**uter (Ab), **I**nner (aB), **L**ast (ab). These label the Punnett square rows and columns. AaBb × AaBb gives a 9:3:3:1 ratio, but only for genes on different chromosomes (unlinked).

**"Incomplete is in-between. Co shows both."** *Mnemonic*
Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote is a blend (red × white gives pink snapdragons). Codominance: both alleles show fully (roan cattle with red and white hairs; AB blood type). In both, a cross between two heterozygotes gives a phenotype ratio equal to the genotype ratio (1:2:1), because the heterozygote looks different from both homozygotes.

**Blood groups: O is only ii.** *Shortcut*
Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant, and both are dominant over i. So two type A parents can have a type O child if both are Iᴬi. A type O child tells you each parent carries i.

**"Dad gives his X to daughters and his Y to sons."** *Insight* (unlocks all sex-linkage)
So a father can never pass an X-linked trait to his son. For X-linked recessive traits:
- They're much more common in males (one X, no backup copy).
- An affected father passes his affected X to every daughter, so each daughter is at least a carrier.
- An affected mother's sons are all affected.

**Pedigree rules.** *Shortcut* (most reliable pedigree tool)
These rules assume one gene, full expression and no new mutations.
- **Two unaffected parents with an affected child:** recessive. It was hidden in the parents.
- **Two affected parents with an unaffected child:** dominant. It was hidden in the child.
- **An affected daughter with an unaffected father, or an affected mother with an unaffected son:** rules out X-linked recessive.
- **Mostly males affected, never passed father to son:** suspect X-linked recessive.
- **An affected father whose daughters are all affected and whose sons are all unaffected:** suspect X-linked dominant.

### Population genetics

**Count alleles, not people.** *Trap*
For an autosomal gene, each person has two alleles, so a population of 100 has 200 alleles. Allele frequency = copies of that allele ÷ total alleles. (For X-linked genes, males carry only one copy.)

**SNPs: one-letter differences.** *Insight*
A single nucleotide polymorphism is a one-letter difference at a known position in the DNA. Most lie in non-coding DNA. Comparing many SNPs shows how closely related individuals or populations are, and some SNPs are linked to disease risk.

**Hardy–Weinberg: start from q².** *Shortcut* (Extension; used in the site's population genetics lesson)
p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. The only number you can usually observe directly is q², the frequency of the recessive phenotype. Take its square root to get q, then p = 1 − q, then carriers = 2pq. This only holds for a large, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation or migration.

**"Rare disease, common carriers."** *Insight* (Extension)
If 1 in 2,500 babies has a recessive condition, q² = 0.0004, so q = 0.02 and carriers 2pq ≈ 0.04, about 1 in 25 people. (These are example figures, similar to cystic fibrosis in some populations.) Most copies of a recessive allele are hidden in healthy carriers, which is why recessive conditions persist.

### DNA sequencing and profiling

**"DNA runs to red."** *Mnemonic*
DNA is negatively charged (phosphate backbone), so in gel electrophoresis it moves towards the positive electrode, the red lead. Smaller fragments move further.

**"Every child band must come from Mum or Dad."** *Shortcut* (paternity and profiling questions)
At each STR marker, a child has one band from the mother and one from the father. (If both copies are the same length, you see one band.) A child's band that neither parent has means that man is excluded as the father, assuming no mutation or error. Real cases compare many markers.

**PCR: hot, cool, warm.** *Mnemonic*
Denature at about 95 °C (separate the strands), anneal at about 55 °C (primers attach; the exact temperature depends on the primers), extend at about 72 °C (polymerase copies). Each ideal cycle doubles the DNA, so after n cycles you have up to 2ⁿ times as many copies.
