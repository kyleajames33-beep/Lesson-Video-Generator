# Module 5 memory aids: Chemistry and Biology

Status: draft aid bank, not narration approval. Select an entry through the [student learning review](student-learning-review.md), check its conditions and science for the exact lesson, and obtain the subject-teacher check before recording. Some broad shortcuts below need correction or scoping; they must not override the lesson's careful causal explanation or prerequisite boundaries.

Prepared 11 October 2026. A curated set of mnemonics, shortcuts, insights and exam-trap fixes for Year 12 Chemistry Module 5 (Equilibrium and Acid Reactions) and Biology Module 5 (Heredity). Only aids that genuinely save a student time or prevent a common lost mark are included.

## How to use these in videos

- Teach the idea first, then give the aid as a hook to keep it. A mnemonic never replaces the explanation (see AGENTS.md: explain each cause before relying on it).
- Say why the aid works whenever that is short. "Write heat into the equation" is memorable because the student can see *why* heating shifts an exothermic reaction left.
- One or two aids per video is usually right. Put the strongest one in the "Key notes: what to write in your book" hold.
- Each entry is tagged: **Mnemonic** (a phrase to recall a list or rule), **Shortcut** (a faster method), **Insight** (a way of seeing it that makes the rule obvious) or **Trap** (a specific mistake it prevents).

## Chemistry Module 5

### Static and dynamic equilibrium

**"Equal rates, not equal amounts."** *Insight / Trap*
At dynamic equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal. Concentrations stay constant but need not equal each other. Picture two cities with equal traffic flowing each way: the populations stay steady, but one city can still be much bigger. The analogy explains balance; chemical reactions also change substances.

**"Keep the material available for both directions."** *Insight*
In the sealed-drink model, carbon dioxide stays available to leave and re-enter the liquid. Opening lets gas escape into the room, so less is available above the drink to return. Some still returns, but the dissolved amount falls. Being sealed alone does not establish equilibrium.

### Non-equilibrium systems, enthalpy and entropy

**Gas moles are the biggest entropy clue.** *Shortcut*
More moles of gas on one side means that side has higher entropy. After that: solid < liquid < gas, and dissolving usually increases entropy.

**"Enthalpy wins cold, entropy wins hot."** *Insight*
In ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, the entropy term is multiplied by T. So when ΔH and ΔS have the same sign, temperature decides:

| ΔH | ΔS | Spontaneous? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| − | + | Always |
| + | − | Never |
| − | − | At low temperature (enthalpy wins cold) |
| + | + | At high temperature (entropy wins hot) |

Students only need to remember the bottom two rows. The top two are common sense.

### Le Chatelier's principle

**"Write heat into the equation."** *Shortcut / Insight* (highest value in the module)
For an exothermic reaction, write heat on the product side: A + B ⇌ C + heat. For an endothermic reaction, put it on the reactant side. This bookkeeping aid helps predict direction: heating favours the heat-absorbing direction. Heat is not a chemical concentration and does not enter the K expression.

**"Change T, change K. Change anything else, K stays."** *Mnemonic / Trap*
For a specified reaction in the school equilibrium model, changing temperature changes K. Concentration or volume changes can shift composition while K stays the same at fixed temperature. A catalyst does not shift equilibrium composition. For an exothermic forward reaction, heating favours the reverse direction and K decreases. Use this after the K lesson.

**"It partly undoes what you did, never fully."** *Trap*
Add more A and the system uses some of it up, but [A] at the new equilibrium is still higher than before you added it. Students who say "returns to the original concentration" lose the mark.

**"Squeeze it and it makes fewer gas moles."** *Mnemonic*
Decreasing volume (increasing pressure) shifts towards the side with fewer moles of gas. Count only gases; solids and liquids don't count. Equal gas moles on both sides means no shift.

**"Inert gas at constant volume does nothing."** *Trap*
Adding argon raises total pressure but doesn't change any reacting gas's concentration (same moles, same volume). No shift. The rule of thumb: if the volume didn't change, no reacting concentration changed.

**"A catalyst changes the speed, not the destination."** *Insight*
Like a faster route to the same destination, a catalyst provides a faster pathway for both directions. At fixed temperature it does not change equilibrium composition or K. It can increase product obtained in a limited time, while the equilibrium amount stays the same. Do not claim equal enhancement factors or equal rates away from equilibrium.

**Reading concentration-time graphs.** *Insight* (use supplied disturbance evidence)
- These patterns suggest explanations; the graph alone may not uniquely identify a disturbance.
- **One line jumps vertically, the others don't:** a concentration change (that substance was added or removed).
- **All reacting gas concentrations jump together at fixed temperature:** consistent with a volume change (all up means compressed).
- **No immediate concentration jump at fixed volume, followed by a different composition:** consistent with a temperature change.
- **A catalyst added to an established equilibrium:** concentrations stay steady. An unchanged graph alone does not prove a catalyst was added.

### Industrial equilibrium: the Haber process

**"Haber is a compromise."** *Insight*
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ is exothermic with fewer gas moles on the right.
- Yield wants low temperature, but low temperature is too slow.
- So use a moderate temperature (roughly 400 to 500 °C) plus an iron catalyst to make up the speed.
- High pressure helps both yield and rate, but is limited by equipment cost and safety.
- Unreacted gases are recycled, so a low yield per pass doesn't waste material.

Every "justify the conditions" question is a rate vs yield vs cost trade-off.

### Colour-change equilibria (practicals)

**"NO₂: hot is brown."** *Mnemonic*
2NO₂ (brown) ⇌ N₂O₄ (colourless), forward exothermic. Heating shifts left, so the mixture goes browner. An ice bath makes it paler.

**"Cobalt: hot is blue."** *Mnemonic*
[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (pink) + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ (blue) + 6H₂O, forward endothermic. Heating shifts right, so it turns blue; cooling turns it pink. The tip is that this is the opposite of the everyday "blue = cold" instinct, which is exactly why it gets examined.

**Colourimetry: measure the colour it absorbs, not the colour you see.** *Insight*
FeSCN²⁺ looks red because it absorbs blue-green light, so set the colourimeter to blue-green, the opposite colour on the colour wheel. Absorbance is proportional to concentration, so a calibration curve from known standards converts absorbance into [FeSCN²⁺].

### Equilibrium expressions and K

**"Products over reactants, coefficients become powers, pure solids and liquids drop out."** *Mnemonic*
For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, K = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Solids, pure liquids and water as the solvent don't appear, because their "concentration" doesn't change.

**"Flip the equation, flip K. Multiply the equation, power K."** *Shortcut*
Reverse reaction: K becomes 1/K. Double all coefficients: K becomes K². Halve them: K becomes √K.

**"Big K favours products in this expression."** *Insight*
K is the product-to-reactant ratio defined by the specified equilibrium expression, with its coefficient powers. Large K favours products in that relationship, but does not universally mean a majority of molecules are products. The size of K says nothing about how fast equilibrium is reached.

### ICE tables

**"Only the C row follows the coefficients."** *Insight / Trap* (prevents many calculation errors)
The Initial and Equilibrium rows can be any numbers. Only the Change row must be in the mole ratio of the equation (−x, −3x, +2x for the Haber reaction). Students who put the I or E rows in ratio get the whole question wrong.

**Work in concentrations, not moles.** *Trap*
Divide moles by volume before substituting into K. If the volume is 2.0 L, every mole value must be halved.

### Reaction quotient Q

**"Q chases K."** *Insight*
Q is calculated exactly like K, but with the current concentrations. The reaction always moves so that Q moves towards K.
- Q < K: too few products, so the reaction goes forward (right).
- Q > K: too many products, so it goes in reverse (left).
- Q = K: at equilibrium.

### Dissolution and the cycad example (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge)

**"Flowing water carries dissolved material away."** *Insight*
In the supplied leaching model, flowing water removes dissolved material and maintains a concentration difference supporting further transfer. This explains one part of a named traditional processing method. It does not establish complete toxin removal or safe seeds; use verified culturally specific evidence before selecting this example.

### Solubility rules

**NAGSAG: usually soluble, then check exceptions.** *Mnemonic* (matches the catalogue categories)
**N**itrates, **A**mmonium, **G**roup 1, **S**ulfates, **A**cetates, **G**roup 17 (chloride, bromide, iodide).
Exceptions worth knowing:
- Halides: silver, lead(II) and mercury(I) halides are insoluble.
- Sulfates: barium, lead(II), calcium and strontium sulfates are insoluble or only slightly soluble. Silver sulfate is slightly soluble.

**Mostly insoluble:** carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides and sulfides, *unless* paired with Group 1 or ammonium. (Hydroxides of barium, and to a lesser extent strontium and calcium, are partly soluble.)

**Spectator ions: split only (aq), cancel what's identical.** *Shortcut*
To write a net ionic equation, split every aqueous ionic compound into ions, leave the precipitate whole, and cancel ions that appear unchanged on both sides.

### Ksp calculations

**The s², 4s³, 27s⁴, 108s⁵ pattern.** *Shortcut* (very high value)
If molar solubility is s, each ion's concentration is its subscript times s. Then raise each to its subscript:

| Formula type | Example | Ksp |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AB | AgCl | s × s = s² |
| AB₂ or A₂B | PbI₂, Ag₂CrO₄ | s × (2s)² = 4s³ |
| AB₃ or A₃B | Fe(OH)₃ | s × (3s)³ = 27s⁴ |
| A₃B₂ or A₂B₃ | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ | (3s)³ × (2s)² = 108s⁵ |

The rule behind it: "the subscript does double duty: it multiplies s, then it's the power."

**Only compare Ksp values directly for the same formula type.** *Trap*
AgCl (AB) vs AgBr (AB): the smaller Ksp is less soluble. But AgCl (AB) vs Ag₂CrO₄ (A₂B): you must calculate s for each first, because the formulas follow different patterns.

**Convert grams to moles before anything else.** *Trap*
Ksp uses mol L⁻¹. If solubility is given in g L⁻¹, divide by molar mass first. Convert back at the end if asked.

### Precipitation and Qsp

**"Over the limit, it falls out."** *Mnemonic*
Calculate Qsp from the ion concentrations after mixing. Qsp > Ksp: a precipitate forms. Qsp < Ksp: no precipitate. Qsp = Ksp: saturated.

**Mixing dilutes both solutions.** *Trap* (very common lost mark)
When 50 mL of one solution is mixed with 50 mL of another, the total volume is 100 mL, so both concentrations halve before you calculate Qsp.

### Common ion effect

**"Already have some of the ion, less room for more."** *Insight*
Adding an ion already in the solubility equilibrium shifts it left, so less solid dissolves. First check that its added concentration is much larger than the amount contributed by dissolution. Only then use an approximation such as Ksp ≈ s × 0.10 for AgCl in 0.10 M NaCl.

## Biology Module 5

### Reproduction

**"Copying keeps a combination. Joining reshuffles combinations."** *Insight*
Asexual reproduction can produce offspring without finding a mate. Sexual reproduction combines inherited information through reproductive cells. Which route helps depends on the organism and conditions; variation does not promise resistance or survival. Explain the specific grower's trade-off before using the anchor.

**The banana story.** *Insight / real example*
The Gros Michel banana, grown as clones, was wiped out commercially by Panama disease in the mid-1900s. Its replacement, the Cavendish, is also clonal and is now threatened by a new strain (TR4). One example makes the risk of low genetic diversity unforgettable, and it works in reproduction, agriculture and population genetics answers.

**"Shelled eggs mean internal fertilisation."** *Insight*
Sperm must reach the egg before the shell forms, so birds and reptiles fertilise internally even though the egg develops outside the body. Fertilisation type is about where the gametes meet, not where the egg ends up.

**"External: water and numbers. Internal: a mate and protection."** *Mnemonic*
External fertilisation needs water and huge gamete numbers, because most gametes are lost. Internal fertilisation needs mating, but protects the gametes from drying out and wastes far fewer.

**"STAMEN has MEN in it."** *Mnemonic*
The stamen (anther plus filament) is the male part that makes pollen. The carpel (stigma, style, ovary) is female.

**Pollination is not fertilisation.** *Trap*
Pollination: pollen lands on the stigma. Fertilisation happens later, when the pollen tube has grown down the style and a sperm nucleus joins the egg inside the ovule.

**"Binary fission makes more bacteria. Conjugation makes different bacteria."** *Trap*
Conjugation transfers DNA (often a plasmid) between bacteria but doesn't make a new cell, so it isn't reproduction. It's a source of variation.

### Mammalian reproduction and hormones

**The names tell you the job.** *Mnemonic / insight*
- **Pro-gest-erone:** "pro-gestation". It maintains the uterus lining and the pregnancy.
- **Oxytocin:** Greek for "swift birth". It drives uterine contractions.
- **Prolactin:** "pro-lactation". It drives milk production.
- **FSH:** follicle-stimulating hormone. It grows the follicle.
- **hCG:** made by the embryo. It keeps the corpus luteum producing progesterone early in pregnancy, and it's what a pregnancy test detects.

**Birth is positive feedback.** *Insight*
Contractions push the baby against the cervix, which triggers more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions. The loop only ends when the baby is born. It's one of very few positive feedback examples in the course, so it's a favourite question.

### Cell replication

**IPMAT for mitosis.** *Mnemonic*
**I**nterphase (DNA copied), **P**rophase (chromosomes **P**repare and condense), **M**etaphase (line up in the **M**iddle), **A**naphase (pulled **A**part), **T**elophase (**T**wo nuclei form). Interphase isn't strictly part of mitosis, but it's where DNA replication happens.

**"Meiosis I splits the pairs. Meiosis II splits the copies."** *Insight* (clears up most meiosis confusion)
Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes, which halves the chromosome number (diploid to haploid). Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, just like mitosis.

**Sister chromatids vs homologous chromosomes.** *Insight*
Sister chromatids begin as copies of one chromosome, joined at a centromere; crossing over can make their sequences differ. Homologous chromosomes are two editions of the same book, one from each parent: corresponding genes, possibly different alleles.

**"Count the centromeres, not the arms."** *Shortcut*
To count chromosomes in a diagram, count centromeres. An X-shaped duplicated chromosome is still one chromosome.

**"Mitosis retains the set. Meiosis reduces the set."** *Insight*
Mitosis retains the starting chromosome set in the daughter nuclei. Meiosis reduces a diploid set to haploid products. Four sperm cells fit the selected animal spermatogenesis example; egg formation has unequal products, and plant meiosis produces spores. Use the organism's actual pathway rather than generalising one example.

### DNA structure and replication

**"Apple in the Tree, Car in the Garage."** *Mnemonic*
A pairs with T, C pairs with G. In RNA, uracil replaces thymine, so A pairs with U.

**Enzyme names tell you the job.** *Mnemonic*
- **Helicase:** unwinds the **helix** and unzips the strands.
- **DNA polymerase:** builds the new **polymer** strand.
- **Ligase:** **ligates**, gluing DNA fragments together (like a ligament ties things).

**"Half old, half new."** *Insight*
Replication is semi-conservative: each new DNA molecule keeps one original strand and gets one newly built strand. Think of a zip where each side finds a new partner.

**"Polymerase only builds 5′ to 3′."** *Insight*
Because DNA polymerase can only add to one end, one strand (leading) is built continuously. The other (lagging) is built backwards in short pieces that ligase joins. This one fact explains why the two strands are copied differently.

### DNA in prokaryotes and eukaryotes

**"Bacterial DNA has no enclosing nucleus."** *Insight*
In the typical bacterial model, the chromosome is often circular and lies in a nucleoid region, with DNA-associated proteins and sometimes plasmids. Do not generalise "no histones" to all prokaryotes: archaea can have histones. In the selected eukaryotic model, linear nuclear chromosomes associate with histones.

**Mitochondrial DNA looks bacterial for a reason.** *Insight*
Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own small, circular DNA because they descended from bacteria (endosymbiosis). This explains a fact that otherwise seems random.

### Polypeptide synthesis

**"Transcription keeps the language, translation changes it."** *Insight*
Transcription copies DNA into mRNA: nucleic acid to nucleic acid, like transcribing notes in the same language. Translation converts the nucleotide code into amino acids: a different language. Order: trans**C**ription comes before trans**L**ation, just as C comes before L.

**"mRNA is a copy of the coding strand, built on the template strand."** *Trap*
mRNA is complementary to the template strand. It therefore has the same sequence as the coding strand, with U in place of T. Exam questions deliberately give one strand and ask for the mRNA.

**Stop codons: "U Are Away, U Are Gone, U Go Away."** *Mnemonic*
UAA, UAG, UGA. The start codon is AUG (methionine).

**"THE FAT CAT ATE THE RAT."** *Insight*
The code is read in triplets with no gaps. Delete one letter (THE ATC ATA TET HER AT) and every word after it becomes nonsense. That's a frameshift. A substitution changes only one word, and may change nothing at all, because several codons code for the same amino acid. This explains why an insertion or deletion is usually far more damaging than a substitution.

**Protein structure: 1 order, 2 local coils, 3 whole fold, 4 more than one chain.** *Mnemonic*
Primary: the amino acid sequence. Secondary: local coils and sheets (alpha helix, beta sheet). Tertiary: the whole 3D fold of one chain. Quaternary: several chains together (haemoglobin has four). The insight: change the primary sequence and the fold, and therefore the function, can change. Sickle cell is one substituted amino acid.

### Genetic variation and inheritance

**Book analogy for chromosome, gene and allele.** *Insight*
A chromosome is a book. A gene is one recipe in it. An allele is a version of that recipe (chocolate cake with or without nuts).

**Three sources of variation: Cross, Independently, Randomly.** *Mnemonic*
**C**rossing over (prophase I), **I**ndependent assortment (metaphase I), **R**andom fertilisation. Independent assortment alone gives 2²³, about 8.4 million, chromosome combinations per human gamete, before crossing over adds more. Mutation is the only source of genuinely new alleles.

**"Dominant doesn't mean common."** *Trap*
Dominant only means it's expressed in a heterozygote. Huntington's disease is dominant and rare. Students often assume the dominant allele must be the most frequent.

**Test cross: one recessive offspring gives it away.** *Shortcut*
To find whether a dominant-looking individual is AA or Aa, cross it with aa. If even one offspring shows the recessive trait, the parent must be Aa.

**FOIL for dihybrid gametes.** *Shortcut*
To find the gametes from AaBb, use the algebra FOIL rule: **F**irst (AB), **O**uter (Ab), **I**nner (aB), **L**ast (ab). These label the Punnett square rows and columns. AaBb × AaBb gives a 9:3:3:1 ratio.

**"Incomplete is in-between. Co shows both."** *Mnemonic*
Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote is a blend (red × white gives pink snapdragons). Codominance: both alleles show fully (roan cattle with red and white hairs; AB blood type). In both, the phenotype ratio equals the genotype ratio (1:2:1), because the heterozygote looks different.

**Blood groups: O is only ii.** *Shortcut*
Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant, and both are dominant over i. So two type A parents can have a type O child if both are Iᴬi. A type O child tells you each parent carries i.

**"Dad gives his X to daughters and his Y to sons."** *Insight* (standard XX/XY inheritance model)
So a father can never pass an X-linked trait to his son. For X-linked recessive traits:
- They're much more common in males (one X, no backup copy).
- An affected father passes the affected X to every daughter; her mother's X determines whether she is a carrier or affected in the recessive model.
- An affected mother's sons are all affected.

**Pedigree rules.** *Shortcut* (specified single-gene Mendelian model)
Apply the supplied assumptions: complete expression of the phenotype, accurate parentage and no new mutation. Use these as deductions or candidate tests in that model, not universal clinical rules.
- **Two unaffected parents with an affected child:** recessive. It was hidden in the parents.
- **Two affected parents with an unaffected child:** dominant. It was hidden in the child.
- **Mostly males affected, never passed father to son:** suspect X-linked recessive.
- **An affected father with all daughters affected:** suspect X-linked dominant.

### Population genetics

**Count alleles, not people.** *Trap*
Each person has two alleles for a gene, so a population of 100 has 200 alleles. Allele frequency = copies of that allele ÷ total alleles.

**Hardy–Weinberg: start from q².** *Shortcut*
p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. The only number you can usually observe directly is q², the frequency of the recessive phenotype. Take its square root to get q, then p = 1 − q, then carriers = 2pq.

**"Rare disease, common carriers."** *Insight*
For a classroom Hardy–Weinberg example using a supplied recessive phenotype frequency of 1 in 2,500, q² = 0.0004, q = 0.02 and 2pq = 0.0392, about 1 in 25.5. This shows why a recessive phenotype can be uncommon while carriers are more common. These supplied figures are not a universal cystic-fibrosis prevalence claim across populations.

### DNA sequencing and profiling

**"DNA runs to red."** *Mnemonic*
DNA is negatively charged (phosphate backbone), so in gel electrophoresis it moves towards the positive electrode, the red lead. Smaller fragments move further.

**"Match the child's inherited markers to the parents."** *Shortcut* (idealised exam profiling model)
At each autosomal STR locus, one allele comes from each parent. Identical alleles may show one observed band rather than two. In a question explicitly excluding mutation and measurement errors, an unexplained child marker conflicts with the proposed parentage. Real profiling uses multiple loci and statistical interpretation, not a single-band shortcut.

**PCR: hot, cool, warm.** *Mnemonic*
Denature at about 95 °C (separate the strands), anneal at a primer-dependent temperature, often near 55 °C, and extend at about 72 °C (polymerase copies). In the ideal doubling model, target copies multiply by 2ⁿ after n cycles. Actual efficiency need not be perfect.
