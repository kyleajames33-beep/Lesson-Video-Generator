## hook

A sealed sparkling drink can look unchanged for hours. Does that mean the carbon dioxide has stopped moving between the liquid and the gas above it? The surprising answer is that an unchanged mixture can hide an ongoing exchange. We need to separate what stays steady from what keeps happening.

## concept-two-states

Think first about a book resting flat on a table. The table supports its weight, and the book stays at rest. That is a static mechanical equilibrium: the whole object is stationary and its forces balance. It does not mean every particle inside the book has stopped moving. Chemical equilibrium has a different kind of balance. In a reversible reaction, reactants form products and products form reactants. At dynamic equilibrium, those two reaction rates are equal and non-zero. Neither direction has stopped. Because each direction undoes the other at the same rate, there is no net change in the mixture. The two groups in this schematic represent substances in one mixture, not separate containers. Their balanced exchanges show the average behaviour, not exact molecular paths.

## definition

Here are the words that make that distinction precise. Dynamic means the opposing processes continue. Equilibrium means they balance, so there is no net change under the stated conditions. A closed system keeps matter inside, although energy can cross the boundary. Heat can still pass through a sealed bottle. An open system allows matter to enter or leave. So closed does not mean insulated, and open does not automatically mean every measured quantity must change.

## concept-conditions

For this lesson, our model is a reversible system in a closed container, at constant temperature and volume, given enough time to settle. The lid keeps material available for the reverse process, but a lid alone does not establish equilibrium. In the sparkling drink, carbon dioxide can leave the liquid and dissolve back into it. Once equilibrium is established, these transfers balance. Opening the bottle lets carbon dioxide escape into the room. Under those conditions, the drink loses dissolved gas overall. Some gas can still dissolve back in. The problem is the net loss, not the complete disappearance of the reverse process.

## worked-example

Suppose A can turn into B, and B can turn into A, one particle for one particle. This is a simplified model in a closed, fixed-volume container at constant temperature. The supplied measurements show four A particles becoming B per second, and four B particles becoming A per second, averaged over time. The forward process removes A, but the reverse process replaces A just as quickly. The same balance keeps B steady. So both concentrations remain constant. They do not need to match each other. In this model there is more A than B. Equal rates describe the traffic between the groups, not the number waiting in each group.

## worked-example-2

Now suppose we only know that a sealed mixture keeps the same colour for ten minutes. Is that enough to establish dynamic equilibrium? It is consistent with equilibrium, but it does not establish ongoing exchange. The reaction might simply be too slow to detect over that time. Being sealed and containing both substances is not enough either. In our A and B model, the extra evidence was conversion continuing in both directions, at matching non-zero rates. That is why we could justify the dynamic explanation. A steady observation tells us what we measured. The process evidence tells us why it stayed steady.

## misconception

The main trap is mixing up a rate with a concentration. Rate describes how fast conversion happens. Concentration describes how much of a substance is present per volume. Dynamic equilibrium requires the forward and reverse rates to match; it does not require equal concentrations. Nor does it require unequal concentrations. Either is possible in a suitable system. And a reaction that has apparently finished is not automatically an example of static equilibrium. It may have run out of a reactant or become too slow to observe. A still-looking result does not tell us which explanation is right.

### Feedback

Yes, this supplied evidence supports dynamic equilibrium. Both conversion rates are non-zero and equal, so each process replaces what the other removes. The concentrations can differ because equal traffic does not require equal amounts in the two groups. If we only had steady readings, we would still need evidence that the opposing conversions continue and balance. A change too slow for our measurements could look steady as well.

## summary

An unchanged mixture can still be reacting. At dynamic equilibrium, the forward and reverse reaction rates are equal and non-zero. That balance keeps composition stable under fixed conditions, without requiring equal concentrations. Our model keeps matter inside the system and allows enough time to settle. Closure helps make equilibrium possible; it does not prove it has been reached. And constant measurements alone do not establish ongoing exchange. Next, we will follow a reversible reaction from its starting mixture and see how those two rates come to match.
