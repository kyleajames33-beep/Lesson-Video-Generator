## Entry, start, stop and next handoff

**Entry knowledge:** cells carry inherited information in DNA; reproduction connects generations; a gamete is a reproductive cell; sexual reproduction involves gamete fusion; asexual reproduction does not. B1 is the support lesson, rather than a presumed public upload.

**Short entry check:** “What makes an event sexual reproduction: the number of parents, or fusion of gametes?” Expected reasoning: fusion of gametes. The one-sentence reminder in the opening supplies support without a second full B1 explanation. This is an entry diagnostic for a teacher or lesson page, not an additional timed quiz in this draft.

**Start:** a frog's gametes can meet in pond water, while a bird can lay an egg after fertilisation has occurred inside its body. The contrast directs attention to location of fusion, rather than the location of an egg when we see it.

**Stop:** the learner can explain hydra budding as a named asexual animal mechanism, trace haploid gametes to a diploid zygote in the stated sexual model, distinguish external/internal fertilisation from embryo development, and explain how encounter conditions affect a broadcast-spawning example without predicting guaranteed survival.

## Complete narration draft

Only paragraphs under “Speech” are proposed recording text. Headings, citations, notes and silent-hold instructions are production material. Segment IDs below are planning identifiers, not a production manifest. Speech timing and scene durations remain unmeasured.

### `title`

Speech: none. Brief course-neutral title, then the opening contrast. No new spoken metadata recital.

### `hook` (`b2-hook`)

**Speech**

A frog releases eggs into a pond as a male releases sperm over them. A bird lays a fertilised egg in a nest. Both eggs are outside the body when we see them, but did fertilisation happen in the same place? Sexual reproduction involves gametes fusing. To classify fertilisation, we need to find where that fusion happened, not just where an egg ended up.

### `concept-fertilisation` (`b2-fertilisation`)

**Speech**

First, what does fusion achieve? In the animal model here, a sperm and an egg each carry one set of chromosomes. We call that haploid. Fertilisation brings the two sets together in a zygote, the first cell of the new individual. The zygote is diploid, with two sets. In these animals, meiosis reduces the chromosome sets during gamete production, and fertilisation restores two sets. That helps maintain chromosome number across generations instead of doubling it each time. The diagram uses just a few chromosomes to represent sets. It is not the full chromosome count or every molecular step of fertilisation.

### `concept-asexual-animal` (`b2-hydra`)

**Speech**

Animals can also reproduce without gametes fusing. In a hydra, a small bud grows from the parent's body. Its cells divide, the bud develops, and it can detach as a separate hydra. That event is asexual reproduction. Budding can produce a new animal without finding a mate, when conditions support its growth. It usually retains a very similar inherited combination, although mutation can introduce differences. This is one animal example, not a rule that every animal can bud. A hydra can also reproduce sexually. We classify the event by its mechanism, rather than giving the animal one permanent label.

### `concept-external` (`b2-external`)

**Speech**

In our frog example, sperm and eggs meet in the pond, outside the female's body. That is external fertilisation. Salmon also release eggs and sperm into water. A moist environment protects exposed gametes from drying, but water alone does not bring every sperm to an egg. Release close together in place and time makes encounters more likely. In a broadcast-spawning example, producing many gametes can increase the opportunities for fertilisation, while dispersal can separate them before they meet. Fertilisation is only the first stage. Eggs and young may still be eaten or encounter unsuitable conditions. Gamete numbers, fertilised eggs and offspring that later reproduce are three different things.

### `concept-internal` (`b2-internal`)

**Speech**

In internal fertilisation, sperm reaches eggs inside the female's body. Keeping the meeting within a moist reproductive tract reduces exposure to drying, which is useful for reproduction on land. Some aquatic animals use internal fertilisation too, so habitat alone cannot classify the event. In a kangaroo, sperm is transferred during mating and fertilisation takes place inside the female. Achieving that transfer can require time and energy, and finding a mate can be a limitation. But internal fertilisation does not tell us a fixed number of offspring, how much parental care they receive, or their chance of survival. Those depend on the animal and its circumstances.

### `definition-comparison` (`b2-comparison`)

**Speech**

Keep three questions separate. Where do gametes fuse? Where does the embryo develop? What support does the offspring receive? A bird uses internal fertilisation, then lays an egg in which the embryo develops outside the body. That answers the first two questions differently. Parental investment means resources such as time, energy, nourishment and protection devoted to offspring. It can affect survival, but it is not the definition of internal fertilisation. To explain an advantage, link a feature to a condition. Protection from drying matters in a dry environment. Releasing gametes together matters when exposed gametes could disperse.

### `worked-example` (`b2-worked`)

**Speech**

Let's apply that reasoning to three cases. In the frog case, the prompt says eggs and sperm meet in pond water. Fusion is outside the body, so fertilisation is external. The water provides a moist setting, and release near the eggs helps sperm encounter them. In the kangaroo case, sperm meets egg inside the female, so fertilisation is internal. The protected moist setting reduces exposure to drying. Now the bird lays an already fertilised egg. Fusion occurred before laying, inside the body. Fertilisation is internal even though development continues outside in the egg. The deciding evidence is the location of fusion, not the sight of an egg in a nest.

### `misconception` (`b2-misconception`)

**Speech**

Does internal fertilisation make an animal's reproduction automatically better? That turns one useful feature into a universal verdict. Protection from drying can be an advantage, but it does not remove the costs of sperm transfer or all the risks faced by offspring. External fertilisation can work well where suitable water, proximity and timing support gamete encounters. We need the mechanism and the conditions before judging an advantage. Neither strategy promises that offspring will survive and reproduce.

### `quick-check` prompt (`b2-check-prompt`)

**Speech**

Try a changed condition. In a supplied broadcast-spawning model, adults release eggs and sperm into the same patch of water at the same time. A second group releases the same numbers into water with a stronger current that carries sperm away from the eggs. The gametes remain viable for the same length of time in both groups. Predict the effect on fertilisation opportunities in the second group, and explain why equal gamete numbers do not promise equal numbers of surviving offspring. Pause here to give your reason.

Prompt and feedback must become separate recordings. Start the measured interval after the final prompt speech ends; reveal the first answer-bearing element only at its verified end. Maintain a neutral stimulus and prompt-only captions. Do not run narration, solution arrows, a verdict, answer caption or solved outcome during the interval. Measure and review the final assembled boundary later.

### `quick-check` feedback (`b2-check-feedback`)

**Speech**

In the stated model, carrying sperm away reduces opportunities for sperm and eggs to meet, even though the group releases the same numbers of gametes. The changed condition is their separation, not their initial number or viable lifetime. That supports a prediction of fewer fertilisation opportunities; it does not give us an exact count of fertilised eggs. And fertilisation is not the same as survival to reproduction. Development, predation and later conditions still matter. If your answer was “same numbers, same success”, add the missing link: gametes must encounter each other, and the resulting offspring must then survive and reproduce.

### `summary` (`b2-summary`)

**Speech**

Animal reproduction has more than one mechanism. A hydra bud can make a new animal without gamete fusion. In our sexual model, haploid gametes combine into a diploid zygote. External and internal fertilisation describe where that fusion occurs, not where development happens or how much care follows. Explain an advantage by connecting a feature with the stated conditions, then keep fertilisation and later survival separate. Next, we will compare reproduction in plants, fungi, bacteria and protists. Mammalian pregnancy and hormonal control come after that.

## Response evidence and review plan

The changed-conditions task should elicit three links: the specified current separates gametes; fewer encounters are predicted despite equal initial numbers; fertilisation does not guarantee later survival/reproduction. A response that mentions only “more eggs” or “water” is missing the encounter mechanism. A response that predicts an exact survival count exceeds the supplied evidence. Feedback repairs those specific errors without assigning marks or claiming mastered coverage.
