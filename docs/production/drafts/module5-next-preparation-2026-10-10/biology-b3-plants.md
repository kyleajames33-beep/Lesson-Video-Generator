## Entry, start, stop and next handoff

**Entry knowledge:** reproduction connects generations; cells contain inherited information in DNA; gametes are reproductive cells; sexual reproduction involves fusion of gametes, while asexual reproduction does not. Basic root, stem and leaf vocabulary helps with a runner. `bio-m5-b01` is the canonical prerequisite. Its selected draft provides the fusion criterion and a strawberry runner example; it is support material, not a presumed public upload. **Entry diagnostic:** “If one plant supplies both gametes, does that make fusion asexual?” Expected reason: no, the event is sexual because gametes fuse. The opening reminder and the self-pollination scene supply support if this distinction is insecure. This is a diagnostic for a lesson page or teacher, not a second timed task added to the video.

**Start:** one strawberry plant can produce a new plant from a runner. A self-compatible flowering plant can also supply both gametes in a sexual reproductive event. One visible parent does not settle the classification.

**Stop:** the learner can explain pollen transfer, pollen-tube delivery and sperm-egg fusion in the supplied flowering-plant model; distinguish zygote-to-embryo development from ovule-to-seed development; explain strawberry runner propagation without fusion; classify supplied self-fertilisation and cross-fertilisation cases by fusion; and predict why interrupting the delivery pathway can prevent fusion despite successful pollination. ## Full candidate narration

Only paragraphs under **Speech** are proposed spoken words. Headings, IDs, citations and all production notes remain silent. Title card: **Reproduction in Plants: Pollen, Seeds and Runners**, with no required speech. Timing awaits accepted words and measured audio; no original long-lesson timings are copied.

### `hook` (`b3-hook`)

**Speech**

A strawberry plant can send out a runner that becomes another plant. A flowering plant can also supply both gametes that fuse during self-fertilisation. In both cases, one plant can be involved. Yet the first event is asexual and the second is sexual. The difference is the mechanism: did gametes fuse?

### `concept-flower` (`b3-flower`)

**Speech**

In a flowering plant, the anther produces pollen. A pollen grain is not simply a sperm cell. It is a small structure that produces or carries the sperm cells. The stigma receives pollen, and the style connects that receiving surface to the ovary. Inside the ovary are ovules. An egg cell lies within an embryo sac inside an ovule. These locations matter because pollen arriving at the stigma has not yet brought a sperm cell to the egg.

### `concept-delivery` (`b3-delivery`)

**Speech**

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. In this compatible flowering-plant example, a pollen grain then germinates and grows a tube through the style towards an ovule. The tube provides a route for sperm cells to reach the embryo sac. A sperm cell fuses with the egg cell, forming a zygote. That fusion is fertilisation. Pollen transfer and gamete fusion are different events, so successful delivery to the stigma alone does not prove fertilisation occurred.

### `concept-seed` (`b3-seed`)

**Speech**

In this sexual pathway, the zygote divides and develops into an embryo. The surrounding ovule develops into a seed, which contains the embryo, a protective coat and stored resources for early growth. The ovary usually develops into the fruit. The embryo and the whole seed are therefore different structures. This is the embryo-forming part of the flowering-plant pathway, rather than every event in its life cycle. Forming a seed also does not guarantee that a new plant will establish.

### `concept-runner` (`b3-runner`)

**Speech**

A strawberry runner is a stem extending from the parent plant. At a node, roots and a shoot can develop into a new plant. Growth and mitotic cell division build that plant without sperm and egg fusing. This is vegetative propagation, a form of asexual reproduction. The new plant usually retains the parent's inherited combination, although mutations can introduce differences. The runner is not just making the original plant larger: a new rooted plant can become independent. Suitable resources and conditions still matter.

### `definition-self-cross` (`b3-self-cross`)

**Speech**

Self-pollination transfers pollen to the same flower or to another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination transfers it between different plants. These terms describe the source and destination of pollen. If compatible pollen leads to sperm-egg fusion, either route can lead to sexual reproduction. Self-fertilisation is not asexual just because one plant supplies both gametes, and it does not guarantee a genetic copy of that plant. Cross-fertilisation can combine inherited information from different plants, but pollen from another plant does not guarantee a seed or a surviving offspring.

### `worked-classification` (`b3-worked`)

**Speech**

Let's compare three supplied cases. First, a strawberry runner develops roots and a shoot, with no gamete fusion. The new plant arose by vegetative propagation, so that event is asexual. Second, a self-compatible flowering plant supplies pollen and an egg, and the prompt confirms that sperm and egg fuse. That event is sexual, even though both gametes came from one plant. Third, pollen comes from a different plant, and fusion is again confirmed. This event is sexual too. The deciding evidence is fusion, not the number of plants we can see or a pollen-transfer arrow by itself.

### `concept-conditions` (`b3-conditions`)

**Speech**

Suppose pollen transfer becomes limited, but conditions around a strawberry runner still support new roots and shoots. That vegetative route can produce a new plant without pollen transfer. It still needs resources and suitable growing conditions. The sexual pathway can combine inherited information through gametes, but it requires the delivery and fusion steps to succeed. These are different opportunities and constraints. Neither route promises that the resulting plant will survive and reproduce.

### `quick-check` prompt (`b3-check-prompt`)

**Speech**

Here is a supplied model. Two groups receive viable, compatible pollen on their stigmas. In the second group, all pollen tubes stop before reaching the ovules. The eggs have not been fertilised, and there is no other pollen route. In this model, a new embryo requires sperm-egg fusion. Has pollination occurred in the second group? Predict whether fertilisation and a new embryo will follow through this pathway, and explain your reason. Pause here to work it through.

**Protected response interval:** initial planning hypothesis of 12 seconds, starting after the complete prompt audio ends. No answer-bearing speech, captions, diagram states, labels or feedback during that interval. Keep the model and both demands visible. This is a proposed opportunity, not measured timing or learner evidence. Adjust only after reviewing the exact voiced revision.

### `quick-check` feedback (`b3-check-feedback`)

**Speech**

Pollination has occurred because pollen reached the stigma. But in the supplied model, the tubes stop before sperm can reach the eggs. There is no sperm-egg fusion through this route, so no zygote or new embryo forms through it. Saying only that pollen arrived misses the later delivery step. Saying fertilisation occurred would confuse transfer with fusion. The model gives us no basis for a survival count.

### `summary` (`b3-summary`)

**Speech**

For plants, classify the reproductive event by its mechanism. In our sexual example, pollen transfer leads to sperm delivery, fusion and embryo development. The ovule develops into a seed. A strawberry runner forms a new plant without gamete fusion. Self-pollination and cross-pollination describe pollen transfer; neither is fusion itself. These mechanisms can produce new plants, while continuity also depends on descendants surviving and reproducing. The next contribution compares reproduction in fungi, bacteria and protists.
