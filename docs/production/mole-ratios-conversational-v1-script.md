## hook

Two hydrogen molecules make two water molecules. So is every reaction one for one? That would make the maths very easy. Unfortunately, the oxygen in that same reaction has a different deal: one oxygen molecule for two water molecules. The equation gives us the relationship. The job is to choose the two substances we're comparing.

## title

Mole ratios. Reading the amounts in a balanced equation.

## concept

In two H two plus O two gives two H two O, the numbers out front compare whole chemical entities: two hydrogen molecules, one oxygen molecule, two water molecules. We can scale all those counts up together. Multiply by Avogadro's number and the same relationship becomes two moles, one mole, two moles. The small two inside H two O has a different job. It describes each water molecule: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Changing that small number would change the substance. These drawings are a counting model, not the size or arrangement of real particles.

## definition

Balancing is an atom-accounting check. The same atoms have to appear on both sides, because an ordinary chemical reaction rearranges them. We adjust the numbers of whole entities, keeping each formula intact. That's why the coefficients can give us an amount ratio. The subscripts help us check the atom totals, but they aren't the numbers we use for the wanted-over-known conversion.

## formula

Here's a two-to-one relationship in a different reaction. Each mole of calcium hydroxide needs two moles of hydrochloric acid for this neutralisation. Three tenths of a mole of the base therefore needs six tenths of a mole of acid. Doubling the amount of base would double the required acid, without changing the recipe. These crates are labelled mole amounts. They aren't literal containers of molecules, and this equation doesn't show every ion in the solution.

## worked-example

Before using a ratio, the equation needs to balance. This iron example has four iron atoms and six oxygen atoms on each side. The subscripts stayed the same; the coefficients made the totals agree. Now compare iron with iron three oxide. Four to two, or two to one. That's the relative number of iron atoms and oxide formula units, and it's also their mole ratio. We aren't describing separate iron three oxide molecules in the solid.

## worked-example-2

Suppose zero point three zero zero moles of calcium hydroxide reacts completely. How much hydrochloric acid does it require? The equation says twice the amount of acid for the base. Multiplying by two over one therefore makes sense before we reach for the calculator. The required acid amount is zero point six zero zero moles. Going the other way would use one over two. The ratio follows what we want to find; it isn't one fraction to memorise for every question.

## misconception

There are two easy ways to get a plausible-looking wrong answer. One is taking the little numbers inside the formulas as the mole ratio. The other is using the right coefficients upside down. A quick sense-check helps. If the reaction needs twice as much acid as base, an acid answer that's half the base amount doesn't fit the equation. For a product prediction, we also need the stated reactant amount to react completely, with enough of the other reactants. The equation alone can't guarantee what we'll recover in the lab.

## quick-check-prompt

Back to the water equation. Four point zero zero moles of hydrogen react completely, with enough oxygen. How many moles of water form, and how much oxygen is required? It's the same hydrogen amount, but we're comparing it with two different substances. Pause here if you'd like to work out both ratios.

## quick-check-answer

Water uses the two-to-two ratio. Four point zero zero moles of hydrogen therefore gives four point zero zero moles of water. Oxygen uses one over two, so the required oxygen amount is two point zero zero moles. One starting amount, two different comparisons. The balanced coefficients explain why the answers differ.

## summary

The useful question is: which substance do we know, and which one do we want? A balanced equation compares intact chemical entities. The same proportions apply to moles. The wanted coefficient divided by the known coefficient converts between those amounts, and a quick check against the recipe helps catch an upside-down ratio.
