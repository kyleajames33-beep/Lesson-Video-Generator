## How to use these in videos

- Teach the idea first, then give the aid as a hook to keep it. A mnemonic never replaces the explanation.
- Say why the aid works whenever that is short.
- One or two aids per video is usually right. Put the strongest one in the "Key notes: what to write in your book" hold.
- Tags: **Mnemonic** (a phrase to recall a list or rule), **Shortcut** (a faster method), **Insight** (a way of seeing it that makes the rule obvious), **Trap** (a specific mistake it prevents).
- Scope notes are given where a shortcut only holds under stated conditions. Keep those conditions in the narration.

## Chemistry Module 6

### Acid-base models

**Each model fixed a failure of the one before.** *Insight*
Learn the history as a chain of problems, not a list of names:
1. **Lavoisier:** acids contain oxygen. *Failed because* HCl is an acid with no oxygen.
2. **Davy:** hydrogen, not oxygen, is the key element in acids. *Failed because* many hydrogen compounds such as methane aren't acidic, and it didn't explain bases.
3. **Arrhenius:** acids release H⁺ in water; bases release OH⁻. *Failed because* ammonia and sodium carbonate are bases with no OH⁻ of their own, it can't explain why salts such as NH₄Cl are acidic, and acid-base reactions can happen without water (NH₃ gas + HCl gas makes NH₄Cl).
4. **Brønsted–Lowry:** acids donate protons; bases accept them ("acid gives, base takes"). Works without water and explains ammonia. *Limitation:* it can't explain acidic substances with no proton to donate, such as SO₃.

Every "assess the model" answer is: what it explained, then the example that broke it.


### Conjugate pairs and amphiprotic species

**A conjugate pair differs by exactly one H⁺.** *Shortcut / Trap*
- Conjugate base: remove one H⁺ and lower the charge by 1 (H₂SO₄ to HSO₄⁻; NH₄⁺ to NH₃).
- Conjugate acid: add one H⁺ and raise the charge by 1 (NH₃ to NH₄⁺; H₂O to H₃O⁺).

Students lose marks by removing H instead of H⁺ (forgetting the charge change), or by removing two protons at once.

**"Amphi means both."** *Mnemonic*
Like an amphibian that lives on land and in water, an amphiprotic species can donate or accept a proton. Spot it: it has an H it can give *and* can accept another. Water, HCO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻ are the usual exam examples.

**The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.** *Insight*
A strong acid gives up its proton completely, so its conjugate base (Cl⁻ from HCl) has almost no tendency to take one back. A weak acid holds its proton, so its conjugate base is a genuine weak base (CH₃COO⁻). This explains salt hydrolysis below.

### Strong vs weak, concentrated vs dilute

**Strength is how much it breaks up. Concentration is how much you put in.** *Insight / Trap*
Strong: fully ionised in water. Weak: only partly ionised. Concentrated or dilute: how many moles per litre. They're independent, so a dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid can have the same pH. Never use "strong" to mean "concentrated" in a written answer.

**The strong acids: "So I Brought No Clean Clothes."** *Mnemonic*
H₂**S**O₄ (first ionisation), H**I**, H**Br**, H**N**O₃, H**Cl**, H**ClO₄**. In HSC questions, treat other acids as weak unless told otherwise. Strong bases: Group 1 hydroxides, plus barium hydroxide (calcium and strontium hydroxides are strong but only slightly soluble).

### pH and pOH

**One pH unit is ten times.** *Insight*
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], so each unit is a factor of 10 in [H⁺]. pH 3 has 100 times the [H⁺] of pH 5. Going back: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.

**pH + pOH = 14, at 25 °C.** *Shortcut / scope*
Because Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. For a base: find [OH⁻], take pOH, then pH = 14 − pOH.

**Count the hydrogens in diprotic acids.** *Trap*
In HSC calculations, 0.10 mol L⁻¹ H₂SO₄ is treated as [H⁺] = 0.20 mol L⁻¹, and it reacts with NaOH in a 1 : 2 ratio.

**Count the hydroxides.** *Trap*
0.050 mol L⁻¹ Ba(OH)₂ gives [OH⁻] = 0.10 mol L⁻¹, because each formula unit releases two OH⁻.

**Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻], not "pH 7".** *Insight* (links to Module 5)
Water's self-ionisation is endothermic. Write heat into the equation (heat + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻) and heating shifts it right, so Kw increases and neutral pH drops below 7. Pure water at about 60 °C has pH 6.5 and is still neutral.

**Decimal places in pH = significant figures in [H⁺].** *Trap*
[H⁺] = 2.5 × 10⁻³ (2 sig figs) gives pH = 2.60 (2 decimal places). The number before the decimal point only shows the power of ten.

**Dilution can't cross pH 7.** *Insight*
Diluting a strong acid tenfold raises its pH by about 1. Keep diluting and the pH approaches 7 but never goes past it, because water itself supplies [H⁺] of about 10⁻⁷.

**Mixing an acid and a base: neutralise first, then dilute.** *Shortcut* (very common calculation)
1. Find moles of H⁺ and moles of OH⁻.
2. Subtract: whichever is left over is in excess.
3. Divide the excess by the *total* volume.
4. Then take pH (or pOH).

Students who average the two pH values, or forget the total volume, lose the marks.

### Ka, pKa and weak acid pH

**Weak acid pH: [H⁺] ≈ √(Ka × c).** *Shortcut / scope*
From Ka = x² / (c − x). If the acid ionises only slightly (Ka small compared with c), c − x ≈ c, so x = √(Ka × c). Check afterwards that x is under about 5% of c.

**"pKa is like golf: lowest score wins."** *Mnemonic*
pKa = −log Ka, so a smaller pKa means a larger Ka, which means a stronger acid. Same direction as pH: lower means more acidic.

**Ka × Kb = Kw for a conjugate pair.** *Shortcut* (Extension)
So pKa + pKb = 14 at 25 °C. Given the Ka of ethanoic acid, students can find the Kb of the ethanoate ion in one step.

### Salt hydrolysis

**"The salt takes after its stronger parent."** *Mnemonic* (very high value)
Think of each salt as made from an acid and a base:
- Strong acid + weak base (NH₄Cl): acidic solution.
- Weak acid + strong base (CH₃COONa, NaHCO₃): basic solution.
- Strong acid + strong base (NaCl): neutral.
- Weak acid + weak base: depends on the relative strengths, so don't predict without data.

*Why it works:* the ion from the weak parent is a genuine acid or base (NH₄⁺ donates protons; CH₃COO⁻ accepts them). The ion from the strong parent does nothing. The same rule predicts the pH at the equivalence point of a titration.

### Buffers

**A buffer has a defender for each attack.** *Insight*
A buffer is a weak acid with its conjugate base in similar amounts. Add H⁺ and the conjugate base absorbs it (CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH). Add OH⁻ and the weak acid neutralises it (CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O). So the pH changes only slightly.

**A strong acid and its salt is not a buffer.** *Trap*
HCl with NaCl can't absorb added H⁺, because Cl⁻ isn't a base (see conjugate strength above). A buffer needs a *weak* pair.

**Resists, doesn't prevent.** *Trap*
A buffer keeps pH nearly constant against *small* additions. Add enough acid or base to use up one component and the buffer fails. Buffering is strongest when the two components are present in equal amounts, which is when pH = pKa. Blood example: H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻ keeps blood near pH 7.4.

### Enthalpy of neutralisation

**Strong acid with strong base: about −57 kJ per mole of water.** *Insight*
Every strong-strong neutralisation is really H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, so the value is nearly the same whichever pair you use. Weak acids usually give a less negative value, because some energy is used to ionise the weak acid.

**Per mole of water, not per mole of acid.** *Trap*
1 mol H₂SO₄ with 2 mol NaOH forms 2 mol water. Calculate q = mcΔT using the *total* mass of both solutions, then ΔH = −q ÷ moles of water. A temperature rise means ΔH is negative.

### Acid reactions and salt names

**The metal names the first word; the acid names the second.** *Shortcut*
HCl makes chlorides, H₂SO₄ sulfates, HNO₃ nitrates, CH₃COOH ethanoates. Magnesium with sulfuric acid makes magnesium sulfate.

**Three acid reactions and their gas tests.** *Mnemonic*
- Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen (squeaky pop with a lit splint).
- Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide (turns limewater milky).
- Acid + base → salt + water.

### Volumetric analysis (titration)

**"Rinse with what's going in."** *Shortcut* (most common practical question)
- Burette: rinse with the solution it will deliver.
- Pipette: rinse with the solution it will measure.
- Volumetric flask and conical flask: rinse with distilled water only.

*Why:* leftover water in a burette or pipette dilutes the solution you're measuring. Leftover water in the conical flask doesn't change the moles you've already pipetted in, so it doesn't matter. Rinsing the conical flask with the solution would add extra moles.

**NaOH is never a primary standard.** *Trap*
A primary standard must be pure, stable in air, not absorb water and preferably have a high molar mass. Solid NaOH absorbs water and reacts with CO₂ from the air, so its mass is never reliable. Anhydrous sodium carbonate is the usual primary standard; NaOH solutions are standardised against an acid.

**Average only concordant titres.** *Shortcut*
Discard the rough trial. Average titres that agree within 0.10 mL.

**Titration calculation: known to moles, ratio, unknown.** *Shortcut*
n = cV for the solution you know everything about. Use the balanced equation's mole ratio (H₂SO₄ : NaOH is 1 : 2). Then c = n / V for the unknown. Convert mL to L.

**Scale up from the aliquot.** *Trap* (very common lost mark)
If you titrated a 25.00 mL aliquot taken from a 250.0 mL volumetric flask, the flask contained 10 times the moles you found. Many multi-step questions hinge on this one step.

**Back titration: total minus leftover.** *Shortcut*
Used when the sample doesn't dissolve or reacts slowly (calcium carbonate in eggshells, antacid tablets). Add a known excess of acid, then titrate the acid that's left. Acid used by the sample = acid added − acid left over.

### Indicators and titration curves

**Choose the indicator that changes at the equivalence point.** *Shortcut* (uses the salt rule)
- Weak acid + strong base: equivalence above 7, so phenolphthalein (8.3 to 10.0).
- Strong acid + weak base: equivalence below 7 (often about 5), so methyl red (4.4 to 6.2) or methyl orange (3.1 to 4.4).
- Strong acid + strong base: the vertical region is long, so most common indicators work; bromothymol blue changes near 7.
- Weak acid + weak base: no sharp jump, so use a pH meter or conductivity instead.

**"Phenolphthalein turns pink in base."** *Mnemonic*
Colourless below pH 8.3, which includes neutral and weakly basic solutions, and pink above 10.0. A colourless solution is not proof of acid. **B**romothymol **b**lue is **b**ang in the middle (yellow to blue, 6.0 to 7.6).

**End point is not equivalence point.** *Trap*
The equivalence point is where the moles have reacted in the exact ratio. The end point is where the indicator changes colour. A good indicator makes them nearly coincide.

**At half-equivalence, pH = pKa.** *Shortcut* (read pKa off a graph)
Halfway to the equivalence volume, half the weak acid has become its conjugate base, so [HA] = [A⁻] and pH = pKa. Find the equivalence volume, halve it, and read the pH there.

**Read a curve's starting pH.** *Shortcut*
At 0.1 mol L⁻¹, a strong monoprotic acid starts near pH 1; a weak acid starts higher (around 3) and rises more gradually before the jump.

### Conductometric titrations

**H⁺ and OH⁻ are the best conductors.** *Insight*
These two ions conduct far better than other ions. Adding NaOH to HCl replaces fast H⁺ with slower Na⁺, so conductivity falls. It reaches a minimum at equivalence, then rises again as excess OH⁻ builds up. The bottom of the V is the equivalence point.

**Ba(OH)₂ + H₂SO₄ drops almost to zero.** *Insight*
Both products remove ions: BaSO₄ precipitates and H₂O barely ionises. At equivalence there are almost no ions left to conduct.

**Weak acid: starts low and rises.** *Shortcut*
A weak acid has few ions to begin with, so conductivity starts low and rises gently as the salt forms, then climbs steeply once excess strong base is added. The change in slope marks equivalence.

## Biology Module 6

### Mutation types

**Substitutions: Silent, Missense, Nonsense.** *Mnemonic / insight*
- **Silent:** the new codon codes for the same amino acid, so nothing changes.
- **Missense:** it *misses* the right amino acid and codes for a different one. Sickle cell disease is one missense change (glutamic acid to valine in haemoglobin).
- **Nonsense:** the codon becomes a stop codon, so the protein is cut short.

**Frameshift: THE FAT CAT ATE THE RAT.** *Insight* (recap from Module 5)
Insert or delete one base and every codon after it is read wrongly, so the protein usually stops working. That's why a single insertion is usually worse than a single substitution.

**Multiples of three don't shift the frame.** *Insight / real example*
Deleting three bases removes one amino acid but keeps the rest of the reading frame intact. The most common cystic fibrosis mutation (F508del) deletes three bases, losing a single amino acid. That single loss is enough to stop the protein folding properly.

**Chromosomal mutations as a book of chapters.** *Insight*
If a chromosome is a book:
- **Deletion:** a chapter is lost.
- **Duplication:** a chapter is printed twice.
- **Inversion:** a chapter is printed backwards.
- **Translocation:** a chapter is moved into a different book (a non-homologous chromosome), often as a swap between two books.

**Non-disjunction: chromosomes fail to dis-join.** *Mnemonic*
In meiosis, a chromosome pair or the sister chromatids don't separate, so one gamete gets an extra chromosome and another gets one fewer. Fertilisation with the extra one gives a trisomy, such as trisomy 21 (Down syndrome).

**Aneuploidy vs polyploidy.** *Mnemonic*
**Aneu**ploidy: "not a proper set". One or a few extra or missing chromosomes; usually harmful in animals. **Poly**ploidy: "many full sets". Common and often useful in plants; bread wheat has six sets.

### Mutagens

**Ionising radiation knocks electrons off.** *Insight*
"Ionising" means energetic enough to remove electrons, which is enough to break DNA strands (X-rays, gamma rays). UV is not ionising, but it makes neighbouring thymine bases on the same strand bond to each other (thymine dimers), which distorts the DNA.

**Three kinds of mutagen.** *Mnemonic*
- **Electromagnetic radiation:** UV, X-rays, gamma rays.
- **Chemicals:** e.g. benzene and chemicals in tobacco smoke.
- **Naturally occurring:** aflatoxin from mould, transposons ("jumping genes"), and viruses that insert their DNA into ours.

### Somatic vs germline, coding vs non-coding

**"Somatic stays with you. Germline goes to your kids."** *Mnemonic / insight*
Somatic mutations happen in body cells. They can cause disease such as a UV-induced skin cancer, but can't be inherited. Germline mutations happen in cells that make gametes, so they can be passed to offspring and change the gene pool. "Germ" as in germinate: the cells that start the next generation.

**Non-coding doesn't mean unimportant.** *Trap*
A mutation outside a gene's coding sequence can still matter if it hits a promoter or other regulatory region (changing how much protein is made) or an intron splice site (changing which parts are kept). "It's in non-coding DNA, so it has no effect" is a common wrong answer.

**Most mutations are neutral, and some help.** *Insight / real examples*
Lactase persistence lets many adults digest milk (it comes from a change in a regulatory, non-coding region). People with two copies of the CCR5-Δ32 deletion are strongly resistant to the common strains of HIV-1. Mutation is the raw material of evolution, not just a cause of disease.

### Causes of genetic variation in a population

**Who makes new alleles, and who only reshuffles them.** *Insight* (sorts the whole topic)
- **Mutation:** the only source of genuinely *new* alleles.
- **Meiosis and fertilisation:** make new *combinations* of existing alleles. They don't create alleles.
- **Gene flow:** moves alleles between populations.
- **Genetic drift:** changes allele frequencies by chance.
- **Natural selection:** changes allele frequencies by differences in survival and reproduction.

**Gene flow makes populations more alike. Drift and selection make them different.** *Insight*
Migration mixes gene pools. Isolation lets chance and local conditions pull them apart, which is the first step towards new species.

**Drift is luck, and luck matters more in small groups.** *Insight*
Flip a coin 10 times and 7 heads is common; flip it 1,000 times and you'll be close to half. In a small population, chance alone can eliminate an allele or make it common.

**Bottleneck vs founder effect.** *Mnemonic*
- **Bottleneck:** a disaster squeezes a population down to a few survivors (cheetahs; Tasmanian devils have very low diversity, which worsens their facial tumour problem).
- **Founder:** a few individuals leave to start a new population, carrying only some of the alleles (Ellis–van Creveld syndrome is unusually common in the Old Order Amish of Pennsylvania).

Both mean "few individuals, so the gene pool is a random sample".

**Individuals don't evolve. Populations do.** *Trap*
Selection acts on individuals' phenotypes, but the change is measured as allele frequencies in a population over generations. An individual can't change its alleles in response to its environment.

### Biotechnology

**Biotechnology is older than GMOs.** *Trap*
Bread, beer, cheese and selective breeding all use organisms to make products, so they count as biotechnology. Questions asking for "historical" biotechnology want these examples.

**Reproductive technologies choose the parents; they don't change genes.** *Insight*
Artificial insemination and artificial pollination control *which* individuals reproduce. They change allele frequencies in the next generation, but create no new alleles. Using a few top sires or parents reduces genetic diversity.

**The banana problem returns.** *Insight* (link to Module 5)
Any technology that copies the best individual (cloning, heavy use of one sire) narrows the gene pool and raises the risk that one disease affects everything. Use it when a technology reduces genetic diversity (cloning, heavy use of one sire, monocultures).

### Cloning

**The clone matches the nucleus donor.** *Trap* (Dolly the sheep)
In somatic cell nuclear transfer, the nucleus from a body cell goes into an egg whose own nucleus was removed, and the embryo grows in a surrogate. The clone's nuclear DNA matches the *nucleus donor*, not the egg donor or the surrogate. (Its small amount of mitochondrial DNA comes from the egg.)

**Gene cloning copies a gene; organism cloning copies an individual.** *Insight*
Gene cloning makes many copies of one gene, by inserting it into a plasmid in bacteria (PCR also copies a gene, in a tube). Whole-organism cloning makes a genetically matching individual (cuttings, tissue culture, nuclear transfer).

### Recombinant DNA and transgenic organisms

**Cut, paste, put in, pick.** *Mnemonic* (the whole process in order)
1. **Cut:** the same restriction enzyme cuts the gene out and opens the plasmid.
2. **Paste:** DNA ligase joins the gene into the plasmid.
3. **Put in:** bacteria take up the plasmid (transformation).
4. **Pick:** grow them on antibiotic so only bacteria carrying the plasmid's resistance marker survive.

The antibiotic step selects bacteria that took up a plasmid; a second marker is used to tell which plasmids carry the gene. Human insulin has been made this way in bacteria since the early 1980s.

**Same enzyme, matching sticky ends.** *Insight*
A restriction enzyme cuts at a specific sequence. Many leave short single-stranded overhangs (some leave blunt ends). Cutting both the gene and the plasmid with the same enzyme gives overhangs that pair up like matching jigsaw pieces. They're "sticky" because the unpaired bases readily pair.

**Restriction enzymes restrict viruses.** *Insight*
Bacteria evolved them to chop up invading viral DNA. Their recognition sites are usually palindromes: GAATTC reads the same 5′ to 3′ on both strands.

**Transgenic means across species.** *Mnemonic*
"Trans" = across. A transgenic organism carries a gene from a different species. Australian Bt cotton carries a bacterial gene for an insect toxin, which has greatly reduced insecticide spraying. Growing one Bt crop everywhere selects for resistant insects, so Australian growers must also plant refuge crops without the Bt gene, where susceptible insects survive and dilute the resistance alleles. Golden Rice carries genes to make beta-carotene.

### Evaluating genetic technologies (exam technique)

**"Evaluate" needs a verdict.** *Trap*
NESA's "evaluate" means make a judgement based on criteria. A list of benefits and limitations without a final judgement caps the mark. End with a clear position and the main reason for it.

**Four questions for any ethics answer.** *Shortcut*
Who benefits? Who carries the risk? Who decides (consent and access)? What happens to biodiversity and the environment? Answering these for one named example gives a balanced, specific response.
